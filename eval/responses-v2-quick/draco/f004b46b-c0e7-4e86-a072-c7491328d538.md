# Tax-Efficient Investment Strategy for a Freelance Graphic Designer in Toronto

## Executive Summary

At CAD 95,000 annual income in Ontario, you face a marginal tax rate of approximately 29.6%, making RRSP contributions a powerful tax-arbitrage tool. The optimal strategy layers three accounts: first secure a 6-month emergency fund in a TFSA (~$23,750 based on typical expense ratios), then maximize RESP contributions to capture the full Canada Education Savings Grant (CESG) for both children, and direct the bulk of remaining savings to an RRSP—preferably a spousal RRSP if your partner earns less—while cycling tax refunds back into the TFSA. Front-loading RESP contributions to accelerate CESG capture is mathematically superior to even spreading over the next 7 years due to compounding, but must be weighed against the liquidity constraints of variable freelance income.

## 1. Spousal RRSP vs. Individual RRSP: Tax Implications at $95K in Ontario

### Your Marginal Rate Context

According to a 2026 Canadian tax calculator, the marginal rate relevant to an RRSP contributor at this income level is approximately **29.6%**, meaning that contributing $1,000 to an RRSP saves approximately $296.50 in taxes [2]. This calculator also recommends that individuals in this bracket consider income splitting with a lower-earning spouse through a spousal RRSP or pension splitting [2].

### How Spousal RRSPs Work

A spousal RRSP allows a higher-earning spouse to contribute to an RRSP registered in the lower-earning spouse's name. The contributor claims the tax deduction on their own return, and the funds grow tax-deferred inside the plan. Based on CRA guidance, generally only the individual entitled to receive amounts from the RRSP (the annuitant) can withdraw funds [6].

The CRA explicitly states that if you contributed to your spouse's RRSPs in 2023, 2024, or 2025, you may have to include in your 2025 income all or part of the amounts your spouse received in 2025 [6]. To ensure attribution does not apply, the CRA advises: "make sure you have not contributed to any of your spouse's or common‑law partner's RRSPs in the year your spouse or common‑law partner withdraws the funds, or in either of the two preceding years" [6].

This is known as the **3-calendar-year attribution rule**—the attribution period covers three calendar years, not three full years [7]. If the annuitant withdraws after three years with no new contributions, the withdrawal is treated as income to the annuitant at their marginal rate [7].

### When Spousal RRSP Wins

A spousal RRSP is advantageous **if your spouse/partner has significantly lower income** (or no income). The strategy equalizes retirement income between partners, potentially saving thousands per year in taxes during retirement by keeping both spouses in lower brackets. Key considerations:

- **If your spouse earns similarly (~$95K)**: A spousal RRSP provides minimal benefit since the income-splitting advantage is negligible. An individual RRSP is simpler and equally effective.
- **If your spouse earns little or nothing** (e.g., stays home with children): A spousal RRSP is highly advantageous. You get the deduction at ~30%, and withdrawals in retirement are taxed in your spouse's hands at a potentially much lower rate.
- **If you're over 71**: CRA rules allow continued contributions to a spousal RRSP if your spouse is 71 or younger at year-end and you have RRSP deduction room [8].

### Recommendation

If your spouse earns less than ~$50,000, **prioritize the spousal RRSP**. The contribution mechanics and deduction are identical to an individual RRSP—you simply direct contributions to your spouse's plan. Stop contributing at least 3 calendar years before anticipated withdrawals to avoid attribution.

## 2. RESP Front-Loading vs. Even Contributions: 7-Year Horizon

### CESG Mechanics

The Canada Education Savings Grant provides matching contributions to RESPs with a **lifetime maximum of $7,200 per child** [3]. You can receive up to **$1,000 of CESG per calendar year** per child [3], which implies that regular annual contributions of $2,500 yield a $500 grant (a 20% match rate), while catch-up contributions of up to $5,000 per year can recover one missed year's grant to reach the $1,000 annual maximum.

### Your Children's Timelines

| Child | Age Now | University Start | Years to Enrol |
|-------|---------|-----------------|----------------|
| Eldest | 11 | ~2032 | 7 years |
| Youngest | 8 | ~2035 | 10 years |

### Strategy Comparison

**Option A: Even Contributions ($2,500/year/child)**
- Each child receives $500 CESG/year
- Eldest: 7 years × $500 = **$3,500 CESG** (captures $3,500 of $7,200 lifetime)
- Youngest: 10 years × $500 = **$5,000 CESG** (captures $5,000 of $7,200)
- Total CESG captured: **$8,500** of $14,400 available

**Option B: Accelerated Catch-Up ($5,000/year/child initially)**
- Contribute $5,000/year per child for the first 2–3 years to use catch-up provisions
- Each year at $5,000 captures $1,000 CESG (current year + one prior missed year)
- After front-loading, drop to $2,500/year for the remaining years
- Eldest: ~$5,000 CESG over 7 years; Youngest: ~$7,200 CESG over 10 years
- Total CESG captured: **~$12,200** of $14,400 available

**Option C: Maximum Front-Loading ($16,500 in Year 1)**
Contribute $16,500 in the first year (receiving the maximum annual CESG and investing the remainder extra for compounding), then continue with catch-up contributions in subsequent years [3].

### The Math Favors Front-Loading

Front-loading wins for two reasons:

1. **More CESG captured**: Accelerated contributions recover missed grant years, pulling forward government matching money that compounds.
2. **Longer compounding**: Money invested in Year 1 grows for 7–10 years versus money invested in Year 7 which barely compounds before withdrawal.

At a modest 5% annual return, $16,500 invested today for your eldest grows to ~$23,200 in 7 years. The same $16,500 spread evenly ($2,357/year) grows to only ~$20,100—a **$3,100 difference per child** from compounding alone.

### Caveat for Variable Income

The Tawcan guide on RESP optimization advises that "rather than focusing on funding a child's RESP, I believe parents should consider contributing to their TFSAs and RRSPs first before contributing to the RESP" [3].

This is critical. Unlike RRSP and TFSA contributions, **RESP contributions are not tax-deductible** and the funds are restricted to educational use. The most tax-efficient use of RESP money is for post-secondary education expenses [3]. For a freelancer with variable income, locking large sums in an RESP reduces liquidity.

### Recommendation for Your Situation

For your eldest (7-year window): **Contribute $5,000/year for the first 3 years** (capturing $1,000 CESG/year = $3,000), then $2,500/year for the remaining 4 years ($500/year = $2,000). Total CESG: $5,000 of $7,200. Total contributions: $25,000.

For your youngest (10-year window): Same accelerated start, then extend to capture the full $7,200 CESG over the decade.

Combined annual RESP commitment: ~$7,500–$10,000/year initially, dropping to ~$5,000/year later.

## 3. Optimal Monthly Allocation: TFSA, RRSP, and RESP

### Assumptions

- Annual net income: ~$95,000 (variable monthly)
- 6-month emergency fund target: ~$23,750 (assuming ~$3,958/month expenses)
- TFSA annual limit: $7,000 (2025); cumulative room if eligible since 2009: $102,000 [5]
- RRSP annual limit: approximately 18% of earned income
- Married, spouse's income assumed lower (otherwise spousal RRSP less beneficial)

### Prioritized Allocation Framework

| Priority | Account | Annual Target | Rationale |
|----------|---------|---------------|-----------|
| **1** | TFSA (Emergency Fund) | Until $23,750 reached | Liquid, tax-free, no withdrawal penalty—essential for variable freelance income |
| **2** | RESP (Both Children) | $7,500–$10,000 | Accelerated CESG capture with front-loading; irreplaceable government grant |
| **3** | RRSP/Spousal RRSP | $10,000–$15,000 | Tax deduction at ~29.6% marginal rate; reinvest refund |
| **4** | TFSA (Investment) | Refund from RRSP + remainder | "RRSP refund → TFSA loop" maximizes tax-free compounding |

### The RRSP Refund Loop

The FreelancerTax guide for self-employed Canadians explains: "A $10,000 RRSP contribution at a 30% marginal rate gives you a $3,000 refund. Put that $3,000 in your TFSA and it grows tax-free forever" [1]. This is the engine of the strategy. Each RRSP contribution generates a tax refund that funds TFSA contributions, effectively converting pre-tax dollars into tax-free growth.

### Managing Variable Income

The guide for self-employed Canadians recommends that those earning over $55K should "prioritize RRSP — the tax arbitrage is significant; reinvest the refund into TFSA" [1]. However, it also emphasizes: "Before any RRSP contributions, ensure you have 3-6 months of living expenses in your TFSA" [1].

As a freelancer, your monthly income fluctuates. The strategy must adapt:

- **High-income months**: Maximize RRSP contributions (spousal if applicable) to capture the highest tax deduction.
- **Low-income months**: Draw from TFSA emergency fund; defer RRSP deductions to higher-income years using income smoothing.
- **Irregular windfalls**: Prioritize RESP catch-up contributions to maximize CESG, then direct remainder to RRSP.

The self-employment guide also describes carrying forward RRSP contribution room and deferring tax deductions to years where your marginal rate is highest [1].

## 4. Strategy That Maximizes After-Tax Wealth by 2032

The **optimal strategy** combines:

1. **Build the emergency fund first** in a TFSA (~$23,750). This takes ~6 months of focused saving. For a freelancer, TFSA liquidity is non-negotiable—the guide recommends establishing 3–6 months of living expenses before any RRSP contributions [1].

2. **Front-load RESP contributions** for both children at $5,000/child/year for the first 2–3 years to maximize CESG capture, then reduce to $2,500/child/year. This captures ~$10,000–$12,000 in free government grants that cannot be recovered any other way.

3. **Maximize RRSP/spousal RRSP contributions** (especially in high-income months) to capture the ~29.6% tax deduction [2]. If your spouse earns less, direct contributions to a spousal RRSP for retirement income splitting. Observe the 3-calendar-year attribution rule before any withdrawals [6].

4. **Reinvest all RRSP refunds into the TFSA** for tax-free compounding. This "refund loop" is the single most powerful wealth-building mechanic at your income level [1].

5. **Use income smoothing**: Carry forward RRSP deductions to your highest-income years. As a freelancer, this flexibility is a significant advantage—deduct contributions when your marginal rate is highest [1].
