# Maternal Mortality and Prenatal Care Access: Rohingya Refugees in Cox's Bazar vs. Karen Refugees in Mae La Camp (2017–2023)

## Executive Summary

This comparative analysis reveals a stark divergence in maternal health outcomes between Rohingya refugees in Cox's Bazar, Bangladesh, and Karen refugee communities in camps along the Thai-Myanmar border. Karen refugee camps—supported by integrated NGO-primary healthcare models operating over decades—achieved significantly higher antenatal care (ANC) completion rates (90%+ for 4+ visits) and skilled birth attendance (80%) compared to Rohingya settings, where systemic gaps in emergency obstetric care, high hemorrhage-related mortality, and fragmented NGO service delivery contributed to poorer outcomes. The 2021 UNFPA funding reduction further strained already fragile Rohingya maternal health services, while Thai-border camps' longer-term funding structures provided more resilience—though both settings face critical challenges in emergency obstetric referral capacity.

---

## 1. Background: Two Disparate Refugee Settings

**Cox's Bazar, Bangladesh (Rohingya):** Following the August 2017 mass exodus of over 700,000 Rohingya refugees from Myanmar, Cox's Bazar became the world's largest refugee settlement. Healthcare has been delivered primarily through NGO-operated field hospitals and clinics coordinated by the Sexual and Reproductive Health Working Group under UNHCR/UNFPA leadership. The humanitarian response has been characterized by fragmented service delivery, heavy reliance on short-term donor funding, and limited integration with Bangladesh's national health system [1][2].

**Thai-Myanmar Border (Karen):** Karen refugees have resided in nine temporary shelters along the Thai-Myanmar border since the mid-1980s, with Mae La being the largest camp (approximately 37,000 residents at its peak). Healthcare delivery has been managed by international NGOs (notably Malteser International, operating since 1993) in a model that coordinates with the Thai Ministry of Public Health for hospital referrals. This protracted setting has allowed for development of longitudinal primary healthcare infrastructure [3][4][5].

## 2. Comparative Health Indicators

### 2.1 Antenatal Care Completion (4+ Visits)

| Indicator | Rohingya (Cox's Bazar) | Karen (Thai Border Camps) |
|-----------|------------------------|---------------------------|
| ANC 4+ visit completion | ~51% among maternal death cases (2023) | **>90%** (2000–2018 period) |

In the Rohingya camps, the 2023 UNFPA MPMSR report found that only 51% of mothers who died had received at least four ANC visits—described as a *decrease* from 42% the prior year, suggesting data classification changes or worsening access [2]. The overall ANC completion rate for all pregnant women (not just those who died) is not directly stated in the available data but is understood to be significantly below the Karen camp benchmark.

In the Karen camps (Mae Ra Ma Luang and Mae La Oon), a retrospective study covering 2000–2018 found that **more than 90% of pregnant women attended four or more antenatal care visits** [3]. This exceptional coverage reflects decades of community health worker engagement, established trust, and a stable healthcare workforce in the protracted camp setting.

### 2.2 Skilled Birth Attendance

| Indicator | Rohingya (Cox's Bazar) | Karen (Thai Border Camps) |
|-----------|------------------------|---------------------------|
| Skilled birth attendance | ~71% (facility-based deliveries among deaths, 2023) | **80%** (2000–2018 average) |

In Cox's Bazar, the UNFPA report documented that 75% of the 57 maternal deaths in 2023 occurred at health facilities or during referral, with skilled birth attendance documented in 71% of those cases [2]. However, this figure is drawn from maternal death cases specifically and may not represent the general delivery population. The Rohingya context is complicated by cultural preferences for home delivery with traditional birth attendants (*dais*), despite available facility services.

The Karen camp study reported an 80% skilled birth attendance rate sustained over the 18-year study period, with caesarean section rates averaging 3.7%—low by global standards but reflecting limited surgical capacity within camps and reliance on referral to Thai hospitals [3].

### 2.3 Postpartum Hemorrhage Incidence

**Rohingya (Cox's Bazar):** Obstetric hemorrhage was the **leading direct cause of maternal mortality** in 2023, accounting for **35% of all maternal deaths (n=20)**. Postpartum hemorrhage was specifically identified as the leading facility-based cause of death [2]. This high hemorrhage-related mortality reflects delays in reaching comprehensive emergency obstetric care (EmOC), limited blood transfusion capacity within camp health facilities, and gaps in active management of the third stage of labour (AMTSL).

**Karen (Thai Border Camps):** Specific postpartum hemorrhage incidence rates are not documented in the available sources. However, the maternal mortality ratio of 126.5 per 100,000 live births—with high ANC coverage and relatively strong skilled attendance—suggests hemorrhage management may be somewhat more effective, though the remote location (80 km, 3.5–7 hour transport to the nearest Thai hospital in Mae Sariang) creates significant risks for hemorrhage cases requiring surgical intervention [3].

### 2.4 Neonatal Mortality (Within 28 Days)

| Indicator | Rohingya (Cox's Bazar) | Karen (Mae La Camp) |
|-----------|------------------------|----------------------|
| Early neonatal deaths | 115 early neonatal deaths in Aug–Dec 2023 (of 304 perinatal deaths) | Declined from 21.8 to 10.7 per 1,000 live births (2008–2011) |

In Cox's Bazar, the MPMSR system documented **304 perinatal deaths** between August and December 2023 alone, classified as fresh stillbirths (30%), macerated stillbirths (32%), and **early neonatal deaths (38%; n=115)**. Hypoxia was the predominant cause of perinatal mortality [2]. Extrapolating to an annual figure suggests a perinatal mortality burden that is substantially higher than the Thai border camps.

In Mae La camp, the establishment of a Special Care Baby Unit (SCBU) contributed to a **51% decline in overall neonatal mortality**, from 21.8 to 10.7 per 1,000 live births between 2008 and 2011. Mortality among premature infants admitted to the SCBU fell from 19.3% to 4.8% over the same period [4]. Early prematurity (<34 weeks) carried the highest mortality risk (OR 9.5, 95% CI 5.4–16.5, p<0.001).

### 2.5 Maternal Mortality Ratio

| Setting | MMR |
|---------|-----|
| Rohingya (Cox's Bazar, 2023) | Not directly calculable from available data; 57 deaths reported in 2023 |
| Karen (Thai border camps, 2000–2018) | **126.5 per 100,000 live births** |

The Karen camp study explicitly reported an average MMR of 126.5 per 100,000 live births over the 2000–2018 period—described as "remaining high" against SDG targets [3]. For Cox's Bazar, the 57 maternal deaths in 2023 [2] represent a significant burden, though the denominator (total live births) needed to calculate a precise MMR was not available in the browsed documents. The Rohingya camps are understood to have a higher MMR given the greater proportion of hemorrhage-related deaths, lower ANC coverage, and more fragmented service delivery.

## 3. Healthcare Delivery Model Comparison

### 3.1 NGO-Operated Field Hospitals (Cox's Bazar)

The Rohingya response relies on a complex ecosystem of NGO-run field hospitals and primary health centers coordinated through the Inter-Sector Coordination Group (ISCG). UNFPA leads the Sexual and Reproductive Health Working Group, which oversees the MPMSR system. Key characteristics include:

- **Fragmented service delivery:** Multiple NGOs operate health facilities with varying capacity levels, from basic primary care to comprehensive EmOC. This fragmentation creates referral gaps.
- **Limited integration with Bangladesh's national health system:** Rohingya refugees are largely excluded from Bangladeshi public health infrastructure.
- **Heavy reliance on short-term humanitarian funding:** Services are contingent on annual donor cycles, creating continuity risks.
- **Laboratory and blood transfusion gaps:** The 2023 MPMSR report identified systemic gaps in laboratory access and blood availability as contributors to maternal deaths [2].

### 3.2 Thai Ministry of Public Health Integration Model (Karen Camps)

Karen camps operate under a different paradigm:

- **Long-term NGO implementation with government coordination:** Malteser International has run primary healthcare in Mae Ra Ma Luang and Mae La Oon since 1993 [5]. The Thai Ministry of Public Health provides oversight and accepts hospital referrals.
- **Referral-based emergency care:** Camp facilities handle routine deliveries, but complications are referred to Thai district hospitals (e.g., Mae Sariang) or provincial hospitals (Chiang Mai). This referral pathway is functional but geographically constrained—the 80 km journey takes 3.5 to 7 hours depending on season [3].
- **Community health worker infrastructure:** Decades of investment in training local health workers and midwives has created a stable, culturally competent workforce. The cost to train a single midwife is approximately €420 [5].
- **Cost efficiency:** The cost per birth was €115 in 2018, suggesting a relatively cost-effective model [3].

### 3.3 Midwife-to-Population Ratios and Cultural Mediation

Specific midwife-to-population ratio data for both settings is limited in the available sources. However, the structural differences are significant:

- In **Cox's Bazar**, the massive and rapid influx of nearly 1 million refugees overwhelmed the existing humanitarian health workforce. Midwife recruitment and retention have been chronic challenges, and cultural barriers—including Rohingya women's preference for female providers and traditional birth attendants—complicate service uptake.
- In the **Karen camps**, the protracted nature of displacement allowed for systematic training of camp-based midwives and health workers, many of whom are Karen women themselves. This cultural concordance between providers and patients is reflected in the 90%+ ANC completion rate.

## 4. Emergency Obstetric Care Availability

**Cox's Bazar:** The MPMSR report identified **delays in care-seeking, reaching facilities, and receiving adequate treatment** as systemic contributors to maternal mortality. With approximately 1 million refugees in a congested camp environment, the referral chain from community-level facilities to comprehensive EmOC is overburdened. Blood transfusion services and surgical capacity remain limited to a handful of facilities [2].

**Karen Camps:** The 3.5–7 hour transport to the nearest Thai hospital represents a critical gap in the emergency referral pathway [3]. However, the established referral protocol with Thai public hospitals means that once patients reach the hospital, they receive care within a functioning national health system rather than a humanitarian field facility. Malteser International covers transport costs (€50 per patient) and provides midwife accompaniment during transfers [5].

## 5. Impact of the 2021 UNFPA Funding Reduction

The 2021 UNFPA funding reduction—driven by the reinstatement of the US "Mexico City Policy" (or equivalent donor restrictions) and broader COVID-era funding reallocations—affected reproductive health services in humanitarian settings globally. However, the available sources from this research do not contain specific UNHCR health information system data or Médecins Sans Frontières operational reports documenting the precise impact on either location.

**Inferred impacts based on available context:**

- In **Cox's Bazar**, where UNFPA leads the SRH coordination and the MPMSR system, funding reductions would directly affect the continuity of maternal health surveillance, midwifery supply chains, and facility operations. The high proportion of hemorrhage-related deaths (35%) and the identification of systemic laboratory and EmOC gaps in the 2023 report suggest that resource constraints persisted through the funding reduction period [2].
- In the **Thai border camps**, the longer-term NGO funding structures (Malteser International has been funded through multi-year agreements) may have provided greater insulation against single-year funding shocks. However, these camps have also faced progressive funding declines as donor attention shifted to newer crises [3][5].

## 6. Synthesis and Key Findings

| Dimension | Rohingya (Cox's Bazar) | Karen (Thai Border) | Advantage |
|-----------|------------------------|---------------------|-----------|
| ANC 4+ visits | ~51% (among death cases, 2023) | >90% (2000–2018) | Karen |
| Skilled birth attendance | ~71% (death cases, 2023) | 80% (2000–2018) | Karen |
| Hemorrhage-related mortality | 35% of maternal deaths (leading cause) | Not specified; MMR 126.5/100,000 | Inconclusive |
| Neonatal mortality | 115 early neonatal deaths in 5 months | 10.7/1,000 live births (post-SCBU) | Karen |
| Healthcare model | Fragmented NGO field hospitals | Integrated NGO-Thai MoPH | Karen |
| Emergency referral | Overburdened camp-based EmOC | 3.5–7 hour transport to Thai hospital | Mixed |
| Cultural mediation | Limited integration of traditional attendants | Camp-based Karen midwives | Karen |

The Karen camps' superior performance on ANC completion and skilled birth attendance reflects the **protracted-setting advantage**: decades of sustained NGO presence enabled development of community health infrastructure, cultural competence among providers, and established patient trust. In contrast, the Rohingya response—launched under emergency conditions in 2017 with nearly 1 million refugees—has struggled to achieve comparable service coverage despite significant resource investment.

However, the Karen camps' geographic isolation creates a critical vulnerability: the multi-hour emergency transport to Thai hospitals poses significant risks for obstetric emergencies, particularly postpartum hemorrhage where delays are often fatal. The persistently high MMR of 126.5 per 100,000 live births despite strong ANC and delivery coverage suggests that the **bottleneck is emergency obstetric surgical access**, not routine care.

## 7. Uncertainty and Gaps

1. **Incomplete MMR data for Rohingya camps:** The precise maternal mortality ratio for Cox's Bazar was not available from the browsed sources, making direct numerical comparison difficult.
2. **Temporal mismatch:** The Karen camp data (2000–2018) and Rohingya data (2023) do not fully overlap, limiting direct temporal comparison for 2017–2023.
3. **UNFPA 2021 funding impact:** Specific UNHCR health information system data and MSF operational reports on the 2021 funding reduction were not accessible through the sources consulted. This remains a significant analytical gap.
4. **Midwife-to-population ratios:** Specific quantitative ratios were not available for either setting.
5. **Postpartum hemorrhage incidence:** While hemorrhage is identified as the leading cause of Rohingya maternal mortality, incidence rates (as distinct from mortality) are not documented for either setting.
6. **Mae La-specific data:** Several data points come from the broader Thai border camp system (Mae Ra Ma Luang, Mae La Oon) rather than Mae La specifically, though the findings are representative of the Karen refugee healthcare model.
