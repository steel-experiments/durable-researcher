# Methodological Tensions in Staggered Difference-in-Differences: A Comparative Analysis of Post-Bacon Estimators

## Executive Summary

The Goodman-Bacon (2021) decomposition exposed that conventional two-way fixed effects (TWFE) regression in staggered DiD designs produces biased estimates under heterogeneous treatment effects due to "forbidden comparisons" and negative weighting. Three families of solutions have emerged—Callaway and Sant'Anna's (2021) doubly-robust group-time ATT estimator, Sun and Abraham's (2021) interaction-weighted estimator, and Borusyak, Jaravel, and Spiess's (2024) imputation method—each handling heterogeneity and dynamic timing through distinct mechanisms. While all three avoid the negative-weighting problem, they diverge meaningfully in their assumptions about parallel trends conditioning, efficiency properties, and how they address Roth's (2022) critique of pre-trend testing. The imputation approach claims theoretical efficiency dominance, though Callaway-Sant'Anna appears to have achieved the widest practical adoption in applied work due to its flexible doubly-robust properties and mature software implementation.

---

## 1. The Staggered Adoption Problem: Goodman-Bacon's Critique

Goodman-Bacon (2021) proved that the canonical TWFE DiD estimator is actually a weighted average of all possible 2×2 comparisons between cohort pairs and time periods. In staggered designs, some of these comparisons use already-treated units as controls—so-called "forbidden comparisons." When treatment effects are heterogeneous across cohorts or over time, these comparisons can receive negative weights, potentially yielding an aggregate ATT estimate of the wrong sign even when every unit benefits from treatment [1].

As one lecture synthesis puts it: "Goodman-Bacon (2021) proves that TWFE in staggered DiD is actually a weighted average of different 'comparisons' between different cohorts/groups," and "comparison 2 is actually comparing units that are treated (the later-treated ones) with units that are already treated (earlier treated units)" [1]. The negative weighting problem disappears only under homogeneous treatment effects—a strong and often implausible restriction.

## 2. The Three Solution Families: Mechanisms and Assumptions

### 2.1 Callaway and Sant'Anna (2021): Doubly-Robust Group-Time ATTs

**Mechanism.** The CS estimator decomposes the problem into its atomic units: *group-time average treatment effects* (ATT(g,t)), where g denotes treatment-cohort membership and t the time period. Each ATT(g,t) is estimated using only never-treated or not-yet-treated units as controls, cleanly avoiding forbidden comparisons [2][4].

**Parallel trends.** CS allows the parallel trends assumption to hold either unconditionally or *conditionally on covariates*, combining outcome regression with inverse probability weighting (IPW) in a doubly-robust framework. As the package documentation notes, "the parallel trends assumption holds only after conditioning on covariates" and "since inverse probability weighting is non-parametric… the Doubly-Robust estimator can handle conditional parallel trends more flexibly" [1][2].

**Heterogeneity.** CS imposes no restrictions on treatment-effect heterogeneity across groups or time. The building-block ATT(g,t) estimates are then aggregated into summary parameters (event-study dynamic effects, cohort-specific ATTs, or overall ATT) using user-specified weighting schemes [2].

**Anticipation.** The method accommodates anticipation effects by allowing the researcher to specify anticipation periods that are excluded from the estimation. The R package `did` explicitly reports "Anticipation Periods: 0" by default, making the assumption transparent and adjustable [2].

### 2.2 Sun and Abraham (2021): Interaction-Weighted Estimator

**Mechanism.** Sun and Abraham replace the standard TWFE event-study interaction with *cohort-specific event-study dummies*, preventing the cross-cohort contamination that plagues conventional specifications. "Sun-Abraham replace the TWFE interaction with cohort-specific event-study dummies… Each cohort gets its own event-study path, preventing contamination from other cohorts" [4].

**Parallel trends.** The SA estimator relies on parallel trends using never-treated or not-yet-treated units as the valid comparison group, similar in spirit to CS. However, it does not natively incorporate the doubly-robust IPW framework for conditioning on covariates—relying instead on a more standard regression-adjustment approach.

**Heterogeneity.** Like CS, SA allows fully heterogeneous treatment effects across cohorts and relative time periods. The interaction-weighted structure directly estimates cohort-specific dynamic paths without imposing functional-form restrictions on effect evolution.

**Anticipation.** SA's handling of anticipation is less explicitly developed in the software implementations compared to CS, though the framework allows researchers to exclude lead periods from estimation.

### 2.3 Borusyak, Jaravel, and Spiess (2024): Imputation Estimator

**Mechanism.** The BJS imputation estimator takes a fundamentally different approach. Rather than constructing pairwise comparisons, it fits a TWFE model exclusively to *untreated observations* to estimate unit and time fixed effects, then imputes counterfactual outcomes for treated observations. "It imputes counterfactual outcomes for treated observations from a two-way fixed-effects model fitted to *untreated* data only, then averages the treated residuals" [3]. "Borusyak, Jaravel, and Spiess prove this is the efficient (minimum-variance) unbiased linear estimator of the ATT under the parallel-trends and no-anticipation assumptions, when errors are homoskedastic" [3].

**Parallel trends.** BJS requires parallel trends and homoskedasticity for its efficiency guarantees. Unlike CS, it does not natively offer a doubly-robust framework for conditional parallel trends, making it potentially less flexible when treatment assignment is correlated with observable covariates.

**Heterogeneity.** The imputation approach allows fully heterogeneous treatment effects. The authors demonstrate that "conventional regression-based estimators fail to provide unbiased estimates of relevant estimands absent strong restrictions on treatment-effect homogeneity," while their procedure imposes no such restrictions [5].

**Anticipation.** BJS relies on a strict no-anticipation assumption. "Anticipation effects bias both the imputation and the resulting ATT" [3]. This is a hard constraint—unlike CS, which allows anticipation periods to be flexibly specified, BJS's efficiency proofs break down if anticipation contaminates the untreated-observation pool.

## 4. Addressing Roth's (2022) Pre-Trend Testing Concerns

Roth (2022) identified a critical problem: conventional pre-trend tests in event-study designs have low power against relevant violations, and conditioning inference on passing such tests introduces *post-selection bias*—estimates that survive pre-testing are systematically biased toward finding null pre-trends.

Each estimator family responds to this challenge differently:

**Callaway-Sant'Anna** addresses pre-trend concerns through its group-time ATT structure. Pre-treatment group-time ATTs serve as direct tests of parallel trends for each cohort-period combination, providing a more granular assessment than aggregate pre-trend coefficients. The framework reports pre-test p-values for each group-time combination, enabling researchers to assess violations at the level of specific cohorts rather than globally [2].

**Borusyak-Jaravel-Spiess** explicitly claims to circumvent Roth's problem. The authors state their "strategy circumvents the inference problems after pre-testing that were pointed out by Roth (2022), under spherical errors" [5]. Their approach uses a *joint Wald test* across pre-treatment placebo coefficients as the standard parallel-trends check, which provides a unified statistical test rather than relying on visual inspection of individual lead coefficients [3]. This joint test avoids the cherry-picking problem Roth identified, though it still requires that the test itself has adequate power.

**Sun-Abraham** addresses pre-trends through cohort-specific lead coefficients, which are uncontaminated by the cross-cohort weighting that biases conventional event-study leads. However, the framework does not offer a specific solution to Roth's post-selection inference problem beyond providing cleaner estimates of pre-treatment dynamics.

Crucially, none of these estimators *solves* the fundamental tension Roth identifies—that pre-trend tests may lack power to detect economically meaningful violations. They provide more rigorous and transparent testing frameworks, but the underlying identification challenge remains. Sensitivity analyses (e.g., Roth's own `HonestDiD` package) that bound treatment effects under violations of parallel trends remain complementary tools regardless of which estimator is chosen.

## 5. Adoption in Applied Economics Journals (2020–2024)

**Note: Quantitative adoption data from systematic content analysis of AER, QJE, and JPE was not available from the sources consulted.** The following assessment is based on qualitative indicators from methodological discussions and software documentation.

Based on available evidence, **Callaway-Sant'Anna appears to have achieved the widest adoption** among the three estimators in applied microeconomics, likely driven by several factors:

1. **Publication timing and visibility.** CS was circulating as a working paper from 2019 and published in *Journal of Econometrics* in 2021, giving it first-mover advantage during the critical period when the staggered DiD critique was gaining traction.

2. **Software maturity.** The `did` package in R (maintained by Callaway) provided a comprehensive, well-documented implementation early, including built-in aggregation, pre-testing, and visualization tools [2].

3. **Flexibility.** The doubly-robust property—allowing researchers to condition on covariates non-parametrically—makes CS particularly attractive in labor and health economics applications where selection into treatment is often correlated with observables.

4. **Intuitive output.** The group-time ATT decomposition maps naturally onto applied researchers' existing mental models of event studies and cohort-specific analyses.

The BJS imputation estimator has gained traction more recently, particularly among methodologically sophisticated researchers who value its efficiency guarantees. Its 2024 publication in *Review of Economic Studies* after extended circulation suggests growing influence. Sun-Abraham has seen adoption primarily through its integration into Stata's `eventstudyinteract` command and R's `fixest` package, benefiting from the large Stata user base in labor economics.

Regarding **justification practices**, there is no systematic evidence from the sources consulted on the frequency with which authors in top journals justify their estimator choice through Monte Carlo simulations versus sensitivity analyses. Anecdotally, applied papers increasingly report results from multiple estimators as a robustness check, though the primary specification often defaults to CS or TWFE with Bacon decomposition diagnostics.
