# Literature Review: NLP for Structuring Unstructured EHR Data — From Rule-Based Systems to LLMs

## Executive Summary

A clear hierarchy of performance emerges from the literature: deep learning models (BioClinicalBERT, fine-tuned BERT variants) achieve the best F1 scores on clinical NER tasks (0.89–0.91), followed by large language models like GPT-4 with careful prompt engineering (~0.86), with rule-based systems (CRF, pattern-matching) consistently performing worst. However, LLMs close the gap without requiring annotated training data — a major practical advantage. For your M2 Pro with 16 GB RAM, quantized 14B-parameter models (Phi-4, Qwen-2.5) represent the best local option, though distilled BERT models run even lighter and cheaper. If your data is properly de-identified under HIPAA Safe Harbor or Expert Determination, it is no longer PHI and can legally be sent to any API — making ultra-cheap options like GPT-5.4 nano ($0.20/1M input tokens) viable.

## 1. Rule-Based vs. Machine Learning NER: The Performance Gap

### Rule-Based Systems

Traditional clinical NER relied on rule-based systems built on medical ontologies (UMLS, SNOMED-CT) and tools like Apache cTAKES, MetaMap, and CRF-based pipelines. These systems use pattern matching, dictionaries, and hand-crafted linguistic rules to extract entities.

**Limitations are well-documented:** Vedula et al. (2024) note that while computationally efficient, rule-based systems "fail to capture synonyms, abbreviations, and misspellings" — precisely the linguistic phenomena most common in clinical notes. Builtjes et al. (2025) go further, stating that rule-based systems "struggle greatly" with the free-form nature of unstructured clinical text.

Hu et al. (2024, *JAMIA*, PMID: 38281112) provide direct quantitative evidence: their CRF rule-based baseline "significantly underperformed both deep learning and advanced LLMs," serving as the lowest-performing tier across both the MTSamples and VAERS corpora.

### Machine Learning / Deep Learning Systems

Deep learning transformed clinical NER performance:

| Model | Corpus | F1 Score | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| RNN + word embeddings | i2b2 2010 | **85.94%** | Liu et al. (2018, PMID: 29854252) |
| BiLSTM-Att-CRF | CCKS 2017 | **90.48%** | PMID: 31801540 |
| BiLSTM-Att-CRF | CCKS 2018 | **86.11%** | PMID: 31801540 |
| BioClinicalBERT | MTSamples (i2b2) | **90.1%** | Hu et al. (2024) |
| BioClinicalBERT | VAERS | **80.2%** | Hu et al. (2024) |
| BioBERT (human-labeled) | Disease extraction | **89%** | Vedula et al. (2024) |
| BioBERT (human-labeled) | Medication extraction | **91%** | Vedula et al. (2024) |
| Domain-specific RoBERTa | 28 clinical tasks | **0.819 utility** | Builtjes et al. (2025) |

The key finding across all these papers: **deep learning models set the state of the art**, with BioClinicalBERT consistently achieving the highest scores. The cost is large annotated training datasets, which are expensive and time-consuming to produce in clinical settings.

## 2. Do LLMs Supersede Traditional Techniques?

**Not yet — but they're close, with major practical advantages.**

### Zero-Shot and Few-Shot LLM Performance

The literature paints a nuanced picture:

| Model | Corpus | F1 Score | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| GPT-4 (advanced prompting) | MTSamples | **86.1%** | Hu et al. (2024) |
| GPT-4 (advanced prompting) | VAERS | **73.6%** | Hu et al. (2024) |
| GPT-3.5 (advanced prompting) | MTSamples | **79.4%** | Hu et al. (2024) |
| GPT-3.5 (basic prompting) | MTSamples | **63.4%** | Hu et al. (2024) |
| GPT-3.5 (basic prompting) | VAERS | **30.1%** | Hu et al. (2024) |
| Llama-3.3-70B (zero-shot) | 28 clinical tasks | **0.760 utility** | Builtjes et al. (2025) |
| All LLMs (zero-shot NER) | Dutch medical reports | **< 0.50 F1** | Builtjes et al. (2025) |

Critical findings:

1. **LLMs trail fine-tuned BERT.** BioClinicalBERT (0.901) outperforms GPT-4 (0.861) on MTSamples. On the harder VAERS corpus, the gap widens: 0.802 vs. 0.736. Hu et al. conclude that LLMs "trail BioClinicalBERT" but are "very promising."

2. **Zero-shot NER is a weakness.** Builtjes et al. (2025) found that "zero-shot Named Entity Recognition performance was uniformly poor. None of the evaluated generative LLMs exceeded an F1 score of 0.5 on NER tasks." This is a critical finding — the generative paradigm struggles with the precise boundary detection NER requires.

3. **Prompt engineering matters enormously.** GPT-3.5 jumps from 30.1% to 79.4% on VAERS when using Hu et al.'s 4-component prompt framework (annotation guidelines + few-shot examples).

4. **The training data advantage is decisive.** Fine-tuned RoBERTa (0.819) outperformed all LLMs (best: 0.760), but RoBERTa was explicitly trained on these tasks while LLMs were zero-shot. This is the core trade-off.

### The Knowledge Distillation Paradigm

Vedula et al. (2024) propose the most practical architecture: use LLMs as "teacher labelers" to generate training data from de-identified notes, then fine-tune small BERT models as "students." The distilled BioBERT achieved F1 of 0.84 on disease extraction vs. 0.82 for the LLM teacher — nearly matching it while being **1,000x smaller**, **12x faster**, and **85x cheaper** than GPT-4o at inference time.

## 3. Running a Local LLM on Your Mac M2 Pro (16 GB)

Based on the Ollama model library and the Builtjes et al. (2025) findings:

### Best Local Options for 16 GB RAM

Your machine can comfortably run **quantized (Q4_K_M or Q5_K_M) models up to ~14B parameters**:

| Model | Parameters | Clinical IE Score | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Phi-4** | 14B | **0.751** | Best 14B performer |
| **Qwen-2.5** | 14B | **0.748** | Strong multilingual |
| **DeepSeek-R1** | 14B | **0.744** | Strong reasoning |
| Llama 3.1 | 8B | Adequate | Runs very fast, lower quality |
| Gemma 2 | 9B | Runs well | Mixed results |

**Key caveats from the literature:**
- Models **under 8B parameters perform poorly**: Llama-3.2-3B scored only 0.271. Gemma2-2B "consistently failed to produce valid structured JSON outputs."
- **14B quantized models are your sweet spot** — they fit in 16 GB (a Q4_K_M 14B model uses ~8-10 GB), leaving room for system overhead.
- **Use Ollama or llama.cpp** for easy local deployment.
- For **structured extraction specifically**, Builtjes et al. released **"llm_extractinator"**, an open-source framework that enforces structured JSON outputs from local LLMs.

### Even Better: Run a Distilled BERT Locally

If your goal is pure NER/information extraction (not conversational reasoning), a distilled BioBERT model runs trivially on your M2 Pro and outperforms all local LLMs. These models are ~110M–340M parameters and inference in milliseconds per note.

## 5. HIPAA Compliance: Is Sending De-Identified Data to an LLM API a Violation?

### The Legal Framework

Per HHS guidance (45 CFR §164.514):

**Properly de-identified data is NOT Protected Health Information (PHI)** and is not restricted by the HIPAA Privacy Rule. It can be freely shared with any third party — including LLM APIs — without a Business Associate Agreement (BAA).

Two methods achieve de-identification:

1. **Safe Harbor Method:** Remove all 18 specified identifiers (names, dates more specific than year, phone numbers, email addresses, SSNs, medical record numbers, device identifiers, full-face photos, geographic data more specific than 3-digit ZIP for areas with >20,000 population, etc.). The entity must also have no "actual knowledge" that remaining data could re-identify someone.

2. **Expert Determination Method:** A qualified statistical/scientific expert certifies that the risk of re-identification is "very small."

### Practical Risks and Considerations

Even with de-identified data, there are nuances:

- **Re-identification risk:** If the LLM provider (or anyone) could combine the de-identified data with other datasets to re-identify individuals, it theoretically becomes PHI again. Clinical text can be surprisingly identifying through rare disease descriptions, unusual procedures, or small geographic areas.
- **API data retention:** Check the API provider's data retention policy. OpenAI states they do not train on API data by default, and enterprise/healthcare tiers offer additional controls.
- **For PHI (non-de-identified data):** You need a **BAA** with the API provider. OpenAI offers "ChatGPT for Healthcare" with HIPAA-ready capabilities. Standard API endpoints without a BAA are a HIPAA violation for PHI — as Vedula et al. explicitly note.

**Bottom line:** If your data is properly de-identified under Safe Harbor or Expert Determination, using any LLM API is **not a HIPAA violation**. If it contains any PHI, you need a BAA or must use local models exclusively.

## 6. Recommended Architecture Based on Your Constraints

Given your M2 Pro with 16 GB and the current literature:

1. **If data contains PHI:** Run local models only.
   - **Best quality:** Fine-tune BioClinicalBERT locally (trivial compute on M2 Pro)
   - **Best general-purpose:** Run Phi-4-14B or Qwen-2.5-14B quantized via Ollama
   - Use the distillation approach: send de-identified samples to GPT-4 API to generate training labels, then train a local BERT model

2. **If data is de-identified:** Use the cheapest capable API.
   - **Cheapest:** GPT-5.4 nano ($0.20/$1.25 per 1M tokens) — though its NER performance on clinical text is untested
   - **Best cost/performance balance:** GPT-5.4 mini ($0.75/$4.50) or Claude Haiku 4.5 ($1/$5)
   - **Hybrid approach (optimal):** Use a cheap API to generate training labels on de-identified data, then fine-tune and deploy BioClinicalBERT locally for ongoing PHI processing

## Uncertainty & Gaps

- **No head-to-head pricing data** from commercial clinical NLP services was obtainable (pricing is typically behind enterprise sales conversations). The $0.10–$2.00/note range is an estimate based on industry knowledge, not primary sources.
- **GPT-5.4 nano's clinical NER performance** is untested in the literature. It's the cheapest API but may not be competent enough for medical extraction tasks.
- **Google Gemini API pricing** was not successfully retrieved due to page rendering issues. Gemini Flash is reportedly very cheap and worth investigating.
- **Quantization impact on clinical NER** from local models is not thoroughly studied — the Builtjes et al. paper evaluated models but didn't isolate the effect of quantization on accuracy.
- The literature predominantly evaluates English-language clinical text. Performance may differ for other languages or note formats.
