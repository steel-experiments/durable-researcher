# Turo vs. Getaround: A Tale of Two Car-Sharing Companies (2020–2025)

## A Quick Introduction for Newcomers

Before diving in, a few terms worth knowing:

- **Valuation**: The dollar amount the market (or investors) believe a company is worth. Think of it like appraising a house — except the "house" is an entire business.
- **Venture capital (VC)**: Money invested by professional investors into early-stage, high-growth companies in exchange for a share of ownership. These investors hope the company grows massively so their stake becomes very valuable.
- **IPO (Initial Public Offering)**: The traditional process of listing a company on a stock exchange so anyone can buy and sell its shares. It's rigorous, time-consuming, and heavily regulated.
- **SPAC (Special Purpose Acquisition Company)**: A faster, less-regulated shortcut to going public. A "blank check" company is created, raises money, and then merges with a private company to take it public. SPACs became wildly popular in 2020–2021 but fell out of favor when many proved to be poor investments.
- **Delisting**: When a stock exchange removes a company's shares because they've fallen below minimum price or financial requirements. It's a sign of severe distress.
- **Market cap (capitalization)**: The total value of all a company's shares. If a company has 10 million shares at $10 each, its market cap is $100 million.

## What These Companies Do

Turo and Getaround are both **peer-to-peer car rental platforms** — think of them as "Airbnb for cars." Instead of owning fleets of vehicles like Hertz or Enterprise, they operate apps that connect people who own cars with people who want to rent them. Car owners list their vehicles, set their own prices, and the platform handles booking, insurance, and payment — keeping a commission on each rental.

Despite operating in the same industry and facing the same economic storms, the two companies' fortunes diverged dramatically.

## Getaround: From $1.2 Billion to Shutdown

### Pre-2020 Foundation
Getaround was founded in 2009 and raised roughly **$600 million** in private venture capital over its lifetime. Its biggest backer was SoftBank, a Japanese investment giant, which led a **$300 million funding round** in 2018 (with Toyota also participating). In 2019, Getaround spent **$300 million** to acquire Drivy, a European car-sharing platform, in a bold bet on international expansion.

### 2020 — The COVID Collapse
When the COVID-19 pandemic shut down travel worldwide in early 2020, Getaround's vehicle usage **plummeted by more than 75%**. With no one renting cars, revenue dried up almost overnight. The company was forced to **lay off approximately 150 employees — roughly 25% of its entire workforce** — and reportedly began searching for a buyer to avoid running out of cash entirely.

To survive, Getaround managed to raise **$140 million in emergency funding** from investors including Reid Hoffman (co-founder of LinkedIn) and Mark Pincus (founder of Zynga). This kept the lights on, but at a cost: the company was now under immense pressure to prove it could eventually become profitable.

### 2022 — The Ill-Fated Public Debut
In December 2022, Getaround went public by merging with a SPAC called InterPrivate II Acquisition Corp. The deal initially valued Getaround at approximately **$1.2 billion**. Its shares began trading on the New York Stock Exchange under the ticker symbol **"GETR."**

The outcome was catastrophic. On its very first day of trading, the stock **plunged more than 65%**. Why? By late 2022, the economic environment had shifted dramatically. Inflation was soaring, and the U.S. Federal Reserve had been aggressively raising interest rates to combat it. Higher interest rates make investors wary of risky, money-losing startups because borrowing becomes expensive and safer investments (like government bonds) become more attractive. The SPAC market, once red-hot, had turned ice-cold. Investors simply didn't believe Getaround could deliver the growth needed to justify its price tag.

### 2023 — Unraveling
The stock continued its downward spiral through 2023. Getaround **vacated its San Francisco headquarters** — a telling sign of a company trying to shed every possible expense. Revenue was insufficient to cover operating costs, and the collapsing share price meant the company could not easily raise more money by selling new shares (because each share was now worth pennies).

### 2024 — Near-Total Collapse
By 2024, Getaround's market capitalization — the total value of all its shares combined — had collapsed to approximately **$24 million**, a staggering **98% decline** from its $1.2 billion SPAC valuation just two years earlier.

The consequences rippled through the organization:
- **Another 30% of remaining staff were laid off** in a desperate bid to cut costs.
- The **New York Stock Exchange delisted Getaround's stock**, removing it from the premier U.S. stock exchange. Shares moved to the OTC (over-the-counter) market — a less-regulated trading venue sometimes described as the "minor leagues" of stock markets, where struggling companies end up.

### 2025 — The End of the Road
On **February 12, 2025**, Getaround **ceased all operations in the United States**, citing liquidity problems (meaning it simply ran out of accessible cash). The company retains only its European business, a remnant of the 2019 Drivy acquisition. The U.S. platform — once operating in over 1,000 cities — was shut down entirely.

## Turo: Cautious and Standing Tall

### Pre-2020 Foundation
Turo (originally named RelayRides) was founded in 2010 and rebranded in 2015. Its most significant financial milestone came in **July 2019**, when the media holding company **IAC invested $250 million**, valuing Turo at over **$1 billion**. In startup jargon, this made Turo a "unicorn" — a privately held company worth more than a billion dollars.

### 2020 — COVID Shock, Then Recovery
Like Getaround, Turo suffered when the pandemic hit. However, Turo **recovered much faster**. The reason: a shift in consumer behavior. Americans wary of air travel and traditional rental agencies turned to road trips in private cars — exactly what Turo offers. Turo also pivoted its international strategy, **shutting down private peer-to-peer listings in Germany** in April 2020 and focusing only on commercial hosts there to reduce risk.

### 2022 — Expansion and IPO Preparation
Turo used the post-pandemic recovery to grow aggressively. It **acquired OuiCar**, a French car-sharing service; **re-entered the New York State market** (from which it had been banned since 2014 due to insurance regulatory issues); and **expanded operations to Australia**.

Rising new and used car prices also worked in Turo's favor: consumers priced out of buying vehicles looked to renting as a cheaper alternative, boosting Turo's revenue.

Late in 2022, Turo **filed confidential paperwork with U.S. regulators to begin the IPO process** — signaling its intent to go public through the traditional (more rigorous) route rather than a SPAC.

### 2023 — Growth Stalls, IPO Delayed
In 2023, Turo's **growth rate slowed significantly** — described by *TechCrunch* as having "cratered." The broader economic environment (inflation, high interest rates, and consumer uncertainty) took a toll. However, unlike Getaround, Turo **remained profitable** during this slowdown.

Crucially, Turo watched the public market's brutal treatment of companies like Getaround and made a strategic decision: **it repeatedly delayed its IPO**. By staying private, Turo avoided the public stock price collapse that destroyed Getaround's valuation. Private companies aren't subject to daily stock market whims — their valuations are adjusted periodically by investors, providing more stability.

### 2024 — Steady as She Goes
As of March 2024, Turo hosted **360,000 active vehicle listings** on its platform and was still publicly stating it was preparing for an eventual IPO. No significant employee layoffs were reported during this period.

### 2025 — A Crisis and a New Product
On **January 1, 2025**, Turo faced a severe public relations crisis when **two separate deadly incidents** occurred on the same day involving vehicles rented through its platform: a truck ramming attack in New Orleans and a Tesla Cybertruck explosion outside the Trump International Hotel in Las Vegas. Turo's CEO stated that the attackers' rental applications had contained no warning signs. While this was a reputational blow rather than a financial one, it underscored the platform's exposure to misuse.

In **October 2025**, Turo launched **multi-month rental options**, expanding into the long-term rental segment — a sign that the company was still investing in growth.

## Why Such Different Outcomes?

Three factors explain why Turo survived and Getaround didn't:

1. **Timing of going public.** Getaround went public at the worst possible moment — late 2022, when investors were fleeing risky, unprofitable companies due to rising interest rates. Turo, wisely, held off. Once a company is public, its valuation is set daily by the stock market. A collapsing stock price makes it nearly impossible to raise new funds, creating a death spiral.

2. **Profitability.** Turo operated leanly enough to turn a profit. Getaround burned through cash, spending $300 million on a European acquisition just before the pandemic, then spending more to go public. Without profits, Getaround depended entirely on investor confidence — which evaporated.

3. **Scale.** With 360,000 active listings, Turo had a much larger and more diversified network of cars and renters, making it more resilient to economic shocks. Getaround's smaller footprint meant any downturn hit harder.
