# Your Project, Explained: NLP on EHR Data for Asian Subgroup Autoimmune Disease Prevalence

---

## What Your Professor Actually Means (The Big Picture)

Here's the problem your professor wants you to solve: In healthcare data, patients of Asian descent are almost always lumped into a single "Asian" category. But "Asian" encompasses wildly diverse populations — Chinese, Indian, Filipino, Vietnamese, Korean, Japanese, and many others — with dramatically different health profiles. Treating them as one group hides critical health disparities.

Your professor wants you to:

1. Take a dataset of **de-identified patient records** (digital medical charts with names/SSNs removed for privacy).
2. Use **Natural Language Processing (NLP)** — essentially, computer algorithms that can "read" text — to scan doctors' notes and figure out the *specific* Asian subgroup each patient belongs to (e.g., Chinese vs. Vietnamese vs. Korean).
3. **Match** those subgroups to the same categories used by **CDC WONDER**, a federal public health database.
4. **Calculate how common autoimmune diseases are** within each specific subgroup (prevalence rates).

That's the whole project. Now let's break it down piece by piece.

## What Do Electronic Health Records Look Like?

Electronic Health Records (EHRs) are digital patient charts. A well-known example is **MIMIC-III**, a free de-identified database from MIT that contains ICU patient data from Beth Israel Deaconess Medical Center. Your data will likely be structured similarly.

### Structured Data (organized in database tables)

These are fields you can easily filter and query, like a spreadsheet:

| Table | What It Contains | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **PATIENTS** | Demographics | `SUBJECT_ID: 12345, GENDER: F, DOB: 1972` |
| **ADMISSIONS** | Hospital visit info | `HADM_ID: 67890, ADMIT_DATE: 2015-03-14` |
| **DIAGNOSES_ICD** | ICD diagnosis codes | `SUBJECT_ID: 12345, ICD9_CODE: 710.0 (SLE)` |
| **PRESCRIPTIONS** | Medications | Drug names, dosages, routes |
| **LABEVENTS** | Lab test results | Blood glucose, CBC results |

**The race field** in these tables typically only says "Asian" — not the specific subgroup. This is the core problem.

### Unstructured Data (free-text clinical notes)

This is stored in a table like **NOTEEVENTS** and contains the actual text doctors and nurses type. This is where the goldmine lives. A de-identified clinical note might look like:

> *"Patient is a 45-year-old Vietnamese female presenting with joint pain and fatigue. History of photosensitivity rash. Family history notable for lupus in maternal aunt. Patient emigrated from Ho Chi Minh City in 1998. Speaks Vietnamese and English."*

Notice: the structured field says "Asian," but the note reveals **Vietnamese**. That's exactly what you need NLP to extract.

## What NLP Techniques Will You Use?

You'll be applying NLP to scan those unstructured clinical notes and automatically identify mentions of specific Asian nationalities, ethnicities, or countries of origin. Here are the key techniques:

### 1. Named Entity Recognition (NER)
Training a model to find and classify specific words as "nationality" or "ethnicity" entities. For example, recognizing that "Filipino" or "Hmong" or "Pakistani" refers to a demographic group.

### 2. Rule-Based Matching / Regex
The simplest and most practical approach for this project. You create a predefined list (a "dictionary") of terms and search for them:

- **Demonyms:** Chinese, Korean, Vietnamese, Japanese, Filipino, Indian, Pakistani, Cambodian, Hmong, Laotian, Thai, etc.
- **Country references paired with context words:** "born in Korea," "emigrated from Vietnam," "of Chinese descent"
- **Language references:** "speaks Tagalog," "Vietnamese-speaking patient"

### 3. Context Detection (Negation/Uncertainty)
You need to distinguish between confirmed information vs. negated or uncertain mentions. For example:
- ✅ *"Patient is a Korean female"* → Count this
- ❌ *"Patient is not of Japanese descent"* → Ignore this
- ❓ *"Possibly of Filipino heritage"* → Flag as uncertain

### 4. Section Detection
Clinical notes have sections (Chief Complaint, History of Present Illness, Family History, Social History). Subgroup mentions are most likely to appear in **Social History** or **History of Present Illness**. Detecting sections helps you target your search.

### The Go-To Tool: MedSpaCy

**MedSpaCy** (built on top of **spaCy**) is a Python library designed specifically for clinical NLP. It includes:

| Component | What It Does | Why You Need It |
|---|---|---|
| `medspacy.ner` | Rule-based entity extraction with `TargetRule` | Extract Asian subgroup mentions |
| `medspacy.context` | ConText algorithm for negation/uncertainty | Filter out negated or hypothetical mentions |
| `medspacy.section_detection` | Identifies clinical sections | Target relevant note sections |
| `medspacy.postprocess` | Clean up and standardize extracted entities | Map extractions to CDC categories |

## What Subgroups Are in CDC WONDER?

**CDC WONDER** (Wide-ranging ONline Data for Epidemiologic Research) is a public-facing CDC database that tracks mortality, disease, and population data with an ad-hoc query system.

According to federal data standards (OMB Directive 15, used by both the Census Bureau and CDC), the primary **Asian subgroups** you need to extract and match are:

| CDC WONDER Category | Example Terms You'd Search For in Notes |
|---|---|
| **Asian Indian** | Indian, South Asian, Punjabi, Gujarati, Hindi-speaking |
| **Chinese** | Chinese, Mandarin-speaking, Cantonese, from mainland China |
| **Filipino** | Filipino, Filipina, Tagalog-speaking, from the Philippines |
| **Japanese** | Japanese, from Japan |
| **Korean** | Korean, from South Korea, Korean-speaking |
| **Vietnamese** | Vietnamese, from Vietnam, Vietnamese-speaking |
| **Other Asian** | Hmong, Laotian, Cambodian, Thai, Pakistani, Bangladeshi, Sri Lankan, Burmese, Indonesian, Malaysian, etc. |

Your goal is to build an NLP pipeline that takes raw clinical text and maps every mention to one of these standard categories so your results are directly comparable to CDC WONDER data.

## How to Apply This to Autoimmune Disease Prevalence

### Step-by-Step Logic

**Autoimmune diseases** are conditions where the immune system attacks the body. Common examples include:

| Disease | ICD-10 Code(s) |
|---|---|
| Systemic Lupus Erythematosus (SLE) | M32.x |
| Rheumatoid Arthritis | M05–M06 |
| Type 1 Diabetes | E10.x |
| Autoimmune Thyroiditis (Hashimoto's) | E06.3 |
| Graves' Disease | E05.0 |
| Multiple Sclerosis | G35 |
| Psoriasis/Psoriatic Arthritis | L40.x |

### Prevalence Rate Formula

**Prevalence** = (Number of patients with the disease in a subgroup) ÷ (Total number of patients in that subgroup) × multiplier

For example: If your EHR has 500 Korean patients and 15 have lupus, the prevalence of lupus among Korean patients in your dataset is 15/500 = 3%.

## Your Research Plan: Step by Step

### Phase 1 — Foundation (Weeks 1–2)
- [ ] **Get data access.** Complete any required training (e.g., CITI training for human subjects research if using MIMIC-III or similar databases).
- [ ] **Explore the data.** Load the EHR dataset. Identify the structured fields (demographics, ICD codes, race) and unstructured fields (clinical notes).
- [ ] **Learn the schema.** Understand which tables link to which (patient ID → notes → diagnoses).
- [ ] **Download CDC WONDER data.** Go to wonder.cdc.gov, query for autoimmune disease data by race/ethnicity (specifically Asian subgroups), and download the results so you know exactly what categories you're matching to.

### Phase 2 — Dictionary Building (Week 3)
- [ ] **Create a keyword dictionary** mapping terms to CDC WONDER Asian subgroups. For example: `{"Chinese": ["Chinese", "Mandarin", "Cantonese", "mainland China", "from China"], "Vietnamese": ["Vietnamese", "Vietnam", "Ho Chi Minh", "Saigon"], ...}`
- [ ] **Curate an ICD-10 code list** for autoimmune diseases you'll study. Start with 5–10 well-documented conditions (SLE, rheumatoid arthritis, type 1 diabetes, Hashimoto's, etc.).

### Phase 3 — NLP Pipeline Development (Weeks 4–6)
- [ ] **Set up your Python environment** with pandas, spaCy, and medspaCy.
- [ ] **Build the extraction pipeline:**
  1. Preprocess clinical notes (lowercase, sentence split).
  2. Apply rule-based matching using your keyword dictionary.
  3. Apply context detection to filter out negated/uncertain mentions.
  4. Extract and map identified entities to CDC WONDER categories.
- [ ] **Validate your results.** Manually review a sample of 100–200 notes to check accuracy (precision and recall). How many subgroups did you correctly identify? How many did you miss?

### Phase 4 — Disease Integration & Analysis (Weeks 7–9)
- [ ] **Query ICD codes.** Filter patients with autoimmune disease diagnoses from the structured diagnosis tables.
- [ ] **Merge datasets.** Join your NLP-extracted subgroup labels with the autoimmune disease flags.
- [ ] **Calculate prevalence rates** for each autoimmune disease within each Asian subgroup.
- [ ] **Compare to CDC WONDER.** How do your EHR-derived rates compare to national statistics? Are certain subgroups disproportionately affected?

### Phase 5 — Reporting (Weeks 10–12)
- [ ] **Visualize results** — bar charts of prevalence by subgroup, comparison tables vs. CDC WONDER.
- [ ] **Write up findings** — document your NLP methodology, accuracy metrics, and prevalence results.
- [ ] **Discuss limitations** — NLP isn't perfect; not all notes mention ethnicity; sample sizes may be small for some subgroups.
