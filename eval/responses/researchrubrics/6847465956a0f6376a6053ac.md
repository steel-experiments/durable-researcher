# How Much Should We Change Our Lives for Future Generations? Climate Change, Ethics, and Policy

## Executive Summary

The question of what the current generation owes future people sits at the intersection of moral philosophy and climate policy. Multiple ethical frameworks — utilitarianism, deontology, and contractualism — converge on the conclusion that we bear substantial obligations, though they differ on the grounds and extent of those duties. The non-identity problem, introduced by Derek Parfit, complicates any appeal to individual harm, but does not dissolve our moral responsibilities. Real-world policies like Wales's Well-being of Future Generations Act and Sweden's carbon tax demonstrate that intergenerational responsibility can be codified into law without wrecking current prosperity. Counterarguments rooted in discounting and uncertainty carry force, but the precautionary logic of catastrophic risk and the weight of philosophical consensus suggest the current generation must accept significant, though not unlimited, lifestyle transformation.

## 1. Ethical Frameworks for Intergenerational Duty

Three major ethical frameworks offer distinct answers to how much we must sacrifice for those not yet born.

### Utilitarianism: Maximizing Welfare Across Time

Classical utilitarianism, tracing from Henry Sidgwick through to contemporary philosophers like Peter Singer, holds that the moral status of all sentient beings — including future ones — must be counted equally in the aggregate of well-being [1], [3]. Under this framework, the current generation is obligated to reduce emissions and alter consumption patterns insofar as the long-term benefits to future people outweigh the costs borne today. Nicholas Stern's 2006 *Stern Review on the Economics of Climate Change* is the canonical utilitarian policy document. By applying a near-zero "pure time discount rate" of 0.1%, Stern concluded that inaction could cost 20% of global GDP "each year, now and forever," justifying an immediate investment of 1% of global GDP to stabilize greenhouse gases below 550ppm [3], [4].

Utilitarianism's strength is its impartiality: it refuses to privilege currently living people simply because they exist now. Its weakness is that it reduces all moral questions to cost-benefit calculations, potentially justifying severe sacrifices by the present generation if the numbers demand it. Cambridge economist Partha Dasgupta argued that Stern's parameters implied an absurdly high savings rate of 97.5%, though Brad DeLong later corrected this to a more reasonable 22.5% [4].

### Deontology: The Duty Not to Harm

Deontological frameworks ground obligations in rights and duties rather than consequences. Philosopher Henry Shue, in his influential work *Climate Justice*, argues for a strict "duty not to impose harm" on future generations, regardless of economic cost to the present [2], [5]. Under this view, our fossil-fuel-dependent lifestyles violate the basic rights of people who will inherit a degraded planet — and this violation is wrong even if aggregate welfare is somehow maximized.

Deontology's advantage is that it refuses to sacrifice individuals for the greater good. Its challenge is the **non-identity problem** (discussed below), which threatens to undermine the very concept of "harm" to future people. It also faces institutional obstacles: democratic political systems operate on short electoral cycles, systematically biasing policy toward the preferences of currently living voters [1], [2].

### Contractualism: What No One Could Reasonably Reject

Contractualism, particularly in the tradition of John Rawls and Thomas Scanlon, grounds obligation in the idea of mutual justifiability. Rawls's **"just savings principle"** holds that each generation has a duty to preserve and pass on sufficient resources and institutional foundations so that subsequent generations can sustain a just society [1]. This is not unlimited sacrifice — it requires only what would be agreed to by rational agents concerned with maintaining just institutions across time.

Scanlon's contractualism, articulated in *What We Owe to Each Other*, demands that our actions be defensible by principles no one could reasonably reject [2]. Because severe climate change would compromise the basic living conditions of future people, contractualism dictates a strong moral obligation to act — one grounded not in aggregate welfare maximization but in the inviolability of each person's basic interests.

**Comparison:** Utilitarianism demands whatever maximizes total well-being, which could require either more or less sacrifice than other frameworks depending on the numbers. Deontology demands we not violate rights, full stop. Contractualism demands we act on principles that are mutually justifiable. In practice, all three converge on the conclusion that substantial action is required, but they justify it on different grounds — aggregate welfare, rights protection, and mutual reasonableness, respectively.

## 2. The Non-Identity Problem

Perhaps the most philosophically destabilizing challenge to climate ethics is Derek Parfit's **non-identity problem**, formulated in his landmark 1984 work *Reasons and Persons* [1], [3].

The argument runs as follows: different climate policies today will alter the circumstances of human life — migration patterns, economic conditions, who meets whom — over decades and centuries. These altered circumstances will change which specific conceptions occur, meaning that entirely different individuals will exist depending on which policies we adopt. A person born into a climate-ravaged world in 2100 would simply not have existed under a different policy regime. Therefore, that person cannot coherently claim to have been "harmed" by our inaction: they would not exist at all without it. Under the standard **"person-affecting view"** — where an act is wrong only if it makes some specific person worse off — our current emissions do not wrong anyone [1], [3].

This creates a dangerous philosophical loophole. If no future individual is made worse off *relative to their own alternative existence*, then standard harm-based obligations appear to dissolve.

**Responses to the non-identity problem:**

- **The Impersonal Approach:** The wrongness of climate destruction does not depend on harming specific individuals. Causing a future of severely compromised human life is objectively wrong, regardless of who specifically inhabits it [2], [3]. We have duties not to the *identities* of future people but to the *state of the world* we leave behind.

- **The Interest Theory of Rights:** Even though future people cannot currently exercise or demand rights, if our actions severely frustrate their future subsistence interests, we violate their rights regardless [1]. This counters the **Will Theory** of rights, which argues that only beings who can currently claim and enforce rights possess them.

- **Rawlsian Indirect Reciprocity:** The just savings principle sidesteps the non-identity problem by focusing on maintaining just institutions and a livable environment as a structural requirement, not as a personal obligation owed to specific individuals [1].

Far from undermining climate ethics, the non-identity problem ultimately strengthens the case for systemic change: it forces us to recognize that our obligations are not to particular future persons but to the conditions that make decent human life possible at all.

## 3. Real-World Policies Codifying Intergenerational Responsibility

### Wales's Well-being of Future Generations Act (2015)

Wales passed the WBFGA in 2015, legally obligating 44 public bodies to consider the long-term impacts of their decisions through seven "Well-being Goals" and five "Ways of Working" [2], [5]. The act created the world's first **Future Generations Commissioner**, a position first held by Sophie Howe. Howe leveraged the legislation to block a £1.4 billion motorway expansion, redirecting funds to public transport on the grounds that the road project failed the needs of future people [2].

**Results:** Since 2015, Wales has reduced direct carbon emissions by 27% and become the second-best country globally for recycling rates [2]. The model has gained international traction: the UN adopted a 2024 'Pact for the Future', the EU appointed its first commissioner for intergenerational fairness, and Scotland voted to introduce similar legislation [2], [5].

**Limitations:** The act lacks enforcement mechanisms — the Commissioner can only "name and shame" non-compliant bodies. Goals are criticized as ambiguous and disconnected from lived experience. Local authorities report insufficient guidance and funding for implementation [5].

### Sweden's Carbon Tax

Sweden's carbon tax, instituted in 1991 at SEK 250 (~EUR 23) per tonne of fossil CO₂ and set to reach SEK 1,520 (~EUR 138) by 2026, operationalizes intergenerational responsibility through the "polluter pays" principle [6]. By forcing current consumers to internalize the future environmental costs of their behavior, it makes lifestyle change economically rational. Alongside the EU Emissions Trading System, carbon pricing now covers over 95% of Sweden's fossil emissions. Crucially, the stepwise, gradual increase has proven politically feasible, driving clean technology adoption and behavioral change without halting economic growth [6].

### Comparison of Approaches

| Feature | Wales WBFGA | Sweden Carbon Tax |
|---|---|---|
| **Mechanism** | Institutional mandate + Commissioner oversight | Price signal on emissions |
| **Philosophical basis** | Contractualist/Deontological (duty to future people) | Utilitarian (internalizing externalities) |
| **Scope** | All public body decisions | Fossil fuel consumption |
| **Enforcement** | Weak (name and shame) | Strong (financial penalty) |
| **Evidence of effectiveness** | 27% emissions reduction, cultural shift | 95% emissions coverage, clean tech growth |

## 4. Counterarguments: Discounting and Uncertainty

### The Discounting Objection

Economists like William Nordhaus (2018 Nobel laureate) argue for a high **social discount rate** (~3-6%), contending that future generations will be wealthier and better equipped to handle climate impacts through technology. At a 3% discount rate, $5 trillion in predicted future climate damages shrinks to $382 billion in present value, drastically limiting justified policy ambition [3], [4]. Nordhaus's optimal carbon price was only $7.40/ton — roughly one-tenth of Stern's recommendation. His model assumed a 4°C temperature increase would reduce per-capita GDP by only 2-4% [3].

**Response:** A major flaw in Nordhaus's approach is that macroeconomic aggregates mask catastrophic localized harm. Losing the entire African continent to climate disasters would barely dent global GDP figures, yet would represent incalculable human suffering [3]. Furthermore, Martin Weitzman's **"fat tails"** argument demonstrates that the unprecedented scale of greenhouse gas increases places us in uncharted territory where catastrophic low-probability events dominate expected losses, undermining confidence in gradual approaches [4].

### The Epistemic Uncertainty Objection

Critics argue that we cannot perfectly predict future technology, economic growth, or precise climate outcomes, so we should not impose drastic costs on the current economy based on imperfect models [3], [6].

**Response:** Deep uncertainty cuts both ways. If anything, the possibility of catastrophic, irreversible outcomes (tipping points, runaway feedback loops) strengthens the case for precautionary action. The irreversibility of climate damages — once ice sheets collapse or species go extinct, there is no do-over — means that waiting for certainty is itself a high-stakes gamble [3], [4].

### The "Present Crises First" Objection

Some argue that immediate crises — the cost-of-living emergency, pandemics, housing shortages — must take precedence over speculative future harms [5].

**Response:** This reflects what scholars call an **evolutionary hangover** — a psychological bias toward immediate threats that is maladaptive in the context of slow-moving, existential risks like climate change [5]. The Welsh model shows that integrating long-term thinking into governance need not come at the expense of addressing present needs; it requires expanding the decision-making frame.

## 5. So How Much Should We Change?

The convergence of multiple ethical frameworks — despite their different foundations — on the demand for substantial action is itself significant. Utilitarianism says we must act when benefits outweigh costs, and the Stern Review demonstrates they do. Deontology says we must not violate future people's basic rights, and unchecked emissions clearly do. Contractualism says we must adopt principles no one could reasonably reject, and no future person could reasonably accept a principle permitting the destruction of their livable environment.

The non-identity problem complicates but does not dissolve these obligations. Policies like Wales's WBFGA and Sweden's carbon tax demonstrate that meaningful intergenerational responsibility can be codified into law without requiring economic self-immolation. Sweden's gradual but relentlessly rising carbon price shows that lifestyle change can be phased in practically. Wales's institutional innovation shows that governance can be reshaped around long-term thinking.

The honest answer is uncomfortable: the current generation must accept **substantial** lifestyle and systemic changes — higher energy costs, altered consumption patterns, reduced mobility by air, shifts in diet and land use — not as acts of charity toward hypothetical future people, but as the fulfillment of obligations that every major ethical framework recognizes. The question is not whether to change, but how to distribute those changes fairly within the current generation, ensuring that the costs of intergenerational justice do not fall disproportionately on those least able to bear them.

## Uncertainty & Gaps

- **Quantifying "substantial" change:** None of the sources provide a precise threshold for how much lifestyle sacrifice is ethically required versus excessive. The Stern-Nordhaus debate illustrates that reasonable economists disagree by an order of magnitude on the appropriate carbon price.
- **Intra-generational equity:** The sources focus on intergenerational obligations but touch only lightly on how the burden of climate action should be distributed among currently living people — a critical practical question.
- **Enforcement mechanisms:** The Wales model's reliance on "name and shame" rather than binding enforcement raises questions about whether soft institutional mandates can match the effectiveness of hard economic instruments like carbon taxes.
- **Deep philosophical comparison:** While multiple frameworks were surveyed, a rigorous comparative analysis of how each framework would handle trade-offs between present suffering (e.g., energy poverty) and future harm was not fully available in the sources consulted.
