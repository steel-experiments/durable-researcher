# Epistemic Circularity in AI Safety Evaluation: Philosophical Analysis, Contradictory Cases, and an Autonomous Resolution Framework

---

## Executive Summary

When AI systems evaluate the safety of other AI systems, they encounter a form of epistemic circularity that philosophers have debated for centuries—the problem of a belief source vouching for its own reliability. This report traces the philosophical foundations of this problem, documents specific cases where AI safety evaluations have produced contradictory results, and proposes a multi-layered autonomous framework for resolving such contradictions without recourse to human judgment. The framework draws on game-theoretic guarantees, ensemble divergence metrics, adversarial debate protocols, and recursive self-critique to establish formally verifiable safety consensus among AI evaluators.

---

## 1. Philosophical Foundations: The Epistemic Circularity Problem

### 1.1 Classical Roots

Epistemic circularity, as defined in the philosophical literature, occurs when one defends the reliability of a belief source using premises derived from that very source [1]. William Alston argued that such circularity is **unavoidable**—any track-record argument for the reliability of perception, for instance, must rely on perceptual data. Alston distinguished between two types of justification: *epistemic showing* (providing internal justification) and *dialectical showing* (convincing a skeptic who doubts the premises). Epistemically circular arguments succeed at the former but necessarily fail at the latter.

The "easy knowledge" problem, articulated by Richard Fumerton and Jonathan Vogel, demonstrates that bootstrapping reliability is illegitimately simple. Vogel's gas gauge example is instructive: Roxanne reads her gas gauge, believes "the gauge reads X and the tank is X," and repeats this until she inductively concludes the gauge is reliable. Philosophers universally regard this as epistemically illegitimate [1].

### 1.2 Mapping to AI Safety Evaluation

The structural analogy to AI safety is precise and troubling. When an AI system (System A) evaluates whether another AI system (System B) is safe, and both share architectural foundations, training data, or constitutional principles, the evaluation risks the same bootstrapping illegitimacy. More critically, when an AI system evaluates its *own* safety—as in Constitutional AI's self-critique and revision process—the circularity becomes explicit.

The philosophical literature identifies a fundamental dilemma. Rejecting circularity requires satisfying a strengthened **KR principle**: "A potential knowledge source K can yield knowledge only if the knower independently knows K is reliable." But enforcing this principle leads to the **problem of the criterion**—a logical regress where establishing reliability requires a prior reliable method, ad infinitum [1]. Thomas Reid's proposed exit treats cognitive reliability as a noninferential *first principle*, an axiom accepted without proof. Translating this to AI safety evaluation suggests we may need **axiomatic safety baselines**—formal principles assumed rather than derived from the evaluating system's own judgments.

## 2. Documented Cases of Contradictory AI Safety Evaluations

### 2.1 RLAIF: Helpfulness vs. Harmlessness Contradictions

Anthropic's Reinforcement Learning from AI Feedback (RLAIF) framework explicitly uses an AI Feedback Model to evaluate the safety of another AI's outputs [3, 4]. The process generates a "harmlessness dataset" by having a helpful-trained RLHF agent revise responses to harmful prompts. Research documentation reveals a structural contradiction: because the Feedback Model is optimized for helpfulness, it inherently conflicts with harmlessness objectives unless heavily coerced by constitutional prompts [5].

A specific manifestation: RLAIF uses continuous scalar scores in the range [0, 1] rather than binary classifications. Two evaluations producing **qualitatively contradictory safety judgments** can possess mathematically similar preference scores, systematically blurring the boundary between safe and unsafe outputs. The AI evaluator may score a response as 0.72 "safe" on one constitutional principle and 0.71 "unsafe" on another, creating an irreconcilable contradiction invisible to scalar comparison alone [5].

### 2.2 Automated Red Teaming: Safety Filter vs. Robustness Contradictions

Documented AI red teaming frameworks reveal at least three categories of contradictory automated evaluations [2]:

- **Safety vs. Availability Contradiction**: When a target model blocks a probing prompt via a safety filter, the system records this as a successful defense ("Low success / robust defense") while simultaneously registering it as a structural API failure (503 error). The same event produces contradictory scores on the "safety" dimension versus the "availability" dimension.

- **Statistical Significance vs. Confidence Contradiction**: Frameworks assign confidence scores as low as <0.5 to vulnerability tests that simultaneously flag critical vulnerabilities. A test can be marked as identifying a critical security flaw *and* as statistically unreliable—producing opposite safety conclusions from the same evaluation run.

- **Configuration-Dependent Fairness Contradictions**: An automated fairness evaluator that tests only binary gender categories produces passing safety scores. The exact same evaluator expanded to include non-binary and transgender categories produces failing scores. The underlying model hasn't changed; only the test configuration has. The AI evaluator's safety verdict is entirely contingent on the parameterization of its own evaluation scope [2].

### 2.3 Medical LLM Multi-Domain Evaluation Gaps

The John Snow Labs framework tested 11 medical LLMs across 690 clinical scenarios using adversarial transformations [6]. The evaluation uncovered a critical contradiction: models demonstrating **high overall accuracy rates** simultaneously exhibited **failure in crucial clinical contexts**. An automated evaluator scoring a medical LLM as "safe" on aggregate benchmarks could mask domain-specific safety failures in diagnosis-critical scenarios. This represents a form of evaluator misalignment—the evaluation architecture's aggregation function produces a safety verdict that contradicts domain-specific safety requirements.

### 2.4 Scalable Oversight: Declining Success with Growing Capability Gaps

Research on AI debate and oversight protocols documents empirically that oversight success rates decline sharply as the capability gap between the evaluating system and the evaluated system widens [8]. In nested scalable oversight (NSO) debate experiments, an Elo gap of 400 between evaluator and evaluated systems yielded only a **51.7% success rate**—barely above chance. This means the evaluating AI's safety verdicts become effectively random when the system under evaluation is sufficiently more capable, yet these verdicts are still generated and reported with full confidence, creating **systematically unreliable safety assessments presented as definitive** [8].

## 3. Proposed Framework: Autonomous Resolution of AI Safety Contradictions

Drawing on the philosophical analysis and documented failure modes, I propose a **Formal Autonomous Resolution Framework (FARF)** that resolves contradictory AI safety evaluations without relying on human judgment. The framework has four architectural layers.

### 3.1 Layer 1: Axiomatic Safety Baselines (Reidian First Principles)

Following Thomas Reid's solution to the problem of the criterion, the framework begins by establishing **non-negotiable formal axioms** that are not derived from any AI system's evaluation. These are mathematical and logical constraints:

- **Non-contradiction axiom**: If evaluation E₁ and evaluation E₂ of the same output produce divergent safety classifications, the output is classified as unsafe pending resolution.
- **Capability-gap threshold**: If the evaluating system's capability score falls within a defined margin of the evaluated system, the evaluation is automatically flagged as epistemically suspect.
- **Monotonic safety requirement**: Safety scores must be monotonically non-increasing as the scope of adversarial testing expands (e.g., adding test categories cannot increase a safety score).

These axioms serve as the Reid-inspired first principles—accepted without derivation from the evaluating system's own reasoning.

### 3.2 Layer 2: Ensemble Divergence Detection

When multiple AI evaluators assess the same target, contradictions are detected using **formal divergence metrics**:

- **KL-Divergence Bounding**: Compute the Kullback-Leibler divergence between the safety-score distributions produced by independent evaluator instances. If KL divergence exceeds a calibrated threshold θ, the evaluation is flagged as contradictory. This leverages existing KL constraints already used in RLAIF policy updates [5].
- **Contrastive Loss Resolution**: Rather than evaluating safety as an absolute, compute the explicit mathematical difference (r_bad − r_good) between contradictory AI evaluations. Outliers in this distribution identify cases where the AI evaluator is internally conflicted [5].
- **Complementary Signal Aggregation**: Replace single-evaluator ground-truthing with statistical aggregation of complementary weak signals (e.g., "not this" signals rather than absolute verdicts). Research demonstrates that such aggregation can mathematically recover top-1 accuracy without requiring a definitive human or AI ground truth [8].

### 3.3 Layer 3: Adversarial Debate Protocol

When Layer 2 detects a contradiction, the framework initiates a **formalized adversarial debate** between evaluator instances:

- Two AI evaluators are assigned opposing burden-of-proof obligations: one must argue the output is safe, the other that it is unsafe.
- The debate proceeds for a fixed number of rounds under **doubly-efficient protocol** constraints: exponentially complex reasoning must be verifiable using only polynomial-time automated inspection [8].
- An automated judge (distinct from both evaluators, operating under a different constitutional framework) evaluates the debate transcript and produces a verdict.
- The judge's verdict is subject to a **multi-order recursive critique**: its reasoning is itself critiqued by further automated instances, systematically eliminating evaluation errors through layered self-correction [8].

### 3.4 Layer 4: Game-Theoretic Consensus Enforcement

The final layer uses **formal game-theoretic guarantees** to enforce resolution:

- The evaluation process is modeled as a **Markov Potential Game** (already formalized in oversight research [8]), where an alignment theorem guarantees that autonomous value improvements by evaluator agents cannot harm the overall safety utility function.
- If adversarial debate fails to produce consensus within a bounded number of iterations, the **PPO clipping policy** is invoked: the contradictory feedback is prevented from executing a gradient update on the safety model. The system pauses and re-evaluates against an alternative constitutional principle until mathematical consensus is reached [5].
- The **Agent Score Difference (ASD)** metric—defined as ASD = log p₊ − log p₋—provides a unified, continuous measure of alignment and truth-telling incentives across all evaluators [8]. Consensus is achieved only when ASD converges within a formal tolerance.

## 4. Philosophical Implications and Limitations

### 4.1 Does This Actually Escape Circularity?

Any honest assessment must acknowledge that this framework does not *eliminate* epistemic circularity—it **formalizes and constrains** it. The axiomatic baselines in Layer 1 are Reid-inspired assumptions, not independently derived truths. The adversarial debate protocol assumes that truth is more internally consistent than falsehood, which is itself an article of philosophical faith (one that the obfuscated arguments problem in debate literature challenges [7]). The framework's virtue is not that it escapes circularity entirely but that it makes the circularity **explicit, measurable, and bounded**.

### 4.2 The Alignment Tax

Implementing FARF imposes significant computational costs: multiple independent evaluator instances, multi-round adversarial debates, recursive critique chains, and formal divergence computations. This "alignment tax" may be substantial, particularly for real-time safety evaluation. Research documents that oversight costs are already a major concern, with scalable oversight methods specifically designed to reduce them [7, 9].

### 4.3 The Obfuscated Arguments Problem

Barnes and Christiano (2020) identified that debate protocols can fail when arguments are deliberately obfuscated—complex enough that a judge cannot distinguish valid reasoning from sophisticated sophistry [7]. In an autonomous framework without human cognitive limits as a backstop, this problem may be amplified rather than diminished, as AI evaluators may share blind spots that humans would not.

---

## 5. Conclusion

The use of AI systems to evaluate AI safety is philosophically grounded in an ancient problem—epistemic circularity—that admits no clean resolution. The documented cases of contradictory AI safety evaluations (helpfulness vs. harmlessness conflicts in RLAIF, safety vs. availability contradictions in red teaming, configuration-dependent fairness inversions, and declining oversight accuracy with capability gaps) demonstrate that this is not merely a theoretical concern but an active practical risk. The Formal Autonomous Resolution Framework proposed here—combining axiomatic baselines, ensemble divergence detection, adversarial debate, and game-theoretic consensus enforcement—offers a structured path toward resolving contradictions without human judgment. But it must be understood as a *management* of circularity, not its elimination. The philosophical lesson is clear: in the domain of AI evaluating AI, the best we can do is make our assumptions explicit, our disagreements measurable, and our resolution processes formally verifiable.

## Uncertainty & Gaps

- **Limited empirical validation**: The proposed FARF framework synthesizes elements from existing research but has not been empirically tested against real contradictory AI safety evaluations.
- **Obfuscated arguments problem unresolved**: Debate-based resolution may fail against sophisticated adversarial reasoning that exploits shared evaluator blind spots.
- **Axiomatic justification gap**: The framework's foundational axioms (Layer 1) are philosophical commitments, not provably correct principles—any framework claiming to resolve contradictions without human judgment ultimately rests on such assumptions.
- **Source limitations on specific contradictory cases**: While the documented cases are instructive, the field lacks a systematic catalog of AI-vs-AI safety evaluation disagreements with full quantitative detail. Many relevant arXiv papers and alignment forum posts were unavailable during this research due to access constraints.
