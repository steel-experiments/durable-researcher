# THE NEW FORGE: How Silicon Valley Built a Military-Industrial Complex

## A Blog Series in Five Parts

---

# PART I: THE MAFIA'S ORIGINS — From PayPal to Power

**How a fired cohort of tech prodigies became the most influential political network in modern American history.**

In 2002, eBay acquired PayPal for $1.5 billion. What looked like a standard Silicon Valley exit story became something far more consequential. The acquisition triggered a cultural collision — eBay's corporate bureaucracy clashed with PayPal's libertarian-leaning founder ethos — and by 2006, all but 12 of PayPal's original 50 employees had departed. They didn't scatter. They consolidated.

The group that Silicon Valley would later dub the **"PayPal Mafia"** went on to found or fund an extraordinary roster of companies: LinkedIn, YouTube, Yelp, SpaceX, Tesla, Facebook, Palantir, Affirm. Collectively, they are credited with reviving the consumer internet after the 2001 dot-com bust. But the PayPal Mafia's most consequential legacy wouldn't be social networks or ride-sharing apps. It would be the fusion of Silicon Valley capital, engineering talent, and American military power.

The group's ideological texture was always distinct. PayPal's early culture was shaped by Peter Thiel's contrarian libertarianism — heavily influenced by thinkers like René Girard and Leo Strauss — and a shared skepticism of institutional authority. But in the years after PayPal, this skepticism curdled into something more pointed: a belief that the state wasn't the enemy so much as an instrument that had fallen into the wrong hands and needed to be reclaimed.

**Peter Thiel**, often called the "don" of the PayPal Mafia, took the most direct path. In 2003, a year after the PayPal sale, he founded **Palantir Technologies**, bankrolling the initial $30 million himself. He recruited Alex Karp — a former Stanford Law School classmate with a PhD in neoclassical social theory — as CEO. The company's name was drawn from J.R.R. Tolkien's *seeing stones*: magical artifacts that let their users see across vast distances. The metaphor was deliberate. Thiel envisioned adapting PayPal's fraud-detection algorithms to map terrorist networks and "reduce terrorism while preserving civil liberties."

Silicon Valley venture capitalists initially balked at the expense and ambition of the platform. But the **CIA's venture capital arm, In-Q-Tel**, saw the potential. Its approximately $2 million investment proved pivotal, connecting Palantir with intelligence community users who spent two years iterating on the software in classified environments.

This was the moment — quiet, largely unreported at the time — when the new Silicon Valley military-industrial complex was born. Not with a congressional vote or a Pentagon press conference, but with a venture investment from an intelligence agency into a company founded by a freshly minted tech billionaire with a philosophical grudge against the foreign policy establishment.

Other Mafia members were building parallel infrastructure. **Elon Musk** founded SpaceX in 2002, which would eventually become one of the Pentagon's most critical launch providers. **Ken Howery** and **Roelof Botha** became partners at Founders Fund and Sequoia Capital respectively, directing rivers of capital toward defense-adjacent startups. **Reid Hoffman** took a different path, becoming one of the Democratic Party's most prominent tech donors — a divergence that would later crystallize the Mafia's political split.

By the early 2010s, Palantir's client list read like a who's-who of the national security state: CIA, FBI, NSA, DHS, and every branch of the military. Vice President Joe Biden publicly credited the company with aiding stimulus fraud detection in 2010. Palantir CEO Alex Karp boasted about the company's grip on the FBI: "They'll have no choice."

The PayPal Mafia hadn't just built companies. They had built an *infrastructure of influence* — capital networks, personal relationships, shared ideology — that would, two decades later, converge with political power in ways no one predicted.

# PART II: PALANTIR — The Seeing Stone

**How a Tolkien-named startup became the $400 billion nerve center of the surveillance state.**

Palantir Technologies is the connective tissue between the PayPal Mafia era and the modern defense-tech boom. Understanding its trajectory is essential to understanding everything that followed.

Founded in 2003 by Thiel, Karp, Nathan Gettings, Joe Lonsdale, and Stephen Cohen, Palantir developed two flagship platforms. **Palantir Gotham** served intelligence agencies, law enforcement, and counterterrorism units — integrating siloed databases to help human analysts identify patterns and connections. **Palantir Foundry** extended similar capabilities to corporate clients like Morgan Stanley and Airbus. The company's philosophy was "intelligence augmentation": human analysts in the loop, not autonomous AI making life-and-death decisions.

For years, Palantir burned cash. Its sophisticated platforms required enormous upfront investment, and government procurement cycles moved at a glacial pace. But the wars in Iraq and Afghanistan — and the explosion of intelligence data they generated — created relentless demand. By 2013, Palantir was embedded across the national security apparatus.

The company's relationship with government was always complicated. Agencies like the NSA and CIA sometimes chafed at Palantir's publicity-seeking. But the intelligence community couldn't easily dislodge the platform once analysts depended on it. Palantir had achieved what every defense contractor dreams of: *mission-critical dependence*.

Palantir went public via direct listing in September 2020. But the real financial inflection came later. After roughly twenty years of losses, Palantir reported its first positive GAAP net income — **$31 million** — in Q1 2023. By 2025, the company's market capitalization had surged past **$400 billion**, an almost absurd figure for a company with only **4,100 employees**.

This valuation wasn't driven by consumer growth or viral adoption. It was driven by the recognition that Palantir had become an *authorized provider for Mission Critical National Security Systems* — a designation that opened the door to massive, multi-billion-dollar defense contracts. In late 2024, Palantir landed a nearly **$1 billion Navy contract**.

But it was in the ideological realm that Palantir's influence proved most far-reaching. In 2025, CEO Alex Karp published **"The Technological Republic,"** a book arguing for a deep integration of Silicon Valley and defense interests. Karp contended that deterrence through technological dominance was essential for national security — a philosophy that *The New Republic* characterized as an embrace of "techno-militarism." Karp described Palantir's founding approach as a "classic German approach" influenced by the Hegelian dialectic — a revealing intellectual framing for a company building the surveillance infrastructure of the American state.

Palantir also served as a *talent pipeline* for the broader defense-tech ecosystem. Former Palantir employees went on to found or join nearly every major defense-tech startup that followed, most notably **Anduril Industries**. Joe Lonsdale, a Palantir co-founder, became one of the most prolific investors in defense technology through his firm 8VC.

Palantir wasn't just a company. It was proof of concept — that a Silicon Valley startup, backed by venture capital and led by tech-world outsiders, could penetrate the most fortified corridors of military procurement and emerge as an indispensable partner to the American security state.

# PART III: ANDURIL — The Sword Reforged

**How a VR founder, a Palantir alumnus, and a Tolkien reference built a $60 billion autonomous weapons empire.**

If Palantir was the proof of concept, **Anduril Industries** was the eruption.

The company's origin story reads like Silicon Valley mythology. In 2014, **Palmer Luckey** — the baby-faced creator of the Oculus Rift — met **Trae Stephens**, a Palantir employee and principal at Thiel's Founders Fund, on Sonoma Island. Both were frustrated by the same observation: Silicon Valley was building the future of everything *except* national defense. The Pentagon was still buying Cold War-era systems at Cold War-era prices from Cold War-era primes. Where was the disruption?

Luckey sold Oculus to Facebook for $2 billion in 2014 but was pushed out in March 2017 under controversial circumstances (partly tied to his funding of a pro-Trump meme group). He was 24 years old, politically energized, and very rich. He and Stephens recruited talent from Palantir and Oculus and formally founded Anduril in June 2017, alongside co-founders Matt Grim, Joe Chen, and Brian Schimpf. The name, like Palantir's, came from Tolkien — Andúril was the legendary sword reforged from the shards of Narsil, wielded by Aragorn. The symbolism was unmistakable: a remade weapon for a remade world.

Anduril's first products were deceptively simple: autonomous Sentry surveillance towers deployed informally on the Texas-Mexico border in 2018. But the company's ambitions scaled with terrifying speed. Its core innovation was **Lattice** — an AI-powered command-and-control software platform that could integrate data from hundreds of sensors (drones, towers, satellites, underwater vehicles) into a single real-time battlefield picture. Lattice was the operating system; the hardware was modular and disposable.

The funding trajectory tells the story:

| Round | Date | Amount | Valuation |
|-------|------|--------|-----------|
| Early raises | 2017–2018 | Seed/growth | ~$100M+ |
| Series C | Sept 2019 | $120M | $1B+ |
| Series D | June 2021 | $450M | $4.6B |
| Series F | Aug 2024 | $1.5B | $14B |
| Series G | June 2025 | $2.5B | $30.5B |
| Series H | March 2026 | $4B | **$60B** |

That final valuation — **$60 billion** — exceeded the combined market capitalization of Ford Motor Company and Alaska Airlines. For a company founded nine years earlier by a guy in a Hawaiian shirt.

Anduril's product line expanded to encompass autonomous drone swarms (Ghost), tube-launched UAVs (Altius), counter-drone interceptors (Anvil), cruise missiles, underwater vehicles, the **YFQ-44A robotic fighter drone** (which first flew in 2025 as part of the Air Force's Collaborative Combat Aircraft program), and autonomous warships built at a revamped shipyard on Seattle's Lake Washington Ship Canal.

The company acquired its way into new capabilities: drone maker Area-I (2021), underwater vehicle company Dive Technologies (2022), solid rocket fuel manufacturer Adranos (2023), and space intelligence firm ExoAnalytic Solutions (2026). In January 2025, Anduril announced **"Arsenal-1"** — a massive, hyperscale manufacturing facility outside Columbus, Ohio, designed to mass-produce autonomous weapons at scale.

Anduril's rise coincided with a geopolitical transformation. The war in Ukraine demonstrated that cheap, autonomous drones could destroy multi-million-dollar weapons systems. Russia's electronic warfare capabilities exposed vulnerabilities in Anduril's own Ghost drones — a humbling reminder that Silicon Valley disruption has limits. But the overall trajectory was unmistakable: warfare was becoming software-defined, and Anduril was positioning itself as the platform company.

Crucially, Anduril became a key supplier to Ukraine, with Palmer Luckey claiming in March 2025 that Altius drones had "taken out hundreds of millions of dollars worth of Russian targets." The company partnered with OpenAI to counter drone threats and formed a production alliance with the UAE's Edge Group to develop autonomous weapons for international markets.

By 2025, Luckey — described by *The New York Times* as the "It Guy of the booming defense-technology industry" — had become a "close confidante" within the Trump administration. The Hawaiian shirts and sandals were a studied rejection of Beltway norms. But the influence was very real.

# PART IV: THE NEW WAVE — Mach Industries and the Defense-Tech Boom

**How a 19-year-old MIT dropout came to represent the next generation of Silicon Valley's weapons builders.**

By 2023, the defense-tech ecosystem had become self-reinforcing. Palantir had proven the model. Anduril had scaled it. Now a new generation of founders — raised on the mythology of both companies — entered the fray.

The most striking example is **Mach Industries**, founded by **Ethan Thornton**, who bootstrapped the company from his high school wood and metal workshop before dropping out of MIT at 19 to build it full-time. Thornton represents something genuinely new: a founder whose formative influences include not just Steve Jobs and Elon Musk, but Palmer Luckey and the Anduril playbook. He didn't pivot to defense; he *started* there.

In 2023, Mach raised a **$5.7 million seed round** led by Sequoia Capital partners Stephanie Zhan and Shaun Maguire. Four months later, the company closed a **$79 million Series A** at a **$335 million valuation**, led by Bedrock Capital with participation from DCVC, Sequoia, Champion Hill Ventures, and the Thiel-backed **1517 Fund** — a fellowship program specifically designed to support young founders who skip or drop out of college. The Thiel connection, again, was unmistakable.

By 2025, Mach had landed a **U.S. Army contract** — awarded through the Army Applications Laboratory in Q3 2024 — to develop a vertical-takeoff precision cruise missile called "Strategic Strike." The company was producing two core products: **Glide**, a bomb deployed from the edge of space designed to be nearly impossible to shoot down, and **Viper**, a jet-powered vertical-takeoff UAV that Mach claimed was **300 times cheaper** to manufacture than traditional UAVs.

To manufacture these systems at scale, Mach began building **"Forge 1"** — a 115,000-square-foot weapons factory at its Huntington Beach, California headquarters. Thornton described the "decentralized" manufacturing model as essential to ensuring the U.S. had a "survival defense industrial base" that could scale rapidly without relying on specialty tooling or centralized locations. Once operational, Forge 1 was designed to produce **1,000 Vipers and 3,000 Glides per month**.

In June 2025, Mach confirmed a **$100 million raise** led by Khosla Ventures and Bedrock Capital — a staggering trajectory for a company whose founder couldn't legally drink.

Mach's rise was part of a broader paradigm shift in defense procurement. The old model — billion-dollar, decade-long contracts with legacy primes like Lockheed Martin and Raytheon — was being challenged by startups offering cheaper, high-volume, iteratively developed systems. Venture firms like Sequoia, Bedrock, Founders Fund, and Khosla were building dedicated defense-tech portfolios, effectively creating a *shadow procurement system* that paralleled — and increasingly competed with — the traditional Pentagon acquisition process.

# PART V: CONVERGENCE — The 2025 Trump Administration and the Techno-Militarist State

**How the PayPal Mafia completed its journey from libertarian outsiders to the inner circle of American power.**

The 2024 presidential election didn't just return Donald Trump to the White House. It formalized an alliance between Silicon Valley's defense-tech builders and the American national security state that had been building, mostly in the shadows, for over two decades.

*The Economist* declared that the PayPal Mafia would "take over America's government." The headline wasn't hyperbolic.

**Elon Musk**, who donated over **$250 million** to Trump's 2024 campaign, was appointed as a senior advisor and head of the newly created **Department of Government Efficiency (DOGE)** — a vehicle for slashing federal bureaucracy that, inevitably, also touched defense procurement and intelligence systems. Musk's SpaceX was already one of the Pentagon's most critical launch providers. Now its founder sat inside the building.

**David O. Sacks**, PayPal's former COO, was named the **White House AI and crypto czar** in December 2024 — a position that gave him influence over the very regulatory frameworks governing defense-tech companies.

**Peter Thiel** didn't take a formal role. He didn't need to. His **political protégé, JD Vance**, was elected Vice President. His defense company, Palantir, was collaborating with Musk's DOGE on a massive **IRS data integration project** in 2025. And Palantir's contracts with the administration were staggering: **$113 million** in DHS and Pentagon contracts, plus a **$795 million Department of Defense contract**, with major deals under deliberation with the Social Security Administration and IRS.

**Ken Howery**, PayPal's former CFO, served as U.S. ambassador to Denmark during the second Trump administration — a strategically significant post given Greenland's importance to Arctic military strategy and rare earth mineral access.

The policy architecture reinforced the corporate one. Trump pushed the Pentagon to adopt "cutting-edge technologies to counter China," a directive that funneled resources toward precisely the companies the PayPal Mafia network had built. The **"Golden Dome" missile defense program** — a next-generation shield concept — became a target for Anduril's expanding space division. An **April 2025 executive order** to "restore America's maritime dominance" directly supported Anduril's autonomous warship programs on the Seattle ship canal.

Palmer Luckey, Anduril's founder, became a "close confidante and respected force within the Trump administration," as *GeekWire* reported. The man who had been fired from Facebook for funding right-wing meme operations was now advising the president on weapons systems.

And Alex Karp, Palantir's CEO, published *The Technological Republic* — a manifesto for what he openly called a new paradigm: Silicon Valley and the defense establishment fused into a single entity, driven by the conviction that American technological supremacy was the only viable foundation for global security. The New Republic called it "techno-militarism." The label stuck.

## THE ARC: A Storyline in Retrospect

Looking back over two decades, the trajectory is remarkably coherent:

**Phase 1 (2002–2008): The Scatter.** The PayPal Mafia disperses across Silicon Valley, founding consumer internet companies and accumulating capital. Thiel, uniquely, turns toward government and defense with Palantir.

**Phase 2 (2008–2016): The Infrastructure.** Palantir embeds itself in the intelligence community. SpaceX becomes a critical military launch provider. Founders Fund and other Mafia-linked venture firms begin directing capital toward defense-adjacent startups. The ideological foundations — libertarian skepticism of the state curdling into a desire to *control* the state — harden.

**Phase 3 (2017–2022): The Eruption.** Anduril's founding in 2017 marks the moment defense tech becomes a legitimate Silicon Valley category, not a weird niche. Luckey's fame and wealth attract a new generation of founders. Venture capital floods in. The wars in Ukraine and the Middle East demonstrate the combat relevance of autonomous systems.

**Phase 4 (2023–2025): The Wave.** A second generation of defense-tech startups — Mach Industries, and dozens of others — emerges, normalizing what was once radical. Sequoia, Khosla, Andreessen Horowitz build dedicated defense practices. The manufacturing base begins to shift: Arsenal-1 in Ohio, Forge-1 in Huntington Beach.

**Phase 5 (2025–): The Convergence.** The PayPal Mafia enters government. Defense-tech companies become too large and too embedded to dislodge. The line between Silicon Valley and the Pentagon dissolves. A new military-industrial complex — younger, richer, more ideologically coherent, and backed by venture capital rather than cost-plus contracts — consolidates its position.

## CODA: What's Different This Time?

Eisenhower warned about the military-industrial complex in 1961. What would he make of this one?

The differences are structural. The old complex was built on **cost-plus contracts** — guaranteed profit margins that incentivized bloat and delay. The new complex is built on **venture capital** — risk capital that demands rapid iteration, software-style product cycles, and winner-take-all market dynamics. The old complex was dominated by slow-moving primes in Virginia and Connecticut. The new one is run by billionaires in Hawaiian shirts who tweet through geopolitical crises.

The similarities are also structural. Concentration of power. Revolving doors between industry and government. The normalization of perpetual warfare as a business model. And the seductive belief — shared by the PayPal Mafia, by Anduril's founders, by Mach Industries' teenage CEO — that technology can solve problems that are ultimately political.

The new Silicon Valley military-industrial complex isn't coming. It's here. The only question is whether anyone will build the institutions to govern it before it becomes as permanent as the last one.
