# From Pigeonholes to Ramsey's Theorem: A Progressive Olympiad Problem Sequence

## Overview

What follows is a curated sequence of olympiad-style problems that build from elementary combinatorics toward the full statement and proof of **Ramsey's Theorem**. Each problem introduces a key idea that the next one relies on. At every stage, I provide intuition for *how a solver might discover the solution*, not just the solution itself.

The mathematical arc is: **Pigeonhole Principle → Graph-theoretic Pigeonhole → Monochromatic structures → Full Ramsey's Theorem**, with beautiful detours through geometry and number theory along the way.

---

## Problem 1: The Pigeonhole Principle — Divisibility

> **Problem.** Prove that among any $n+1$ positive integers, there exist two whose difference is divisible by $n$.

### Intuition

The first question to ask: *"What does it mean for $a - b$ to be divisible by $n$?"* It means $a$ and $b$ leave the **same remainder** when divided by $n$. There are only $n$ possible remainders: $0, 1, 2, \ldots, n-1$. These are your "boxes." The $n+1$ integers are your "pigeons." By the Pigeonhole Principle, two pigeons must share a box — i.e., two integers share a remainder — and their difference is divisible by $n$.

> **Key takeaway:** Finite categories force collisions. This is the engine that drives everything that follows. [Source: AwesomeMath [1]]

## Problem 2: Geometric Pigeonhole — Points in a Square

> **Problem.** Show that among any five points placed inside a unit square, at least two must be within distance $\frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$ of each other.

### Intuition

The Pigeonhole Principle requires *boxes*. How do you create boxes in a continuous space like a square? **Tile it.** Divide the unit square into four equal sub-squares of side $\frac{1}{2}$. Five points into four boxes means at least one sub-square contains two points. The maximum distance within a $\frac{1}{2} \times \frac{1}{2}$ square is its diagonal: $\sqrt{(1/2)^2 + (1/2)^2} = \frac{\sqrt{2}}{2}$.

> **Key takeaway:** The Pigeonhole Principle applies to geometric settings — you just need to create the right partition. This bridge from discrete to spatial thinking prepares us for the geometric Ramsey-type problems ahead. [Source: AwesomeMath [1]]

## Problem 3: Convex Quadrilaterals — The "Happy Ending" Problem Begins

> **Problem (Esther Klein, 1933).** Prove that among any five points in the plane in general position (no three collinear), four of them form the vertices of a convex quadrilateral.

### Intuition

The natural tool for analyzing "outermost" points is the **convex hull**. Consider the cases:

- **Case 1: The convex hull has ≥ 4 points.** Then the hull vertices themselves form a convex quadrilateral. Done.
- **Case 2: The convex hull is a triangle** (3 vertices). Two points $P, Q$ lie strictly inside. Draw the line through $P$ and $Q$; it exits the triangle through two sides. Pick one vertex from each of the two sides it crosses, together with $P$ and $Q$. These four points form a convex quadrilateral.

> **Key takeaway:** This was the problem that launched Ramsey theory. Klein proved $f(4) = 5$ in 1933; Erdős and Szekeres generalized it, and the resulting paper essentially *created* the field of Ramsey theory. Known values: $f(3) = 3$, $f(4) = 5$, $f(5) = 9$, $f(6) = 17$. [Source: Wikipedia [2]]

## Problem 4: Monotone Subsequences — The Erdős–Szekeres Theorem

> **Problem.** Prove that any sequence of $(r-1)(s-1) + 1$ distinct real numbers contains a monotonically increasing subsequence of length $r$ or a monotonically decreasing subsequence of length $s$.

### Intuition

Assign to each element a **label** $(x_i, y_i)$: let $x_i$ be the length of the longest increasing subsequence ending at position $i$, and $y_i$ the length of the longest decreasing subsequence ending at position $i$.

Now observe: if $a_i < a_j$ and $i < j$, then the longest increasing subsequence ending at $j$ is *at least* one more than that ending at $i$. So labels are **partially ordered** — two elements can share the same label only under very restrictive conditions.

If no increasing subsequence of length $r$ exists, then $x_i \leq r-1$ for all $i$. If no decreasing subsequence of length $s$ exists, then $y_i \leq s-1$. So the labels lie in a grid of size $(r-1) \times (s-1)$, giving at most $(r-1)(s-1)$ distinct labels. But we have $(r-1)(s-1) + 1$ elements — so by Pigeonhole, two share the same label, which forces a contradiction.

> **Key takeaway:** The Pigeonhole Principle, applied to *pairs of coordinates* rather than single values, unlocks structural information about order. This theorem is a direct consequence of **Dilworth's theorem** and is deeply connected to Ramsey theory — the original Erdős–Szekeres paper proved it using the same Ramsey-type argument used for convex polygons. [Source: AwesomeMath [1], Grozev [3]]

## Problem 5: The Party Problem — Friends and Strangers ($R(3,3) = 6$)

> **Problem.** Prove that in any group of six people, there are either three pairwise mutual friends or three pairwise mutual strangers. Show that this fails for five people.

### Intuition

Model the situation as a **graph**: six vertices (people), each pair connected by a **red** edge (friends) or **blue** edge (strangers). This is a 2-coloring of the complete graph $K_6$.

**The proof (upper bound):** Pick any person $A$. She has 5 edges to the others. By the Pigeonhole Principle, at least $\lceil 5/2 \rceil = 3$ of these edges share the same color — say red (friends). Call those three people $B, C, D$.

Now look at the triangle $BCD$:
- If **any** edge among them is red (say $BC$), then $\{A, B, C\}$ form a red triangle — three mutual friends.
- If **no** edge among them is red, then all three edges $BC, BD, CD$ are blue — three mutual strangers.

Either way, we're done.

**The lower bound (five people suffice to avoid it):** Arrange five people in a circle. Color edges connecting neighbors around the pentagon red, and all "diagonal" edges blue. This gives a 2-coloring of $K_5$ with **no** monochromatic triangle. (Check: each vertex has exactly 2 red and 2 blue edges, and no three vertices are mutually connected by the same color.)

> **Key takeaway:** This is the **gateway to Ramsey theory on graphs**. The core technique — fix a vertex, use Pigeonhole on its edges, then analyze the induced subgraph — is exactly the technique used in the proof of the full Ramsey's theorem. [Sources: AoPS [4], Medium [5], Cut-the-Knot [6]]

## Problem 6: Schur's Theorem — Arithmetic Ramsey

> **Problem.** Prove that if the integers $\{1, 2, \ldots, 9\}$ are colored with two colors, there exist monochromatic $x, y, z$ with $x + y = z$.

### Intuition

First, warm up with $\{1, 2, 3, 4, 5\}$: Try to avoid a monochromatic solution to $x + y = z$. Color 1 **Red**. Then $1 + 1 = 2$ forces 2 to be **Blue**. Then $2 + 2 = 4$ forces 4 to be **Red**. Then $1 + 3 = 4$ forces 3 to be **Blue**. Now 5 must be colored. If Red: $1 + 4 = 5$ ✓. If Blue: $2 + 3 = 5$ ✓. No escape!

For the general case with $\{1, \ldots, 9\}$, the key insight is to **frame integers as distances**:

1. Place 9 vertices $v_1, v_2, \ldots, v_9$ in a row.
2. Color the edge $v_iv_j$ with the color of the integer $|i - j|$.
3. This produces a 2-colored complete graph $K_9$.

By Problem 5's reasoning (since $R(3,3) = 6 < 9$), there exists a monochromatic triangle on vertices $v_a < v_b < v_c$. Since all three edges share the same color, the three integers $x = b - a$, $y = c - b$, and $z = c - a$ are all the same color. And $x + y = (b-a) + (c-b) = c - a = z$.

> **Key takeaway:** Graph Ramsey theory directly implies arithmetic Ramsey results. The translation "integers → edge-lengths in a path graph" is a powerful technique. This is **Schur's theorem** (1916), one of the earliest Ramsey-type results. The known Schur numbers are $S(1) = 2, S(2) = 5, S(3) = 14, S(4) = 45, S(5) = 161$ (the last proved in 2017 using 2 petabytes of computing!). [Source: Wikipedia [7]]

## Problem 7: Sharpening the Bound — Herbert Taylor's Theorem

> **Problem.** Let $R_m$ denote the minimum $N$ such that any $m$-coloring of the edges of $K_N$ contains a monochromatic triangle. Show that $K_{R_m}$ with a **single edge removed** can be $m$-colored without a monochromatic triangle.

### Intuition

By definition, $K_{R_m - 1}$ admits an $m$-coloring with no monochromatic triangle. Use a **"cloning" construction**: pick any vertex $P$ in this coloring and add a new vertex $P'$ that connects to all other vertices using the *exact same colors* as $P$ does. The edge $PP'$ is left uncolored.

Why does this work? Any triangle involving $P'$ would "mirror" a triangle involving $P$ (replace $P'$ with $P$ in the triangle, and the colors match). Since $P$'s coloring has no monochromatic triangles, neither does $P'$'s. The resulting graph is $K_{R_m}$ missing exactly one edge, with no monochromatic triangle.

> **Key takeaway:** Ramsey numbers are *sharp* — removing a single edge drops you below the threshold. This cloning technique is a powerful constructive tool. [Source: Cut-the-Knot [6], Golomb Mathematics Magazine 2006]

## Problem 8: The Inductive Step — Proving $R(3,4) \leq 9$

> **Problem.** Prove that any 2-coloring (red/blue) of the edges of $K_9$ contains either a red triangle ($K_3$) or a blue $K_4$.

### Intuition

This is where the **inductive machinery** of Ramsey theory kicks in. We already know:
- $R(3,3) = 6$ (from Problem 5)
- $R(2,4) = 4$ (trivially: if 4 vertices all have blue edges to any fixed vertex, either two of those blue edges share a blue connection or we get a red edge among them)

**The argument:** Fix a vertex $v$ in $K_9$. It has 8 neighbors. Partition them:
- $S$ = vertices connected to $v$ by red edges
- $T$ = vertices connected to $v$ by blue edges

Since $|S| + |T| = 8$, either $|S| \geq R(2,4) = 4$ or $|T| \geq R(3,3) = 6$ (because if both were smaller, we'd have $|S| \leq 3$ and $|T| \leq 5$, giving $|S|+|T| \leq 8$, which is tight — we need to verify $R(3,3) + R(2,4) = 6 + 4 = 10 > 9$, so actually $|S|+|T| = 8 < 10$, and by pigeonhole one side exceeds its threshold).

More precisely, the bound comes from $R(s,t) \leq R(s-1,t) + R(s,t-1)$.

- If $|S| \geq R(2,4) = 4$: Among those vertices, either there's a red $K_2$ (just a single red edge, giving a red triangle with $v$) or a blue $K_4$.
- If $|T| \geq R(3,3) = 6$: Among those vertices, either there's a red $K_3$ or a blue $K_3$ (which together with $v$ gives a blue $K_4$).

> **Key takeaway:** The general recurrence $R(s,t) \leq R(s-1,t) + R(s,t-1)$ is proved by exactly this argument: fix a vertex, split neighbors by color, apply induction. This is the **heart of Ramsey's theorem**. [Source: AoPS [4]]

## Problem 9: Ramsey's Theorem — The Full Statement and Proof

> **Theorem (Ramsey, 1930).** For any positive integers $s$ and $t$, there exists a least positive integer $R(s,t)$ such that any red/blue coloring of the edges of $K_n$ with $n \geq R(s,t)$ contains a red $K_s$ or a blue $K_t$.

### Proof by Double Induction

**Base cases:** $R(1, t) = 1$ for all $t$ (a single vertex is trivially a $K_1$ of any color), and $R(s, 1) = 1$ by symmetry. Also $R(2, t) = t$ (either all edges from a vertex are blue, giving a blue star, or at least one is red, giving a red $K_2$).

**Inductive step:** Assume $R(s-1, t)$ and $R(s, t-1)$ exist. Define:
$$R(s,t) \leq R(s-1, t) + R(s, t-1)$$

Consider any 2-coloring of $K_n$ where $n = R(s-1, t) + R(s, t-1)$. Fix a vertex $v$. Partition the remaining vertices:
- $S$ = vertices with a **red** edge to $v$
- $T$ = vertices with a **blue** edge to $v$

Since $|S| + |T| = n - 1 = R(s-1,t) + R(s,t-1) - 1$, we must have either $|S| \geq R(s-1, t)$ or $|T| \geq R(s, t-1)$.

- **If $|S| \geq R(s-1, t)$:** By induction, the coloring restricted to $S$ contains either a red $K_{s-1}$ (which together with $v$ forms a red $K_s$) or a blue $K_t$.
- **If $|T| \geq R(s, t-1)$:** By induction, the coloring restricted to $T$ contains either a red $K_s$ or a blue $K_{t-1}$ (which together with $v$ forms a blue $K_t$).

In all cases, we find a red $K_s$ or a blue $K_t$. $\blacksquare$

### Intuition for the Big Picture

The proof is a **recursive decomposition**: at each step, a single vertex acts as a "classifier," splitting the problem into two smaller subproblems by the Pigeonhole Principle. The structure is exactly the same as Problem 5 (one vertex, pigeonhole on its edges, recurse) — just generalized. Every problem in this sequence was a special case or warm-up for this argument.

> Known Ramsey numbers: $R(3,3) = 6$, $R(3,4) = 9$, $R(3,5) = 14$, $R(3,6) = 18$, $R(3,7) = 23$, $R(3,9) = 36$, $R(4,4) = 18$, $R(4,5) = 25$. The exact value of $R(5,5)$ is unknown (known to be between 43 and 48)! Erdős famously said: *"Imagine an alien force demands we compute $R(5,5)$ or they destroy Earth. We should gather all our computers and mathematicians. If they demand $R(6,6)$, we should launch a preemptive attack."* [Source: AoPS [4]]
