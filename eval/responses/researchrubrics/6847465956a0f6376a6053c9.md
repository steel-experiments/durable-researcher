# Building a Custom Embedded Processor for a Smart Thermostat: From Silicon Architecture to Open-Source Testing

*A practical guide to designing, coding, and verifying your own thermostat processor using entirely free, open-source tools on Linux.*

---

## Why Build Your Own Processor?

Off-the-shelf microcontrollers are cheap and abundant. So why would you design a custom embedded processor for something as seemingly simple as a thermostat? The answer lies in control — control over power consumption, control over exactly which peripherals live on your silicon, and control over your supply chain. A thermostat is also the perfect training ground: the problem is bounded (read temperature, compare to setpoint, toggle a relay), yet it exercises every fundamental skill in embedded processor design — from ISA selection to real-time firmware to hardware-software co-verification.

This post walks through every decision you'll face, with a fully open-source workflow that runs on Linux.

---

## RISC vs. CISC: The Architecture Decision

The first fork in the road is your processor's instruction set architecture (ISA). The two dominant philosophies are **RISC** (Reduced Instruction Set Computer) and **CISC** (Complex Instruction Set Computer). For a thermostat — and frankly for nearly all modern embedded systems — RISC is the clear winner. Here's why.

### RISC: Small Instructions, Big Advantages

RISC architectures (ARM, RISC-V, AVR, MIPS) use a small, highly optimized set of simple instructions that generally execute in a single clock cycle. This yields three critical benefits for embedded designs:

- **Predictable timing.** Single-cycle instructions mean deterministic execution — essential when you're reading sensors at precise intervals and driving real-time control loops.
- **Lower power consumption.** Simpler decode logic draws less current. For battery-powered or always-on thermostat devices, this is paramount.
- **Smaller silicon footprint.** Fewer transistors mean your design fits on smaller, cheaper FPGAs.

Benchmarks from embedded system analyses show RISC architectures achieving up to 1,000 MIPS and 500 MFLOPS, compared to CISC's 800 MIPS and 400 MFLOPS [[1]](#sources). RISC also demonstrates superior low-power state transitions under standards like ACPI and IEEE 1801 (Unified Power Format) [[2]](#sources).

### CISC: When Complexity Pays Off

CISC architectures (x86, older Motorola 68000) use complex, multi-cycle instructions that do more work per instruction. Their primary advantage is **code density** — a CISC instruction might accomplish what takes three or four RISC instructions, meaning you need less program memory. However, CISC designs suffer from higher power dissipation due to complex decoding mechanisms, making them poorly suited for the constrained form factors typical of thermostat hardware [[2]](#sources).

### The Modern Reality: RISC-V

For a new custom design, the open-standard **RISC-V ISA** is the most compelling choice. It offers the power efficiency of RISC without the licensing constraints of proprietary architectures like ARM. RISC-V is modular — you implement only the extensions you need (base integer, compressed instructions, multiplication, etc.) — and it has massive open-source backing, with pre-verified IP blocks available out of the box [[3]](#sources).

## Choosing an Open-Source RISC-V Core

You don't need to design a CPU from scratch. Several high-quality, open-source RISC-V cores are ready for FPGA implementation.

### PicoRV32: Size-Optimized and Battle-Tested

**PicoRV32** (by YosysHQ) is a compact RISC-V core implementing the RV32IMC instruction set. It's an ideal starting point for a thermostat processor:

| Feature | Specification |
|---|---|
| LUT Usage (Xilinx 7-Series) | 750–2,000 LUTs |
| Max Clock Frequency | 250–450 MHz |
| Cycles Per Instruction | ~4–5.232 |
| Performance | 0.516 DMIPS/MHz (with fast mul/div) |
| License | ISC (permissive) |

PicoRV32 supports configurable ISA subsets (RV32E, RV32I, RV32IC, RV32IM, RV32IMC), multiple memory interfaces (native valid-ready, AXI4-Lite, Wishbone), and includes an optional interrupt controller with 32 IRQ inputs — essential for reacting to sensor thresholds [[5]](#sources).

### VexRiscv: Highly Configurable with a Full SoC

**VexRiscv** (written in SpinalHDL) is more flexible but still FPGA-friendly. Instead of a fixed design, you generate a custom SoC tailored to your thermostat's requirements. For most thermostat projects, the **Murax SoC** configuration is ideal:

| Configuration | Clock Speed | DMIPS/MHz | Logic Cells (iCE40) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Murax (interlocked) | 51 MHz | 0.45 | 2,422 LC |
| MuraxFast (bypassed) | 66 MHz | 0.65 | 2,799 LC |

Murax integrates an APB bus, 8 kB of on-chip RAM, 32 GPIO pins, timers, and a UART — everything needed to read sensors and drive HVAC relays, fitting on a low-cost Lattice iCE40 FPGA using the fully open-source IceStorm toolchain [[6]](#sources).

## The Open-Source Development Toolkit

Here's the beautiful part: you can design, simulate, synthesize, and test your entire processor without spending a dollar on software. Everything runs on Linux.

### HDL Simulation: Verilator and Icarus Verilog

**Verilator** is an open-source Verilog/SystemVerilog simulator (backed by the CHIPS Alliance and the Linux Foundation) that doesn't interpret HDL — it compiles it into highly optimized, multithreaded C++ code. The performance is staggering: roughly 100× faster than interpreted simulators like Icarus Verilog, and with multithreading, a total of 200–1,000× speedup over interpreted flows. Verilator consistently meets or beats commercial simulators from Cadence, Mentor, and Synopsys [[4]](#sources).

**Icarus Verilog** is a simpler, interpreted simulator that's excellent for quick testbench iteration. PicoRV32 ships with testbenches (`testbench.v`, `testbench_ez.v`) that run directly under Icarus [[5]](#sources).

### Synthesis and FPGA Implementation: Yosys + nextpnr

**Yosys** is the cornerstone of open-source FPGA synthesis. Licensed under the permissive ISC license, it processes synthesizable Verilog-2005 designs and maps them to:

- **Xilinx 7-Series** FPGAs
- **Lattice iCE40** and **ECP5** FPGAs
- **ASIC** standard cell libraries (Liberty format)

It also includes built-in formal verification for property checking and equivalence verification via SymbiYosys (sby) [[3]](#sources).

**nextpnr** handles place-and-route after Yosys synthesis, completing the fully open-source FPGA flow for Lattice devices.

A minimal Yosys synthesis script looks like:

```verilog
read_verilog my_thermostat_cpu.v
synth -top mytop
dfflibmap -liberty mycells.lib
abc -liberty mycells.lib
clean
write_verilog synth.v
```

### OpenCores: Community IP

**OpenCores** has been the reference community for free and open-source gateware IP cores since 1999. It hosts processor cores, peripheral controllers (UART, SPI, I²C, PWM), and complete SoC designs that you can integrate directly into your thermostat processor [[4]](#sources).

## Interfacing with the Physical World

A thermostat processor is worthless without sensors and actuators. Here's how your custom CPU talks to the real world.

### Temperature Sensing: ADC vs. Digital Protocols

You have two paths for reading temperature:

**Analog sensors** (thermistors, LM35) require your processor's internal ADC. These are simple but susceptible to electrical noise — a real concern when your thermostat shares a board with relay-driven HVAC loads.

**Digital sensors** are far more robust. The **SHT31** (temperature + humidity) uses I²C, requiring just two wires: SDA (Serial Data) and SCL (Serial Clock). Your processor acts as the I²C master, querying the sensor's address for data. For higher temperature ranges, a **MAX6675** with a K-type thermocouple communicates via SPI [[7]](#sources), [[8]](#sources).

### Controlling the HVAC: GPIOs and Relays

Your processor's low-voltage GPIO pins (typically 3.3V) drive **relay modules** that act as electrically isolated switches for the 24V AC HVAC control lines:

- **Heating:** Connect R (power) to W (heat)
- **Cooling:** Connect R to Y (compressor)
- **Fan:** Connect R to G (fan)

The GPIO peripherals in your SoC (like Murax's 32 GPIO pins) map directly to these relay control lines [[7]](#sources), [[8]](#sources).

## Firmware: The Brain of Your Thermostat

### Bare-Metal vs. RTOS

You have two fundamental firmware architectures to choose from:

**Bare-metal** firmware runs a single `while(1)` loop with interrupt service routines (ISRs). It offers complete control and the lowest possible latency — ideal for simple thermostats. The entire control logic might be under 500 lines of C.

**RTOS-based** firmware (using FreeRTOS or Zephyr) separates logic into parallel tasks. A typical thermostat might have:

- `sensor_reader_task`: Samples temperature every 5 seconds
- `thermostat_control_task`: Executes the PID loop every 10 seconds
- `network_task`: Handles MQTT communication for remote monitoring

On a dual-core processor, you can pin the Wi-Fi/MQTT stack to Core 0 and the real-time PID loop to Core 1, preventing network jitter from affecting temperature control [[7]](#sources).

### PID Control: Beyond Simple On/Off

A basic "bang-bang" thermostat (turn heater on when cold, off when hot) creates uncomfortable temperature swings and premature equipment wear. A **PID (Proportional-Integral-Derivative)** controller provides smooth, precise regulation:

```
Output = (Kp × P) + (Ki × I) + (Kd × D)
```

- **Proportional (P):** Responds to current error (setpoint − actual temperature). Drives the output proportionally.
- **Integral (I):** Accumulates past error to eliminate steady-state offsets (e.g., a persistent cold draft).
- **Derivative (D):** Predicts future error from the rate of change, acting as a brake to prevent overshoot.

Real-world tuning data from experimental thermostat systems shows how different methods trade off settling time versus overshoot [[8]](#sources):

| Tuning Method | Kp | Ki | Kd | Settling Time | Overshoot |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Skogestad's | 2.0 | 6.92 | 0.082 | 47 s | 20.93% |
| Relay Tuning | 2.0 | 0.644 | 1.548 | 143 s | 1.74% |
| Ziegler-Nichols | 1.0 | 1.5625 | 0.016 | 47 s | 1.74% |

### Building the Firmware Toolchain on Linux

To compile firmware for your RISC-V processor, build the **riscv-gnu-toolchain** on Linux. This gives you the C/C++ compiler (`riscv64-unknown-elf-gcc`), assembler, linker, and newlib C library. Ubuntu 20.04+ even includes pre-packaged RV32 tools. For PicoRV32 specifically, you can build a pure RV32I-optimized toolchain from a specific git revision [[5]](#sources).

Your firmware workflow:

1. **Write** thermostat logic in C, using custom IRQ handling for sensor interrupts.
2. **Compile** with the RISC-V cross-compiler.
3. **Simulate** the processor running your firmware in Verilator or Icarus Verilog.
4. **Debug** interactively using OpenOCD (JTAG-over-TCP bridge to your Verilator simulation) and GDB to step through C code in real time [[6]](#sources).
5. **Synthesize** the design with Yosys, place-and-route with nextpnr, and flash to your FPGA.

## Putting It All Together: The Complete Workflow

Here's your end-to-end open-source development flow on Linux:

```
┌─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┐
│  HARDWARE DESIGN (Verilog/SpinalHDL)                    │
│  ├── Select or generate RISC-V core (PicoRV32/VexRiscv)│
│  ├── Add peripherals (I²C master, UART, GPIO, timers)  │
│  └── Wire up sensor interfaces and relay drivers        │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  SIMULATION & VERIFICATION                              │
│  ├── Verilator: Compile HDL → C++ simulation           │
│  ├── Icarus Verilog: Quick testbench iteration          │
│  ├── Yosys/SymbiYosys: Formal property checking        │
│  └── OpenOCD + GDB: Interactive firmware debugging      │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  FIRMWARE DEVELOPMENT                                   │
│  ├── riscv-gnu-toolchain: Cross-compile C/assembly      │
│  ├── Implement PID control loop                         │
│  ├── Configure ISRs for sensor reading                  │
│  └── Optional: FreeRTOS/Zephyr for multitasking         │
├─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┤
│  FPGA DEPLOYMENT                                        │
│  ├── Yosys: Synthesize Verilog → gate-level netlist     │
│  ├── nextpnr: Place-and-route for target FPGA          │
│  └── Flash bitstream to Lattice iCE40/ECP5             │
└─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────┘
```

## Key Takeaways

1. **RISC (specifically RISC-V) is the right choice** for a thermostat processor — lower power, predictable timing, modular design, and zero licensing costs.
2. **You don't need proprietary tools.** Yosys, Verilator, nextpnr, and the RISC-V GNU toolchain form a complete, professional-grade development environment on Linux.
3. **Start with an existing core.** PicoRV32 (750 LUTs) or VexRiscv's Murax SoC (2,422 LC) give you a proven CPU with peripherals, letting you focus on your thermostat-specific logic.
4. **Use PID control, not bang-bang.** The difference in comfort and equipment longevity is dramatic, and the implementation is straightforward in C.
5. **Simulate before you fabricate.** Verilator's 200–1,000× speed advantage over interpreted simulators means you can run extensive firmware tests entirely in software before committing to hardware.
6. **Think about Dynamic Voltage and Frequency Scaling (DVFS).** Even on an FPGA, clock gating and power management states matter for an always-on device [[1]](#sources), [[2]](#sources).

The barrier to designing your own embedded processor has never been lower. The tools are free, the cores are proven, and a thermostat provides just enough complexity to make the project genuinely educational without being overwhelming. Fire up your Linux machine, install Yosys and Verilator, and start building.
