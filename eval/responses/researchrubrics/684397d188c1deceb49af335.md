# Probably Approximately Correct (PAC) Learning: A Complete Beginner's Guide

## What Is Machine Learning, Really?

Imagine you're trying to teach a child to recognize dogs. You show them hundreds of pictures—some with dogs, some without. After a while, the child can reliably point out dogs in photos they've never seen before. **Machine learning** works on the same principle: a computer studies a batch of examples (the *training data*) and then tries to make correct predictions on new, never-before-seen examples.

But here's the nagging question that keeps researchers up at night: **How do we know the computer has studied *enough* examples?** Could it have just gotten lucky with the training data? How many pictures of dogs do we need to show before we can trust its judgment?

In 1984, computer scientist **Leslie Valiant** provided a rigorous mathematical answer to this question. His framework is called **Probably Approximately Correct (PAC) Learning**—and despite the seemingly contradictory name, it is one of the most elegant ideas in all of computer science [1][2][3].

---

## The Big Idea, In Plain English

Think of studying for a driving test using a practice exam. PAC learning makes a simple promise:

> If you practice with **enough** questions, you will **probably** (with high confidence) score **approximately** a perfect score (with very few errors) on the real test.

That's it. The name captures both halves of the promise:

- **"Probably"** — We can't be 100% certain, but we can be, say, 95% confident.
- **"Approximately Correct"** — We accept that the model might still make a few mistakes, as long as the error rate is very small.

---

## The Cast of Characters: Key Terms

Before we write any formulas, let's meet the players:

| Symbol | Name | What It Means (In Plain English) |
|--------|------|----------------------------------|
| **c** or **f** | Target Function | The *true, hidden rule* we want the computer to learn (e.g., "is this a dog?"). |
| **h** | Hypothesis | The computer's *current best guess* at the rule, after studying training data. |
| **D** | Distribution | The entire universe of possible data—seen and unseen. Think of it as the "real world." |
| **H** | Hypothesis Space | The "bucket" of *all possible rules* the computer is allowed to choose from. |
| **ε** (epsilon) | Error Tolerance | The maximum fraction of mistakes we're willing to accept (e.g., ε = 0.05 means ≤ 5% errors). |
| **δ** (delta) | Failure Probability | The maximum chance we're willing to tolerate that the whole thing goes wrong (e.g., δ = 0.05 means ≤ 5% chance of failure). |
| **m** | Sample Size | The number of training examples the computer needs to see. |
| **1 − δ** | Confidence | How confident we are that the model's error is below ε (e.g., 95% confident). |

## Two Kinds of Error

### True Error — How wrong are we *in the real world*?

The **True Error** measures how often the computer's guessed rule disagrees with the true rule when looking at a random piece of data from the entire universe:

$$E_{\text{true}}(h) \;=\; P_{x \sim D}\bigl[h(x) \neq c(x)\bigr]$$

**Translation:** Pick a random data point $x$ from the real world $D$. What's the probability that our guess $h(x)$ disagrees with the true answer $c(x)$? That probability is the true error.

The problem? We can never compute this directly because we don't have access to the entire universe of data.

### Empirical Error — How wrong are we *on the data we have*?

The **Empirical Error** counts mistakes on the training examples we actually possess:

$$E_{\text{emp}}(h) \;=\; \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \mathbb{I}\bigl[h(x_i) \neq c(x_i)\bigr]$$

**Translation:** Out of our $N$ training examples, what fraction did the computer get wrong? The symbol $\mathbb{I}$ is an **indicator function**—it outputs **1** when there's a mistake and **0** when the answer is correct. We count up all the 1's and divide by $N$.

The fundamental tension in machine learning is: *Can we trust that low empirical error means low true error?*

## The PAC Formula, Built From Scratch

### Step 1: The PAC Inequality

The central claim of PAC learning is captured in one inequality:

$$\boxed{P\bigl(E_{\text{true}}(h) \leq \varepsilon\bigr) \;\geq\; 1 - \delta}$$

**In English:** The probability that our model's true error is at most $\varepsilon$ is at least $1 - \delta$. In other words, we're at least $1 - \delta$ confident that the model is at least $1 - \varepsilon$ accurate.

### Step 2: How Can a Bad Model Fool Us?

Imagine a hypothesis $h$ that is actually *terrible*—its true error is greater than $\varepsilon$ (it's wrong more than $\varepsilon$ fraction of the time in the real world). But by sheer luck, it happens to classify every single one of our $m$ training examples correctly. We'd be fooled into thinking it's a great model!

What's the probability of this happening?

- The chance that this bad $h$ gets **one** random example right by luck is at most $(1 - \varepsilon)$, since it's wrong with probability $> \varepsilon$.
- The chance it gets **all $m$** examples right by luck is:

$$P(\text{one bad } h \text{ fools us}) \;\leq\; (1 - \varepsilon)^m$$

### Step 3: Making the Math Simpler

The expression $(1-\varepsilon)^m$ is exact but unwieldy. We use a very useful inequality from calculus:

$$1 - x \;\leq\; e^{-x} \quad \text{for all real } x$$

This is a tight approximation: for small $x$, $1-x \approx e^{-x}$. Applying it with $x = \varepsilon$:

$$(1-\varepsilon)^m \;\leq\; e^{-\varepsilon m}$$

Now the expression is simpler: the probability a single bad rule fools us decays **exponentially** as the sample size $m$ grows.

### Step 4: Accounting for All Possible Bad Rules (The Union Bound)

We don't have just one bad rule to worry about—we might have many. The **Union Bound** (also called Boole's inequality) tells us:

> The probability that *at least one* of several bad events occurs is at most the **sum** of their individual probabilities.

If our hypothesis space $H$ contains $|H|$ possible rules, the worst case is that *all* of them are bad. Summing their failure probabilities:

$$P(\text{any bad rule in } H \text{ fools us}) \;\leq\; |H| \cdot e^{-\varepsilon m}$$

**Why $|H|$?** Because in the worst case, every one of the $|H|$ rules could be bad, and each has at most an $e^{-\varepsilon m}$ chance of fooling us. We add them all up.

### Step 5: Deriving the Sample Complexity Formula

We want the failure probability to be at most $\delta$. So we set:

$$|H| \cdot e^{-\varepsilon m} \;\leq\; \delta$$

Now we **solve for $m$** step by step:

**Divide both sides by $|H|$:**

$$e^{-\varepsilon m} \;\leq\; \frac{\delta}{|H|}$$

**Take the natural logarithm (ln) of both sides.** Recall that $\ln(e^x) = x$ and that $\ln$ preserves inequality direction (since $\ln$ is a monotonically increasing function):

$$-\varepsilon m \;\leq\; \ln\!\left(\frac{\delta}{|H|}\right)$$

**Use the logarithm property** $\ln(a/b) = \ln a - \ln b$:

$$-\varepsilon m \;\leq\; \ln(\delta) - \ln(|H|)$$

**Multiply both sides by $-1$** (which flips the inequality):

$$\varepsilon m \;\geq\; \ln(|H|) - \ln(\delta)$$

**Rewrite** $-\ln(\delta) = \ln(1/\delta)$:

$$\varepsilon m \;\geq\; \ln(|H|) + \ln\!\left(\frac{1}{\delta}\right)$$

**Divide both sides by $\varepsilon$:**

$$\boxed{m \;\geq\; \frac{1}{\varepsilon}\!\left(\ln|H| + \ln\frac{1}{\delta}\right)}$$

This is the **Sample Complexity Formula** for finite hypothesis spaces. It tells us exactly how many training examples we need.

### What This Formula Tells Us

Let's break down the pieces intuitively:

| Term | What It Means | Why It Makes Sense |
|------|---------------|-------------------|
| $\ln\|H\|$ | Complexity of the rule space | More possible rules → more ways to be fooled → need more data |
| $\ln(1/\delta)$ | How confident we want to be | Higher confidence (smaller $\delta$) → need more data |
| $1/\varepsilon$ | How accurate we want to be | Lower tolerance for error → need more data |

Everything is in **logarithms** and **polynomials**. This is crucial: if the data requirement were **exponential** (like $10^{1/\varepsilon}$), then even tiny improvements in accuracy would require billions more samples, making learning practically impossible. PAC learning guarantees that learning is **tractable**—the data requirement grows at a manageable rate.

## A Concrete Numerical Example

Let's say we want to teach a computer a rule using **boolean conjunctions** (rules built with AND statements using $n$ variables). The hypothesis space has $|H| = 3^n$ possible rules.

We set:
- **ε = 0.1** (we want ≤ 10% error, i.e., ≥ 90% accuracy)
- **δ = 0.05** (we want ≥ 95% confidence)

Plugging into the formula:

$$m \;\geq\; \frac{1}{0.1}\!\left(\ln(3^n) + \ln\frac{1}{0.05}\right) \;=\; 10\!\left(n \cdot \ln 3 + \ln 20\right) \;\approx\; 10(1.099n + 3.0)$$

For $n = 10$ variables: $m \geq 10(10.99 + 3.0) = 10 \times 13.99 \approx 140$ samples.

That's remarkably few! With just ~140 labeled examples, we can be 95% confident that our model has less than 10% error [2].

## The Mathematical Engine Behind It All: Hoeffding's Inequality

The PAC bounds ultimately rest on a powerful statistical tool called **Hoeffding's Inequality**. It's worth understanding the chain of reasoning that produces it.

### Step 1: Markov's Inequality (The Starting Point)

For any **non-negative** random variable $X \geq 0$ and any positive value $a > 0$:

$$P(X \geq a) \;\leq\; \frac{\mathbb{E}[X]}{a}$$

**Intuition:** If the average height in a city is 5.5 feet, the probability of finding someone who is 11 feet tall is at most $5.5/11 = 50\%$. The further you go above the average, the less likely it gets.

### Step 2: Chebyshev's Inequality (Adding Variance)

Markov's bound is loose because it ignores how "spread out" the data is. **Chebyshev's Inequality** tightens it by incorporating the **variance** $\sigma^2$:

$$P\bigl(|X - \mu| \geq a\bigr) \;\leq\; \frac{\sigma^2}{a^2}$$

**Intuition:** Not only does the average matter, but how much the data varies matters too. Data clustered tightly around the mean (low $\sigma^2$) has much lower probability of extreme outliers.

### Step 3: Chernoff Bounds (Exponential Tightening)

By applying the exponential function $e^{tX}$ to Markov's Inequality (a technique called the *Moment Generating Function*), we get far tighter bounds:

$$P(X \geq a) \;\leq\; \min_{t > 0} \; e^{-ta} \cdot \mathbb{E}\bigl[e^{tX}\bigr]$$

This bounds the "tails" of the distribution—the probability of extreme deviations—with **exponential** precision.

### Step 4: Hoeffding's Inequality (The Final Result)

Combining Chernoff bounds with the fact that **bounded** random variables (those restricted to a range $[a_i, b_i]$) have tails that decay at least as fast as a Gaussian, we arrive at:

$$\boxed{P\bigl(|\bar{X} - \mathbb{E}[\bar{X}]| \geq \varepsilon\bigr) \;\leq\; 2\exp\!\left(\frac{-2n^2\varepsilon^2}{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(b_i - a_i)^2}\right)}$$

For the common case of **binary (0 or 1)** random variables, where $b_i - a_i = 1$:

$$P\bigl(|\bar{X} - \mathbb{E}[\bar{X}]| \geq \varepsilon\bigr) \;\leq\; 2e^{-2n\varepsilon^2}$$

**What this means:** As your sample size $n$ grows, the probability that your sample average $\bar{X}$ differs from the true average $\mathbb{E}[\bar{X}]$ by more than $\varepsilon$ drops **exponentially**. This is the mathematical reason why more data = more reliable estimates [4].

## When the Real World Is Messy: Agnostic PAC Learning

The derivation above assumes something optimistic: that somewhere in our bucket of rules $H$, there exists a **perfect** rule that makes zero training errors. In reality, data is noisy, and the best rule still makes mistakes.

**Agnostic PAC Learning** handles this by measuring the *gap* between training error and true error. The updated sample complexity formula is:

$$\boxed{m \;\geq\; \frac{1}{2\varepsilon^2}\!\left(\ln|H| + \ln\frac{1}{\delta}\right)}$$

Notice the key difference: the denominator is now $2\varepsilon^2$ instead of $\varepsilon$. This means we need **more data** when we can't find a perfect rule—but the framework still guarantees learning [2][3].

## Going Beyond Finite Rules: The VC Dimension

Our formulas so far require $|H|$ to be a finite number. But what if our hypothesis space contains *infinitely many* possible rules (e.g., all possible lines, curves, or neural networks)?

Enter the **VC Dimension** (Vapnik-Chervonenkis dimension). It measures the **capacity** or **complexity** of a hypothesis space. Specifically, it's the largest number of data points that the rule system can "shatter"—meaning it can perfectly separate them into any possible grouping, no matter how the labels are arranged.

The sample complexity formula using VC dimension $VC(H)$ is:

$$m \;>\; \frac{1}{\varepsilon}\!\left(8\,VC(H)\cdot\log\frac{13}{\varepsilon} + 4\log\frac{2}{\delta}\right)$$

**Core law of statistical learning:** A hypothesis class is (agnostically) PAC-learnable **if and only if** it has a **finite VC dimension** [2].

### VC Dimension Examples

| Hypothesis Class | VC Dimension | PAC-Learnable? |
|------------------|-------------|----------------|
| Axis-aligned rectangles in 2D | 4 | ✅ Yes |
| Polynomial classifiers of degree $d$ | $d + 1$ | ✅ Yes |
| Union of all polynomials (infinite degree) | ∞ | ❌ No |

## The PAC Conditions: A Formal Summary

A concept class $C$ is **PAC-learnable** if there exists an algorithm $L$ such that:

1. **For every** target rule $f \in C$...
2. **For every** probability distribution $D$ over the data...
3. **For every** error tolerance $\varepsilon$ (where $0 \leq \varepsilon \leq 1/2$)...
4. **For every** failure probability $\delta$ (where $0 < \delta \leq 1$)...

The algorithm $L$ is given $\varepsilon$, $\delta$, and a random sample of training data, and it must output a hypothesis $h$ satisfying:

$$\text{error}_D(h) \leq \varepsilon \quad \text{with probability} \geq 1 - \delta$$

and the number of samples required must be **polynomial** in $1/\varepsilon$ and $1/\delta$ [3][5].

## Why PAC Learning Matters

PAC learning gives us something rare in computer science: **a mathematical guarantee**. It tells us that:

- **Learning is possible**—there exist rigorous conditions under which a machine can reliably learn from data.
- **We can quantify the data needed**—the sample complexity formula puts a concrete number on "how much is enough."
- **There are limits**—systems with infinite VC dimension (like unconstrained neural networks) fall outside the theory's guarantees, reminding us that more flexible isn't always better.

The theory does make simplifying assumptions: that future data comes from the same distribution as training data (the *iid assumption*), and that the data contains genuine patterns (the *learnable regularity assumption*). In practice, these don't always hold perfectly—but PAC learning remains the foundational bedrock on which modern machine learning theory is built [1][5].
