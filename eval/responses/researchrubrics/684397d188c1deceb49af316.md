# Air India Flight AI171 Crash — Comprehensive Report

## Executive Summary

On June 12, 2025, Air India Flight AI171, a Boeing 787-8 Dreamliner en route from Ahmedabad to London Gatwick, crashed just 40 seconds after takeoff, killing 260 people — all but one of the 242 people on board, plus 19 on the ground. The sole survivor was British national Vishwashkumar Ramesh, seated in seat 11A. India's AAIB preliminary report, released July 12, 2025, found that both engine fuel-control switches simultaneously moved from "RUN" to "CUTOFF" within a second of each other, starving both engines of fuel at a critical phase of flight. The precise cause — mechanical failure, inadvertent pilot action, or a combination — remains undetermined. A final report is expected within 12 months.

## 2. Timeline of the Crash

Based on the AAIB preliminary report and cockpit voice/flight data recorders [1][2][3]:

| Time (GMT) | Event |
|---|---|
| **08:08:xx** | Aircraft lifts off from Runway 23 at Sardar Vallabhbhai Patel International Airport, Ahmedabad |
| **08:08:42** | Both fuel-control switches transition from "RUN" to "CUTOFF" — engines begin shutting down within one second |
| **08:08:42–08:09:01** | Cockpit voice recorder captures one pilot asking **"Why did you cut off?"** and the other replying **"I did not do so."** |
| **Ascent phase** | Aircraft climbs to approximately **625 feet**, airspeed reaches **180 knots**, then decelerates rapidly |
| **Descent (~29 seconds)** | Ram Air Turbine (RAT) deploys; Auxiliary Power Unit (APU) auto-starts; switches returned to "RUN"; Engine 1 begins relight sequence; Engine 2 fails to regain thrust |
| **08:09:08** | Pilots transmit: **"MAYDAY, MAYDAY, MAYDAY"** |
| **08:09:11** | Aircraft impacts the BJ Medical College hostel complex, erupting into a massive fireball |

The entire sequence from takeoff to impact lasted approximately **32–40 seconds**. The crash site was less than one nautical mile from the runway threshold. Five buildings were destroyed [1][2][3].

## 4. Investigation Findings (AAIB Preliminary Report — July 12, 2025)

### 4.1 Aircraft Airworthiness
The AAIB confirmed the aircraft's certificate of airworthiness was valid until May 2026. Routine maintenance had been completed, and no dangerous goods were on board. The throttle control module had been replaced in 2019 and 2023 for unrelated reasons [1][2].

### 4.2 The Critical Finding — Fuel-Control Switches
The central finding is that **both engine fuel-control switches moved almost simultaneously from the "RUN" to the "CUTOFF" position** at 08:08:42 GMT — a mere second after takeoff — causing an immediate, total loss of thrust [1][2][3].

On the Boeing 787, these switches are located between the pilots' seats, behind the throttle levers. They feature a **metal guard and a locking detent mechanism** — to flip a switch, an operator must physically lift it up and over a catch, making accidental activation highly unlikely under normal conditions [2][3].

### 4.3 The 2018 FAA Advisory
A **2018 US FAA bulletin** warned that fuel-control switches on certain Boeing aircraft (including the same design used on VT-ANB) could have **disengaged locking mechanisms**. Because the bulletin was advisory rather than mandatory, **Air India did not perform inspections** of these switches on the accident aircraft. No defects related to the switches had been reported on this 11-year-old aircraft since 2023 [1][2][3].

### 4.4 No Bird Activity or External Factors
The report noted no significant bird activity in the vicinity at the time of takeoff [2].

## 5. Analysis of Possible Causes

The critical mystery remains: **what caused both fuel switches to move to CUTOFF simultaneously?** The following hypotheses have emerged from aviation experts and reporting:

### Hypothesis A: Inadvertent Pilot Action
- During the high-workload takeoff phase, a pilot may have accidentally bumped or grabbed both switches. This is considered unlikely due to the physical guard and detent mechanism, but cannot be ruled out.
- Air safety specialist **Geoffrey Dell** noted that cutting fuel immediately after takeoff is "bizarre" and typically only done when parking an aircraft. He stated it requires at least a **two-action process** that cannot be done inadvertently [2].
- A breakdown in "sterile cockpit" discipline — meaning extraneous activity or conversation below 10,000 feet — could have contributed [3].

### Hypothesis B: Mechanical/Electrical Failure of the Switch Locking Mechanism
- The 2018 FAA advisory specifically flagged the **potential disengagement of the fuel control switch locking feature** on the exact switch design installed on this aircraft.
- If the locking mechanism had degraded or was improperly installed, vibrations during takeoff could have caused both switches to slip to CUTOFF simultaneously.
- A critical short circuit or electrical anomaly in the switch assembly could have triggered dual movement [3].

### Hypothesis C: Undetected Structural Defect
- An underlying, previously undetected flaw in the Boeing 787 fuel-switch housing or locking assembly could have allowed simultaneous disengagement [3].

### Hypothesis D: Deliberate Action
- The cockpit voice recording suggests one pilot believed the other acted, while the second denied it. Investigators have not yet "speaker-stamped" the voices to determine which pilot was speaking at each point. This remains an open line of inquiry, though former pilot **Ehsan Khalid** cautioned against prematurely blaming the pilots, noting the black box confirms the crew did not intentionally cause the cutoff [1][2].

### Expert Consensus
Most analysts consider the simultaneous dual cutoff to point toward either a **mechanical failure of the locking mechanism** (especially given the unaddressed FAA advisory) or an **inadvertent procedural error** exacerbated by the high-stress takeoff environment. The final report — expected within 12 months — will be critical [1][2][3].

## 6. Aftermath and Fallout

- **Public Trust:** The crash caused a significant spike in flying anxiety across India and intensified regulatory scrutiny on Air India's maintenance practices [3].
- **Regulatory Response:** India's DGCA mandated **immediate safety and power-assurance checks** on Air India's entire Boeing 787 fleet. South Korea also directed its airlines to conduct precautionary checks on Boeing models [2][3].
- **Boeing/FAA Position:** The US FAA and Boeing have privately asserted that the fuel-switch locks are safe. The US NTSB noted there are **no recommended actions for 787 operators** at this time [2][3].
- **Families' Demands:** Nearly 10 months after the crash, approximately 30 families of Gujarati victims wrote to Prime Minister Modi demanding public release of the cockpit voice recorder and black box data, citing a lack of transparency. Families have also struggled with bureaucratic hurdles in claiming victims' belongings [4].
- **Air India CEO Campbell Wilson** urged against premature conclusions, noting the preliminary findings raise additional questions [3].

## 7. Recommendations for Preventing Future Incidents

Based on findings from the investigation, expert commentary, and industry best practices, the following recommendations emerge:

### 7.1 Mandatory Hardware Inspections
- The FAA must issue a **legally binding Airworthiness Directive** (not merely an advisory bulletin) requiring all Boeing 787 and 737 operators to **immediately inspect and verify** the proper engagement of fuel-control switch locking mechanisms. The 2018 bulletin's advisory status allowed Air India to skip these inspections — a regulatory gap that must be closed [1][2][3].

### 7.2 Redesign of Fuel-Control Switch Mechanisms
- Boeing should redesign the fuel cutoff switches to require a **more deliberate, multi-step physical action** (e.g., lift-and-turn, or pull-and-rotate) to prevent accidental engagement. The current detent mechanism, while designed to resist accidental movement, may be insufficient given this incident [3].

### 7.3 Software Safety Interlocks
- Boeing should develop and retrofit **avionics software interlocks** that prevent dual fuel cutoff during critical flight phases (takeoff and initial climb) unless the system detects a dual-engine fire or catastrophic failure requiring intentional shutdown. A secondary confirmation input or dual-pilot command could serve as an additional safeguard [1][2][3].

### 7.4 Enhanced Pilot Training
- Airlines must implement **simulator training specifically addressing sudden dual-engine power loss immediately after takeoff** — a scenario historically under-trained in commercial aviation.
- Cockpit procedures should be updated to **assign one pilot to physically guard the fuel switches** during emergency checklists to prevent inadvertent bumping [1][2].

### 7.5 Sterile Cockpit Enforcement
- Stricter adherence to and monitoring of **"sterile cockpit" rules** below 10,000 feet must be enforced to ensure absolute pilot focus during takeoff and initial climb phases [3].

### 7.6 Engine Relight Optimization
- Boeing and engine manufacturer GE should collaborate to **enhance the auto-relight capabilities of the GEnx-1B engines**, ensuring faster restoration of thrust during catastrophic power losses at low altitudes. In the AI171 crash, Engine 1 began to relight but there was insufficient time/altitude for recovery [1].

### 7.7 Comprehensive Maintenance Audits
- Airlines must perform **rigorous, unannounced mechanical audits** on engine and fuel-control systems. All advisory bulletins from manufacturers and regulators should be treated as mandatory unless explicitly risk-assessed and documented as low-risk by certified engineers [3].

### 7.8 Black Box Transparency
- Investigators should expedite the full release of unredacted cockpit transcripts to determine whether miscommunication, spatial disorientation, or cognitive overload occurred. Transparency will also help rebuild public trust [2].

---

## 8. Uncertainty and Gaps

- **Root cause undetermined:** The preliminary report confirms *what* happened (fuel switches moved to CUTOFF) but not *why*. The distinction between mechanical failure, inadvertent pilot action, or deliberate act remains unresolved.
- **Voice identification pending:** The cockpit voices have not yet been speaker-stamped, meaning it is unclear which pilot asked "why did you cut off?" and which denied doing so.
- **Final report timeline:** The full AAIB investigation report is expected within 12 months of the crash (i.e., by mid-2026). Until then, conclusions are necessarily provisional.
- **Boeing/FAA resistance:** Both Boeing and the FAA have maintained that the fuel-switch design is safe, which may slow the adoption of design changes.
- **Limited survivor testimony:** While Vishwashkumar Ramesh survived, detailed public accounts of his experience inside the cabin during the 29-second descent have been limited.
