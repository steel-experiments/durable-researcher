# Global Uranium Market: Geopolitical & Market Factors — FY2025 Investor Report

---

## Executive Summary

Global uranium prices in FY2025 are defined by a structural supply deficit driven by concurrent Western decoupling from Russian nuclear fuel, the collapse of Niger's uranium sector following a military coup, and accelerating nuclear power demand from China, India, and the data-center boom. Spot U₃O₈ prices have oscillated between ~$64 and ~$83/lb in 2025, recovering from a post-peak correction in 2024 but remaining well below the January 2024 high of ~$100/lb. Long-term contract prices have steadily climbed from $80 to $86.50/lb through 2025, reflecting durable confidence in sustained pricing. Investors should expect continued volatility with a bullish structural bias through at least 2027.

---

## 1. Price Action & Market Structure

**Spot Price:** Uranium spot prices (U₃O₈) peaked at ~$100.25/lb in January 2024 before retreating to ~$72.63 by December 2024—a roughly 28% correction. In FY2025, spot has been range-bound between $64.23 (March) and $82.63 (September), recovering toward year-end at $81.55 in December. [1]

**Long-Term Price:** Long-term contract prices have demonstrated remarkable stability, rising from $80.00/lb in January 2025 to $86.50/lb by December 2025—a steady 8% gain. This spot-long-term divergence signals that utilities are willing to lock in elevated prices, underscoring structural supply anxiety. [1]

**Interpretation:** The rising long-term price floor alongside volatile spot markets is a textbook signal of a market transitioning from oversupply to structural deficit. Producers with long-lived assets in stable jurisdictions stand to benefit disproportionately.

## 2. Supply-Side Geopolitical Disruptions

### 2.1 The Russia Decoupling

The single most consequential structural shift in the uranium market is the Western effort to sever dependence on Russian nuclear fuel.

- **US Ban:** In May 2024, President Biden signed the Prohibiting Russian Uranium Imports Act, banning imports of Russian low-enriched uranium (LEU) effective August 2024. The legislation includes a temporary waiver process and unlocks **$2.72 billion** in federal funding for domestic enrichment capacity. US utilities hold an estimated ~3 years of LEU inventory and pre-existing contracts, providing a near-term buffer. [2]

- **Russian Retaliation:** In November 2024, Russia imposed temporary restrictions on enriched uranium exports to the US. Russia controls **~44% of global enrichment capacity** and supplied **27% of enriched uranium** to US commercial reactors in 2023. US imports from Russia through July 2024 had already fallen 30% YoY to 313,050 kg. [3]

- **EU Pivot:** The EU imports ~€700 million annually in Russian uranium products. Russia accounts for **23% of EU raw uranium**, **27% of conversion services**, and **38% of enriched uranium supply**. Nineteen Soviet-era VVER reactors (11 GW) remain almost entirely dependent on Rosatom. The EU is formulating a phased embargo strategy, with initial targeting of enriched natural uranium. Full replacement of Russian capacity is estimated to take **up to 7 years** (through ~2030). [4]

- **Western Capacity Build-Out:** Orano is planning a **2,500 tSW** enrichment expansion; Urenco is adding **1,800 tSW**. Westinghouse and Cameco are evaluating a new 5,000 tU conversion facility in Springfields, UK. These additions collectively target replacing the **6,600 tSW** that the US and EU currently source from Russia. [4]

### 2.2 The Niger Crisis

The July 2023 military coup in Niger has triggered the most significant African supply disruption in decades:

- **Operational Collapse:** Niger's junta seized the SOMAÏR mine (December 2023), revoked Orano's Imouraren permit (June 2024), and stripped Canadian firm GoviEx of its Madouela deposit rights. Orano suspended operations at Arlit by October 2024. Orano has filed for ICSID arbitration seeking over **€1.4 billion** in damages. [5, 6]

- **Supply Impact:** Niger's production has plummeted from **3,000 tonnes** (5.8% of global supply) in 2021 to a projected **1,200 tonnes** (2.4%) in 2024—a loss of approximately **1,800 tonnes of annual output**. Niger previously accounted for **24.3% of EU uranium imports** and **15–17% of France's** annual requirements. [5, 6]

- **Geopolitical Realignment:** The junta is aligning with Russia's Rosatom (conducting due diligence for ~$850M acquisition) and China's CNNC (offered $720M for assets). Niger's "Sovereign Mineral Strategy" mandates majority state ownership in all extractive projects. Russian and Chinese state-owned enterprises are positioning to capture the supply void. [6]

- **Market Pricing Impact:** The Niger crisis has contributed to **22% uranium price volatility** since Q4 2024. Long-term contracts now embed a **15–20% geopolitical risk premium** for African-sourced uranium versus just 5–8% for Canadian or Australian supply. [6]

### 2.3 Kazakhstan Uncertainty

Kazakhstan (via Kazatomprom) is the world's largest uranium producer. The country faces 2024 export quota constraints and infrastructure challenges (notably sulfuric acid supply for in-situ recovery mining). Any disruption to Kazakh output—whether from Russian influence over Central Asian logistics, infrastructure degradation, or policy shifts—would compound the existing supply deficit. Combined with the Niger disruption, geopolitical shocks now threaten nearly **10% of global uranium supply**. [5]

## 3. Demand-Side Drivers

### 3.1 Global Nuclear Renaissance

Over 60 countries announced nuclear expansion plans at COP28, with a target to **triple nuclear capacity by 2050**. Approximately **24 reactors** are in advanced planning stages globally, requiring an estimated **8,000 tonnes of additional annual uranium supply**. [5, 6]

### 3.2 China & India Expansion

Despite broader economic headwinds from US-China tariff escalation (with effective tariff rates reaching 18.2%, the highest since 1934), China's nuclear build-out remains a central policy priority. China has approximately **150 reactors planned** by 2035. India's nuclear program continues to expand as part of its energy security strategy. These programs provide a **durable demand floor** relatively insulated from macroeconomic cycles. [7, 8]

### 3.3 Data Centers & AI Power Demand

Global electricity consumption rose **4.3% in 2024**, driven by electrification, extreme weather, and AI data centers. Data center electricity demand is projected to surge from **1% of global energy demand (2022) to over 3% by 2030**. This is creating acute demand for reliable, baseload power—increasingly met by nuclear—particularly in the US and Europe. [7]

### 3.4 Clean Energy Investment

Global clean energy investment reached **$2 trillion in 2025**, with nuclear increasingly recognized as essential for grid reliability amid intermittent renewable expansion. [7]

---

## 4. Macro & Financial Backdrop

- **Global Growth Downgrades:** The IMF has revised 2025 global growth downward to **2.8%** (a cumulative 0.8 ppt downgrade since January 2025). Projected global trade growth has fallen to **1.7%**. Slower growth tempers near-term electricity demand growth. [8]

- **Tariff Escalation:** Near-universal US tariffs introduced April 2, 2025, are redrawing global trade maps and raising input costs across energy supply chains. [7, 8]

- **Financial Conditions:** Rising sovereign debt, tightening financial conditions, and potential monetary tightening could weigh on dollar-denominated commodity prices. However, localized supply shocks in critical minerals may counteract macroeconomic headwinds. [8]

## 5. Investment Implications

| Factor | Direction | Impact on Uranium Prices |
|--------|-----------|--------------------------|
| US/EU ban on Russian nuclear fuel | Structural supply removal | **Strongly bullish** |
| Russia retaliatory export restrictions | Supply uncertainty | **Bullish / volatile** |
| Niger coup & production collapse | ~1,800 tU annual supply loss | **Bullish** |
| Western enrichment capacity build-out | Medium-term supply relief (2028–2030) | **Long-term moderating** |
| China/India nuclear expansion | Structural demand growth | **Strongly bullish** |
| AI & data center power demand | Baseload power demand surge | **Bullish** |
| Global economic slowdown / tariffs | Reduced consumption risk | **Mildly bearish** |
| Rising long-term contract prices | Utility willingness to pay | **Bullish signal** |

### Key Conclusions

1. **Structural deficit persists through 2027+.** The combined loss of Russian enrichment access, Niger production, and Kazakh uncertainty removes a significant portion of global supply, while demand from nuclear expansion grows relentlessly.

2. **Jurisdiction matters.** Producers in stable mining jurisdictions (Canada, Australia, Namibia) command an increasing premium. African-sourced uranium now carries a 15–20% geopolitical risk premium.

3. **Western enrichment is a bottleneck.** Russia's 44% share of global enrichment capacity represents the single tightest chokepoint. Companies positioned to win DOE and allied contracts (Cameco, Orano, Urenco, Centrus) are structurally advantaged.

4. **Price floor is established.** Long-term prices steadily rising to $86.50/lb despite macroeconomic headwinds signals that the market has established a new, elevated floor. Sustained pricing above $80/lb through 2026 appears likely.

5. **Chinese and Russian strategic accumulation** of uranium assets in Africa (Niger) introduces a competitive dynamic that could further tighten supply available to Western utilities.

---

## Risk Factors

- **Inventory overhang:** US utilities hold ~3 years of LEU inventory, potentially dampening spot demand in the near term.
- **Waiver extensions:** The US ban includes temporary waivers through 2027; extended waivers could slow the supply transition.
- **Demand destruction from macro weakness:** A deeper-than-expected global recession could slow reactor construction timelines.
- **Kazakh production recovery:** If Kazakhstan resolves its operational challenges, significant new supply could enter the market.
