## Blog Post 1: Invariants and Monovariants — The Art of Finding What Doesn't Change (and What Does)

### Introduction

One of the most powerful ideas in olympiad combinatorics is deceptively simple: **stop trying to track everything, and focus on what stays the same** (an invariant) or what moves steadily in one direction (a monovariant). These two concepts form the backbone of countless competition problems involving games, processes, and transformations.

**Intuition:** Imagine watching a chaotic card-shuffling routine. You could try to track every card — or you could count the number of face-up cards. If that number never changes, you've found an invariant. If it always increases, you've found a monovariant. Either way, you've turned noise into signal.

### When to Use This Technique

- A problem describes a **repeated operation** or **transformation**.
- You're asked to prove something is **impossible** (use an invariant to show the target state violates it).
- You're asked to prove a process **must terminate** (use a monovariant that strictly increases/decreases and is bounded).
- You're asked to determine the **final state** of a process.

### Example Problem: 2019 IMO Problem 3

> **Problem.** A social network has $n$ users, some pairs of whom are friends. Friendship is mutual. The following event may happen repeatedly:
>
> Three users $x$, $y$, and $z$ such that $x$ is friends with both $y$ and $z$, but $y$ and $z$ are not friends, change their friendship statuses: $y$ and $z$ become friends, $x$ is no longer friends with $y$, and $x$ is no longer friends with $z$.
>
> Initially, some number of users have exactly $2n-2$ friends each, and the remaining users have exactly $2n-3$ friends each. Prove that there exists a sequence of such events after which each user is friends with at most one other user.

#### Solution

**Model the problem as a graph.** Let $G$ be a graph where vertices are users and edges are friendships.

**Key Observations — Invariants and Monovariants:**

1. **Connectivity (Invariant).** $G$ is connected. Why? Two non-adjacent vertices with no common friend would need at least $(2n-3) + (2n-3) = 4n-6$ distinct neighbors between them. But the rest of the graph has only $n - 2$ vertices, and $4n - 6 > n - 2$ for $n \geq 2$. So any two vertices either share a friend or are directly connected, and the operation preserves connectivity.

2. **Parity of degrees (Invariant).** In each event, the vertex $x$ loses 2 friends (degree drops by 2), while $y$ and $z$ each gain 1 and lose 1 (net change 0). So the parity of every vertex's degree is preserved. In particular, the number of odd-degree vertices is invariant.

3. **Number of edges (Monovariant).** Each event removes 2 edges ($xy$ and $xz$) and adds 1 edge ($yz$). The total number of edges strictly **decreases** by 1 each step. This is our crucial monovariant — it guarantees the process terminates.

4. **Non-clique property (Invariant).** Since the edge count strictly decreases, and the initial graph doesn't contain all possible edges (easy to verify from the degree conditions), the graph never becomes a clique.

**The Algorithm:**

*Phase 1 — Eliminate cycles.* While the graph has a cycle:
- If the smallest cycle has length $> 3$, there exists a chord-like configuration allowing us to apply the event, reducing the cycle structure.
- If the smallest cycle is a triangle, take a maximal clique $K$. Since the graph is not a clique, there's a vertex outside $K$ adjacent to a vertex in $K$, giving us the configuration to apply an event.

This phase terminates because the number of edges strictly decreases. The result is a tree (connected, no cycles).

*Phase 2 — Break down the tree.* In a tree, whenever a vertex has degree $\geq 2$, we can apply an event (with two of its neighbors), which splits the tree into two smaller trees. Repeat until every component has at most 2 vertices.

Since each step decreases the total edge count, the process terminates. The result is a forest where each tree has at most 2 vertices — meaning each user has at most one friend. $\blacksquare$

#### Key Takeaways

| Insight | Lesson |
|---|---|
| Edge count strictly decreases | Always look for a **non-negative integer monovariant** to guarantee termination |
| Parity of degrees is preserved | **Parity invariants** are among the most common and useful invariants |
| Two-phase approach: break cycles, then trees | Complex processes can often be split into **simpler sub-processes** |
| Graph modeling | When a problem mentions pairwise relationships, **model it as a graph** |

## Blog Post 2: Graph Theory — Cliques, Colorings, and Structural Arguments

### Introduction

Graph theory is arguably the single most important subfield of combinatorics for olympiad competitors. Problems about friendships, tournaments, roads between cities, or handshakes at a party are all graph theory in disguise. The key skills are: (1) recognizing when to model a situation as a graph, and (2) knowing which structural results and extremal arguments to deploy.

**Intuition:** A graph $G = (V, E)$ is just a collection of points (vertices) and connections between them (edges). The magic of graph theory is that this simple abstraction captures an enormous range of mathematical situations.

### Core Concepts for Olympiads

- **Complete graphs and cliques** — sets of mutually adjacent vertices
- **Bipartite graphs** — graphs whose vertices can be split into two groups with all edges between groups
- **Degree sequences** — the multiset of vertex degrees
- **Trees and forests** — acyclic connected graphs
- **Graph coloring** — assigning colors to vertices so no adjacent vertices share a color
- **Turán-type results** — what structures are forced by edge density
