# **THE NEXUS BOY**

### *A Novel with Interchangeable Chapters*

---

## AUTHOR'S NOTE: HOW TO READ THIS BOOK

This novel contains **twelve chapters**. Eleven of them can be read in **any order you choose**. The twelfth — the final chapter — is fixed and must be read last.

The chapters are divided into three pools:

- **THE MEDIEVAL WORLDS (M-I through M-V):** Each chapter drops the protagonist, a sixteen-year-old boy named **Kael**, into a completely different medieval fantasy realm. No two worlds are alike.
- **THE CYBERPUNK WORLDS (C-I through C-V):** Each chapter hurls Kael into a completely different dystopian future. No two futures share the same city, the same rules, or the same technology.
- **THE CONVERGENCE (Chapter 12):** This is the only chapter that must be read last. It is the fixed point — the anchor. Everything you've picked up in any order will resolve here.

**How to choose your path:**
You may read the medieval and cyberpunk chapters in alternating fashion, or binge all of one theme before the other. You may hop randomly. Every chapter contains self-contained story arcs, but each also hides **fragments** — recurring symbols, names, and clues — that gain meaning the more chapters you complete. Like a mosaic, the picture sharpens with every piece you place.

*A suggested "hopscotch" order is provided at the end of this book for those who want a curated experience. But the truest experience is the one you choose.*

## PROLOGUE: THE CRACK IN EVERYTHING

*Read this first. It's the only rule.*

---

The night Kael Ashworth discovered he was not entirely real, he was standing in his kitchen making a peanut butter sandwich.

It was 11:47 PM on a Thursday in October. The refrigerator hummed. The house was quiet the way only an empty house can be quiet — not peaceful, but hollow, like a cathedral with no congregation. His mother had been gone three days this time. She'd left a note on the counter with a twenty-dollar bill and the words *Back soon. Don't open the door for anyone.* Kael had stopped opening the door for anyone a long time ago.

He was spreading the peanut butter when his fingers went through the knife.

Not around it. Not past it. *Through* it — as though the knife and his hand occupied the same space but different layers of existence. The bread tore. The peanut butter smeared across the counter in a long, impossible arc, as if the world had stuttered.

Then the kitchen folded.

There is no other word for it. The walls didn't collapse or dissolve — they *folded*, like a piece of paper being creased along a line that shouldn't exist, and through the fold Kael saw —

Not darkness. Not light. Something worse.

A **spiral**, massive and intricate, carved into the fabric of everywhere. A pattern that seemed to contain all patterns, all geometries, all possible shapes — but it was cracked. A jagged fracture ran from its center to its outermost ring, and through that crack bled a color Kael had never seen and couldn't name, a color that made his teeth ache and his eyes water.

The spiral lasted maybe two seconds. Then the kitchen snapped back into place. The knife clattered onto the counter. The peanut butter jar rolled and fell, shattering on the tile floor.

Kael stood perfectly still, breathing hard, his hand trembling.

On his inner wrist — the one that had passed through the knife — a mark had appeared. It was small, no bigger than a coin: a spiral with a crack through it. The same spiral. It shimmered faintly, like a bruise made of light.

He touched it. It was warm.

The next morning, he told no one. There was no one to tell. He went to school. He came home. He did his homework. He made another sandwich — with a different knife, because the first one had bent at an angle that metal should not bend, as if it had remembered being passed through and had given up on being solid.

For three weeks, nothing happened.

Then, in the middle of a history lecture on the fall of Constantinople, Kael sneezed — and the classroom folded.

---

This time he didn't just see the spiral. He fell *through* the crack in it.

The sensation was impossible to describe accurately, but if forced, Kael would say it felt like being turned inside out, shaken like a snow globe, and reassembled by someone who had the instructions but wasn't sure which way the legs went. It hurt. Not sharply, but deeply, in the way that a dream can hurt — a pain that is somehow more real than real because it bypasses the body entirely and strikes at something underneath.

When the folding stopped, Kael was not in his classroom.

He was lying on his back in a field of grey grass, beneath a sky the color of rust. The air smelled of ash and old iron. In the distance, a city burned — not with the clean orange of a house fire, but with a sickly green flame that seemed to eat the sky. Banners flew from towers that looked like they had been grown from bone rather than built from stone. And rising above everything, visible even from this distance, was a wall — a wall so vast it curved with the horizon, encircling the entire world like a cage.

A man in dented armor, carrying a sword that was more notch than blade, stumbled past Kael and looked down at him with eyes that had seen too much.

"Another one," the man said, in a language Kael should not have understood but did. "The rift spat out another child."

Then the man kept walking, because in this world, a boy lying in a field was the least of everyone's problems.

Kael looked at his wrist. The spiral-crack mark pulsed once.

He was not in history class anymore.

He was not in *anywhere* he recognized.

And this was only the first time.

---

Over the weeks and months that followed — or perhaps years; time moved differently in the fold — Kael learned the rules. Not from a teacher or a manual, but through the brutal curriculum of experience.

**Rule One:** The shift was triggered by intense emotion. Not just any emotion — *decisive* emotion. A moment when Kael's mind crystallized around a single, powerful feeling: terror, wonder, rage, grief, love. The stronger the feeling, the more violent the shift.

**Rule Two:** He never went to the same world twice. Each shift deposited him into a completely new reality. Some were medieval — castles and kings, magic and monsters, sword and sorcery. Some were futures — neon and chrome, corporations and hackers, flesh and wire. But within each category, no two worlds shared anything but a *texture*. Medieval worlds all felt old and heavy and bound by rules that had been written before anyone could remember. Cyberpunk worlds all felt fast and sharp and bound by rules that had been written by people who would be dead by next year.

**Rule Three:** Time was not consistent. In some worlds, he arrived as a child. In others, he arrived as a young man. His body changed, but his mind — his *Kael-ness* — remained intact, like a thread woven through different fabrics.

**Rule Four:** He could not control where he went. But he could feel the shift coming, like a wave gathering beneath him, and he learned to brace.

**Rule Five:** There were fragments. Clues. In every world — medieval or future — he found the same symbols, the same recurring faces, the same echoes. A spiral with a crack. A girl with silver hair who was never quite the same person twice. A word, spoken in different languages, that always meant the same thing: *Nexus.* And something that pursued him — something that existed in the space between worlds, something that fed on the crack in the spiral, something that Kael could only call **the Hollow**.

This is the story of those worlds.

Read them in whatever order you wish. They are all true. They are all happening at once, in the way that a song is all happening at once even though you hear one note at a time.

But remember: every song ends the same way.

## CHAPTER M-I: THE BONEWALL

### *Medieval World One: The Siege of Greyath*

---

Kael arrived in this world as a seventeen-year-old, already wearing clothes that weren't his — a rough-spun tunic the color of dried mud, boots that had been resoled so many times the leather was more patch than original material, and a leather belt with a rusted iron buckle shaped like a falcon.

He was in a ditch.

Above him, the sky was the color of a bruise — deep purple shot through with veins of sickly green. The air tasted like copper and wet stone. Sounds filtered down to him: the rhythmic thudding of something massive striking stone, the distant screaming of men, the groaning of wood under stress.

Kael pulled himself out of the ditch and found himself looking at a battlefield, though "battlefield" was generous. It was a slaughterhouse. The bodies were arranged in patterns that suggested order had once existed — shield walls, formations, charging lines — but chaos had long since won. Soldiers in armor that ranged from functional to ornamental lay tangled together, their blood mixing in the churned mud.

And beyond the battlefield, dominating the horizon like a second sky, was the Bonewall.

It was exactly what it sounded like: a wall built from bones. Not small bones — great, massive bones, femurs and ribs and vertebrae from creatures that must have been the size of cathedrals when they were alive. The wall stretched in both directions as far as Kael could see, and it rose perhaps two hundred feet into the air. Behind it, a city smoldered. Before it, an army camped.

The army was disorganized, demoralized, and dying. Kael could see that even from a distance. Tents leaned at angles that suggested their owners had stopped caring. Cooking fires burned without tending. Soldiers sat in the mud, staring at nothing.

"You're the rift-born."

Kael turned. A woman stood behind him, leaning on a spear. She was perhaps forty, with a face that had been handsome before it had been broken — a scar ran from her left temple to her jaw, and her nose had been reset at least once at a bad angle. She wore chainmail that had been patched with leather and cloth.

"I'm Commander Sera Vane," she said. "Third Legion of the Free Cities. Or what's left of it." She looked at Kael with the flat, assessing gaze of someone who had stopped being surprised by anything a long time ago. "You came through a fold in the air. We've been seeing them more often. Usually they spit out supplies. Sometimes they spit out people." She paused. "Never spit out a boy before."

"I'm not a boy," Kael said, with the automatic defensiveness of someone who was, in fact, a boy.

"You're a boy," Sera said. "Can you fight?"

"No."

"Can you carry things?"

"Yes."

"Then you're a porter. Welcome to the Third Legion. Try not to die before breakfast."

---

The world Kael had landed in was called Greyath — or rather, that was the name of the city behind the Bonewall. The world itself had no name that anyone could agree on. Some called it the Grey Realm. Others called it the Cage, because of the wall that encircled everything. Still others called it the Last Kingdom, though no one could say what it was the last of, or what had come before it.

What Kael learned, in the days he spent carrying water and bandages and bodies across the siege camp, was this:

Greyath had been under siege for **eleven years**.

The besiegers were called the Ashen — an army that seemed to have no homeland, no families, no supply lines, and no end. They simply *were*, like a weather pattern. Every dawn, they attacked the Bonewall. Every dusk, they retreated. Every night, the defenders repaired what they could and mourned what they couldn't. And every few weeks, the Ashen found a new way to be terrible — siege engines that fired burning corpses, tunnels that collapsed sections of the wall from below, mages who could turn the bones of the wall against itself.

The war was not going well.

"The Bonewall was built by the God-Kings," Sera told Kael one night, as they sat by a fire that did almost nothing against the cold. "Thousands of years ago. They slew the Great Beasts and raised the wall from their remains to protect the last city. The wall is alive, in its way. It heals. It remembers." She stared into the fire. "But it's dying. Everything dies."

Kael looked at his wrist. The spiral-crack mark was warm. In this world, in the presence of a wall made from the bones of dead gods, it pulsed in a slow, steady rhythm — like a heartbeat.

"Why do the Ashen want the city?" he asked.

Sera laughed. It was not a happy sound. "No one knows. They don't negotiate. They don't take prisoners. They don't plant flags or claim territory. They just... come. Every day. Like the tide." She looked at Kael. "Some people think they're not human. That they're something that came from outside the wall — from beyond the edge of the world. Something that wants in because we're in here." She shrugged. "I think they're just people. People don't need a reason to be cruel."

---

On the seventh day of Kael's time in Greyath, the Ashen broke through.

It happened at dawn, as it always did, but this time they brought something new: a creature. It was tall — impossibly tall, thirty feet at least — and it was made of the same grey ash that gave the army its name. It moved like a man but was not a man, and where it walked, the ground turned black and died.

The Bonewall groaned. The creature pressed its hands against the ancient structure, and the bones began to crumble. Not break — *age*. The creature was making the wall grow old, forcing millennia of decay into seconds.

"ALL LEGIONS TO THE BREACH!" The call went up, and the camp exploded into motion.

Kael grabbed a fallen soldier's sword. It was too heavy for him. He grabbed it anyway.

At the breach, the world was noise and blood and screaming. The Ashen poured through the gap in the wall like water through a broken dam. They were human-shaped but wrong — their faces were smooth, featureless, as if someone had started sculpting people and given up before the details. They fought without sound, without expression, without fear.

Kael had never fought before. He discovered that fighting was mostly about not dying long enough for someone else to kill the person trying to kill you. He swung the sword more like a club than a blade. He hit an Ashen soldier in the shoulder, and the soldier didn't flinch. He hit another in the head, and it went down, but two more took its place.

Through the chaos, he saw the giant ash creature, still pressing its hands against the Bonewall, still killing the wall with time. And he saw something else: a **spiral**, carved into the creature's back. A spiral with a crack through it.

*His* spiral. His mark.

The recognition hit him like a punch. The emotion was instant and overwhelming — not fear, not anger, but something deeper: **recognition**. The gut-level certainty that he was looking at something that was, in some terrible way, *related to him*.

The wave gathered beneath him.

"No—" Kael gasped, but it was too late.

The world folded. The battlefield creased. The spiral appeared, massive and cracked, and Kael fell through the fracture, leaving Greyath and its dying wall behind.

His last image was of Sera Vane, standing in the breach, her broken spear raised against an army that would never stop coming.

---

He would never know if Greyath survived.

But he would remember Sera's face for the rest of his life — however many lives that turned out to be.

## CHAPTER C-I: NEON REQUIEM

### *Cyberpunk World One: The City of Shenzhen-9*

---

Kael landed in a puddle of something that was either water or light. Given where he was, it could have been either.

He was in a city. No — he was in a *trench* between two cities. Above him, towers rose like glass and steel cliffs, so high their tops disappeared into a ceiling of perpetual smog that glowed orange from the neon signs below. The signs were everywhere: Chinese characters, Japanese katakana, Korean hangul, English slogans, all competing for retinal space in a riot of color that painted the wet streets in shifting, epileptic rainbows.

Kael looked down at himself. He was wearing a sleeveless jacket made of a material that felt like rubber but reacted to his body heat, changing color slightly as he moved. His arms were bare, and on his left forearm — where the spiral-crack mark had been — there was now something new: a **subdermal implant**, a thin strip of flexible screen that displayed his vital signs in glowing blue text.

**HEART RATE: 142 BPM**
**BLOOD OXYGEN: 94%**
**NEURAL SYNC: UNREGISTERED**

He was older in this world. Maybe nineteen. His body was leaner, harder, and there were scars on his knuckles that he hadn't earned.

"Hey. Hey! You gonna move or you gonna stand in the juice all night?"

A girl stood at the edge of the puddle — the *light* puddle — looking at him with the expression of someone who had no patience for anything, ever. She was maybe seventeen, with close-cropped silver hair — not dyed, Kael realized, but naturally metallic, as if each strand had been replaced with fine wire. Her eyes were augmented: the irises were too large, too bright, and they contracted and expanded like camera apertures as they focused on him.

She wore a patchwork of salvaged tech — a cracked AR visor pushed up on her forehead, fingerless gloves with exposed circuitry, boots that had been reinforced with scrap metal plates. Around her neck hung a pendant: a small, spiral-shaped charm.

Kael's breath caught.

"You're new," the girl said, studying him. "New body-print, new neural signature, no Corp-ID. You're either a ghost or a stray." She tilted her head. "I'm Lira. Lira Chen. And you're in my alley, so you're either buying food or getting out."

"I'm Kael," he said. His voice was deeper in this body. "I don't know where I am."

Lira's augmented eyes flickered — processing. "You're in Shenzhen-9. Sector Seven. Below the Skyline, above the Flood. Population: nobody's counted in twenty years." She looked at him more carefully. "You really don't know?"

"I really don't."

"Great." She turned and started walking. "Come on. I know a place where you can eat and I can figure out what kind of trouble you are."

---

Shenzhen-9, Kael learned, was one of the **Megacity Clusters** — a chain of hyper-dense urban zones that stretched across what had once been coastal China. The cities had grown into each other over decades of uncontrolled expansion, creating a continuous urban mass of roughly three hundred million people, stacked vertically into three layers: the Skyline (corporate towers, clean air, sunlight), the Midway (commercial zones, manufacturing, residential cubes), and the Below (everything else).

Lira lived in the Below.

She was a **scraper** — someone who salvaged technology from the ruins of older city layers and sold it on the gray market. She was also, Kael gradually realized, something more. She had contacts everywhere — in the Midway markets, in the Below's underground network of unregistered clinics and data dens, and even, she claimed, in the Skyline.

"The Corps run everything up there," she told him, as they sat in a cramped noodle shop carved into the side of a decommissioned elevator shaft. The noodles were synthetic but hot, and Kael was hungry enough not to care. "Zheng-Dai Chemical. Prometheus Biotech. Helix Systems. They own the air, the water, the data. They even own people's *memories* — you can rent happy ones for fifty credits an hour." She made a face. "Whole city runs on debt and distraction."

"Why do you stay?"

Lira looked at him. Her augmented eyes caught the neon from outside, reflecting it in prismatic shards. "Where else would I go? The Flood took the coast. The Dust took the interior. The Corps took everything else." She ate a noodle. "Besides, I'm looking for something."

"What?"

"A door."

Kael's spiral mark — the subdermal implant in this world — pulsed once. Warm. *Recognizing* something.

"What kind of door?" he asked.

"A real one. Not a Corp lock, not a digital gate. A door that goes... somewhere else." She looked at him, and for the first time, her cynical mask slipped. "I've seen them. In the old code, buried under the city's original architecture. References to a pattern — a spiral — that acts as a key. The Corps are looking for it too. They call it **Project Nexus**." She leaned forward. "And you just showed up, out of nowhere, with no ID, no history, no neural registration, asking me where you are." Her eyes contracted to pinpoints. "So I'm going to ask you once, Kael: what are you?"

Kael looked at his wrist. The implant glowed softly, displaying his heart rate, which had spiked.

"I think," he said slowly, "I'm what you're looking for."

---

Over the next several days, Lira tested him. She took him to a data den — a converted subway station where hackers jacked into the old network beneath the new one — and had him interface with a terminal. The moment Kael's fingers touched the input surface, the screen filled with the spiral pattern. Not as an image. As **code** — infinite, self-referencing, alive.

"Get him off that," the den operator snapped, pulling Kael's hand away. "He's not interfacing. He's *broadcasting*. Every Corp sensor in Sector Seven just lit up."

They ran. Through the Below, through maintenance tunnels, through a market so crowded and neon-soaked that Kael couldn't tell if he was running through a street or a fever dream. Lira moved like she had memorized every crack in every wall, and she pulled Kael through a sequence of passages that seemed to exist in the gaps between the city's official architecture.

They stopped in a dead-end corridor behind a water reclamation plant. Lira was breathing hard.

"You're not augmented," she said. "That's not code you're generating. It's coming from *you*. From your nervous system." She stared at him. "You're organic hardware. You're a living interface."

"I'm a boy," Kael said, because it was the only thing he knew for certain.

"You're a key," Lira said. "And every Corp in Shenzhen-9 is going to want to turn you."

She was right. By the time they emerged from the corridor, drones were already sweeping the sector. Black-bodied, silent, with red sensor eyes that painted everything in targeting lasers. Zheng-Dai Chemical had dispatched a retrieval team.

Lira grabbed Kael's hand and ran.

They made it to the edge of the Below — a maintenance platform overlooking the Flood, the drowned ruins of old Shenzhen, its submerged towers visible as dark shapes beneath the brown water. Behind them, the retrieval team closed in. Five figures in black tactical armor, their faces hidden behind featureless visors, moving with the synchronized precision of people whose bodies were more machine than flesh.

"Lira—" Kael started.

"The door," she said, pulling out a cracked data pad. "I found it. Right here. In the old architecture. Under the water. There's a pattern built into the foundation of the city — a spiral — and it matches the code you were broadcasting." She looked at him. "But I can't open it. I've tried everything."

Kael looked at the dark water below. The spiral on his wrist — on his *implant* — was pulsing faster now, in rhythm with his heartbeat, in rhythm with something far beneath the surface.

"I can open it," he said. And he knew it was true, the way you know your own name.

"Then open it."

Kael closed his eyes. He felt the wave gathering — not the shift-wave this time, but something else. Something that came from *within*, from the mark on his wrist, from the spiral that existed in every world he'd ever visited. He reached for it—

And the water below them **parted**.

Not like the Red Sea. Not dramatically. It just... opened. A spiral-shaped hole descended through the Flood, through the drowned towers, through layers of sediment and ruin, down and down into something that glowed with light that shouldn't exist beneath a thousand feet of dirty water.

At the bottom of the spiral, Kael saw it: a door. A real door. Wooden, iron-banded, absurdly ordinary. And carved into its surface, the spiral-crack symbol.

"Go," Lira whispered. "I can't follow. I'm not— I'm not whatever you are."

Kael looked at her. Silver hair. Augmented eyes. Spiral pendant. In every world, she was there. Different face, different name, same *soul*. Lira. The girl who was always looking for a door.

"I'll find you again," he said.

"You'd better."

He jumped.

## CHAPTER M-II: THE FLOATING KINGDOM

### *Medieval World Two: Aethyr*

---

He fell upward.

That was the first thing Kael noticed. Gravity reversed itself for three exhilarating, terrifying seconds, and then he was lying on a surface that felt like marble but was warm to the touch, like skin. Above him — or below him; orientation was meaningless here — clouds drifted in a sky so blue it looked painted.

He sat up. He was on a platform — a disc of white stone, perhaps fifty feet across, floating in open sky. Around him, other platforms floated at varying heights, connected by bridges made of light. Not metaphorical light. Actual, tangible light — beams that you could walk on, that held weight, that hummed faintly when you touched them.

And beyond the platforms: a **city in the sky**.

Towers of crystalline stone rose from platforms that defied physics, linked by cascading bridges of light and water. Waterfalls poured from the edges of platforms and dissolved into mist before they reached the clouds below. Gardens grew on nothing — trees, flowers, grass, all thriving on platforms of bare stone. And everything — *everything* — was inscribed with patterns. Spirals. The same spiral, over and over, carved into every surface, painted on every wall, woven into every tapestry.

But in this world, the spirals were **whole**. Unbroken. No crack.

Kael looked at his wrist. He was younger here — maybe fourteen. The spiral-crack mark was on his skin, but it was fainter than he'd ever seen it. Almost invisible.

"You are the one without a patron."

Kael turned. A man stood on the opposite edge of the platform. He was tall and thin, wearing robes of deep blue that seemed to shift color as the light changed. His head was shaved, and his eyes were the pale gold of a hawk's. Around his neck hung a metal disc stamped with an unbroken spiral.

"I am Brother Ashael," the man said. His voice was calm and resonant, the voice of someone who had spent a lifetime in silence and chosen each word carefully. "You are in Aethyr, the Kingdom Above the Clouds. And you are the first person in two hundred years to arrive on the Landing Disc without being called."

"Called by who?"

"By the Spiral, of course." Ashael looked at Kael with something that might have been wonder or might have been concern. "The Spiral is the source of all things in Aethyr. It is the pattern from which our world was woven. Every soul that exists here was called by the Spiral — inscribed into its geometry before birth." He paused. "But you... you are not in the Spiral. You are... beside it. Outside it." He tilted his head. "And yet you carry a fragment of it." He pointed at Kael's wrist. "A cracked fragment."

"What does that mean?"

"It means," Ashael said slowly, "that you are either a great blessing or a great catastrophe. In my experience, those are usually the same thing."

---

Aethyr was a theocracy built on geometry.

The Spiral — the same spiral that appeared in Kael's mark — was the foundation of their religion, their architecture, their magic, and their social order. Every citizen of Aethyr had a position in the Spiral, literally and metaphorically. The closer you lived to its center — the city's core, where the Great Spiral Temple hovered — the higher your status. The outer rings were residential, agricultural, industrial. The center was sacred.

Magic in Aethyr was called **Weaving**, and it was the art of manipulating the Spiral's geometry to alter reality. Weavers could fold space (creating the light-bridges), enhance growth (the floating gardens), and even slow time within certain areas. The most powerful Weavers — the Spiral Monks, of whom Ashael was one — could see the pattern in everything and adjust it like a musician tuning an instrument.

But Aethyr had a problem.

"The Spiral is weakening," Ashael told Kael, as they walked along a bridge of light that connected the Landing Disc to the outer city. Below them, clouds drifted like sheep. "For centuries, the pattern has been fraying at the edges. Platforms that once floated steadily now wobble. Bridges of light flicker. Weavers tire faster." He stopped and looked at Kael. "And recently, cracks have begun to appear in the Great Spiral itself. The monks say it is the natural cycle — all things end. The Spiral Council says it is sabotage. The people say it is the end of the world."

"What do *you* say?" Kael asked.

Ashael studied him for a long moment. "I say that a boy just arrived on the Landing Disc carrying a cracked spiral on his skin, and the last person to do that was **two hundred years ago** — a woman who tried to destroy the Great Spiral and nearly succeeded."

Kael felt a chill that had nothing to do with the wind. "What happened to her?"

"She was cast down. Through the clouds, into the world below." Ashael's golden eyes were unreadable. "We call that world the Floor. It is where everything that falls from Aethyr lands. It is where the cracked things go." He looked at Kael's wrist. "Including, perhaps, you."

---

Kael spent two weeks in Aethyr, and for the first time in any world, he felt something close to peace.

The city was beautiful — heartbreakingly, impossibly beautiful. The floating gardens bloomed with flowers that changed color with the time of day. The bridges of light sang when you walked on them, each one a different note, so that crossing the city was like playing a song with your feet. The people were kind, if distant — they treated Kael with the careful respect reserved for something that might be sacred or might be dangerous.

Ashael taught him the basics of Weaving. Kael was, to everyone's surprise, naturally gifted. When he reached for the Spiral, he could feel it — not as an abstract concept, but as a living presence, vibrating beneath the surface of reality. And when he wove, the patterns responded to him more eagerly than they did to monks who had studied for decades.

"You are not a Weaver," Ashael said one evening, watching Kael mend a cracked light-bridge with a gesture. "You are something the Spiral recognizes. Something it *wants*."

"Wants for what?"

Ashael didn't answer.

---

The answer came on the fourteenth night.

Kael woke to screaming. Not the scream of a person — the scream of the city itself. The light-bridges were flickering wildly, their song turned to discordant shrieks. Platforms were tilting. The gardens were dying, their flowers curling and blackening in seconds.

And in the sky above the Great Spiral Temple, Kael saw it: the **crack**.

Not a small crack. A chasm. A fracture in the spiral that ran from the center of the Great Spiral to its outermost ring — and it was growing. Through the crack, Kael could see the same impossible, nameless color he'd seen in his kitchen, the night everything began. The color that made his teeth ache.

The Hollow.

It was here.

"THE FLOOR IS RISING!" someone shouted, and Kael ran to the edge of the nearest platform and looked down. Through the clouds, far below, something was pushing upward. Not land — not earth. **Darkness.** A tide of absolute black that was climbing through the clouds toward the city, dissolving everything it touched.

Ashael appeared beside him, his blue robes whipping in a wind that smelled of nothing — the absolute nothing of a vacuum.

"The Hollow has found you," Ashael said. "It follows the crack. Wherever the crack is, it feeds." He grabbed Kael's arm — the one with the mark. "You must leave. Now. If it takes you, it takes the fragment you carry — and the fragment is the only thing keeping the Spiral from shattering completely."

"But the city—"

"The city is already gone, boy. We just haven't fallen yet." Ashael's golden eyes were fierce. "Go. Go to the next world. Carry the fragment. Keep it alive. The Spiral is in every world — I've seen it in the deepest meditations. If the fragment survives, the Spiral survives. If the Spiral survives, reality survives." He pushed Kael toward the edge of the platform. "Jump."

"I can't control it—"

"You don't need to control it. You need to *feel* it. Feel the fragment. Feel the crack." Ashael's voice dropped. "Feel the fear, Kael. And let it take you."

The black tide was rising. The Great Spiral was screaming. The light-bridges were dying.

Kael felt the fear — pure, crystalline, absolute — and the wave gathered beneath him.

He jumped off the edge of the world.

## CHAPTER C-II: MEMORY MERCHANTS

### *Cyberpunk World Two: The Drowned City of New Venice*

---

He woke in a bathtub.

Not a clean bathtub. A medical bathtub — one of those sensory deprivation tanks that had been retrofitted with neural cables and fluid suspension systems. The fluid was warm and opaque, the color of weak tea, and Kael was floating in it with electrodes attached to his temples.

A face appeared above him. A man, middle-aged, with the kind of expensive augmentation that made you look less human rather than more — too-smooth skin, eyes that were clearly synthetic (they glowed faintly amber), and a smile that didn't involve any muscles other than the ones around his mouth.

"Excellent," the man said. "You're awake. I'm Dr. Aizen Creel. And you've just been downloaded."

"Downloaded?" Kael sat up, fluid sluicing off him. He looked at his hands — they were his, but not his. Older. Maybe twenty-one. Fine scars traced circuit-like patterns up his forearms.

"Your consciousness," Dr. Creel said, as if explaining to a child. "You arrived as raw neural data — no body, no identification, no payment method. Fortunately for you, I have a surplus of unclaimed bodies. Unfortunately, unclaimed bodies are unclaimed for a reason." He gestured to a mirror on the wall.

Kael looked. The face looking back was gaunt, sharp-featured, with dark circles under the eyes and a look of permanent exhaustion. On the left temple, a neural port glowed — active and unregistered.

"Previous owner died of memory degradation," Creel said cheerfully. "Common affliction in New Venice. People sell too many memories and their brains simply... hollow out. This body has been vacant for six months. You're the first signal it's received." He handed Kael a towel. "Welcome to New Venice. Try not to sell anything you might need."

---

New Venice was built on the bones of old Amsterdam — literally.

When the North Sea had reclaimed the Netherlands, the survivors had done what humans always do: they'd built on top of the ruins. The new city was a marvel of engineering — a floating metropolis of interconnected platforms, artificial islands, and bridged canals that gleamed with bioluminescent algae. It was beautiful, in the way that a venomous animal is beautiful — gorgeous to look at, deadly to touch.

Because New Venice's economy ran on **memories**.

The technology had been developed by Helix Systems (a megacorp that Kael recognized from Shenzhen-9 — or rather, a version of it; each world had its own variations). The process was simple: neural extraction. A trained technician could pull a specific memory from your brain, encode it as digital data, and sell it on the open market. Rich people bought other people's memories the way they bought art — for the experience, for the novelty, for the emotional tourism of feeling something without the inconvenience of having lived it.

The poor sold their own.

First they sold the happy memories — they were worth the most. Then the interesting ones. Then the sad ones. Then the boring ones. Eventually, a person could sell so much of their interior life that they became what the street called a **Hollow** — a body still breathing, still walking, but empty. No past. No identity. No self.

Kael felt a cold spike of recognition at the word.

*Hollow.*

---

He found Lira — or rather, this world's version of her — working in a memory parlor called **The Recollection**. She was a technician: the person who actually extracted and encoded the memories. She wore a white coat over street clothes, and her hair was long and silver-blonde, pulled back in a practical ponytail. Her eyes were natural — no augmentation — but there was a quality to them, a sharpness, that was the same in every world.

"You're the downloaded body," she said, when Kael walked in. "Word travels fast on the platforms. Everyone wants to know who you are."

"I don't know who I am," Kael said. "But I know who *you* are. Or — a version of you."

Lira looked at him with the same assessing gaze he'd seen in Shenzhen-9. "That's a strange thing to say to someone you've never met."

"I've met you. In other places. Other worlds."

"Other worlds." She said it flatly. Not disbelieving — just waiting.

"I know it sounds insane. But in one of those worlds, you were looking for a door. And in another, you—" He stopped. He hadn't been to another cyberpunk world yet. But he *felt* it — the certainty that in every world, past and future, Lira was there. Searching. Building. Fighting.

"Show me," Lira said.

"Show you what?"

"Show me how you know me. Prove it's not a scam. Memory extraction works both ways — I can read what's in your head. If you're lying, I'll know. If you're not..." She looked at his temples, at the neural port. "If you're not, then you might be the most valuable thing that's ever walked into my parlor."

She sat him in the extraction chair. She placed the neural crown on his head. And she dove into his memories.

What she found there was not normal.

Memories of Greyath and the Bonewall. Memories of Aethyr and the floating kingdom. Memories of Shenzhen-9 and the submerged spiral door. Memories of his mother's empty house, his kitchen, the knife that bent, the peanut butter sandwich that had started everything. And beneath all of them, woven through every memory like a thread through fabric, the **spiral**. Cracked. Living. *Waiting*.

Lira pulled out of the extraction. Her hands were shaking.

"You're not human," she whispered.

"I'm a boy," Kael said, for the second time in two worlds.

"You're a *pattern*. You're a living pattern. Your consciousness isn't generated by your brain — it's... *projected* through it. Like light through a lens." She stared at him. "And that pattern — the spiral — it's the same one."

"The same one as what?"

Lira walked to the back of the parlor and opened a safe. From it, she removed a data crystal — old tech, almost antique. She slotted it into a display unit, and a three-dimensional image flickered to life.

It was a spiral. A massive, intricate spiral, constructed from memory data — thousands of individual memories, all from different people, all containing the same recurring image. The spiral with a crack.

"I've been collecting these for years," Lira said. "Fragments of the same image, pulled from hundreds of different minds. Most people don't even know they have it — it's buried so deep it's almost subconscious. But it's in *everyone*, Kael. Every person in New Venice. Every person I've ever extracted." She turned to face him. "You're the source. You — or whatever you *really* are — is embedded in everyone's mind. Like a... like a default setting. Like a foundation."

"Like a pattern," Kael said.

"Like a **nexus**."

---

They were interrupted by the sound of breaking glass and the thud of boots.

Three figures entered the parlor. They wore the matte-black armor of Helix Systems security, and their faces were hidden behind featureless visors that made Kael think, with a sick lurch, of the Ashen.

"Kael Ashworth," the lead figure said. The voice was synthesized, androgynous, and absolutely devoid of emotion. "You are a copyright violation. Your neural pattern matches the Helix Systems proprietary template Nexus-7, registered in 2084. You will be coming with us for immediate decompilation."

"He's not a copyright," Lira said, stepping between Kael and the security team. "He's a person."

"He is code," the figure replied. "And code belongs to whoever wrote it."

Kael stood. The spiral on his wrist — a neural implant in this world, a birthmark in the last one, always the same symbol — was burning. Not with heat. With *recognition*. The same feeling he'd had in Greyath when he'd seen the spiral on the ash creature's back. The same feeling he'd had in Aethyr when the Hollow had risen through the clouds.

He reached for the feeling. Let it crystallize. Let it sharpen into something pure and decisive.

**Rage.**

The wave gathered.

The world folded.

The last thing Kael saw in New Venice was Lira's face — eyes wide, mouth open, one hand reaching toward him — and the spiral, burning in the air between them, cracking the world open like an egg.

## CHAPTER M-III: THE THORN KINGDOM

### *Medieval World Three: Briar*

---

Kael arrived in a world at war with its own forest.

He was fifteen here, dressed in a page's rough linen, and he was running. Not from something — *through* something. The forest was alive, and it was hostile. Vines whipped at his face like ropes wielded by invisible hands. Roots rose from the ground to catch his ankles. Trees leaned into his path, their branches reaching for him with the deliberate malice of limbs.

The sky above the canopy was green. Not the green of a normal sky at sunset — the green of *leaves*. The forest was so thick that the sky had been replaced by a ceiling of foliage, and the only light came from luminescent fungi that clung to the tree trunks in patterns that looked suspiciously deliberate.

He burst out of the tree line and into a clearing, where a fortress made of living wood rose from the earth like a wound. The walls were tree trunks, grown together and reinforced with iron bands. The towers were massive thorned vines, coiled and woven into platforms. And the gate — the gate was a mouth, literally shaped like one, with wooden teeth and a tongue-like drawbridge.

"GET INSIDE, BOY!"

A soldier — barely more than a boy himself, maybe eighteen, wearing armor made of boiled leather and bark — grabbed Kael's arm and hauled him through the gate-mouth. Behind them, the forest surged, vines launching themselves at the closing gate like grappling hooks.

"What—" Kael gasped.

"The Briar," the soldier said, panting. "It's pushing again. It's been pushing for three days. We lose another foot of wall every hour." He looked at Kael with the hollow eyes of someone running on adrenaline and nothing else. "You from one of the outer villages?"

"I'm not from anywhere near here."

"Good. Nobody from the outer villages is alive anymore." He clearly considered this a comforting statement.

---

Briar was a kingdom enclosed.

In this world, the land was divided into two zones: the **Held Lands** — the cleared territory surrounding each human fortress, maintained through constant warfare against the forest — and the **Deep Briar**, the endless, sentient, malevolent forest that covered the rest of the world and was slowly, methodically, *hungrily* encroaching on human territory.

Nobody knew what had made the Briar sentient. The oldest records — kept in the fortress-library of Thornwall, the capital — told of a time before the forest had moved, when it had been ordinary woodland. But something had changed. Something had *woken it up*. And ever since, humanity had been in retreat.

Kael was assigned to the kitchens of Thornwall, which suited him fine. The cook — a vast, red-faced woman named Maren — was the first genuinely kind person he'd met in any world who wasn't going to die in the next fifteen minutes. She fed him, gave him a cot, and asked no questions about where he'd come from.

But Kael couldn't stay in the kitchens. The spiral mark on his wrist — in this world, it was a brand, a burn scar, as if someone had pressed a hot iron into his skin — led him deeper into the fortress. It grew warmer as he descended into the lower levels, past the dungeons, past the store-rooms, into chambers that hadn't been opened in centuries.

At the bottom of Thornwall, in a room that predated the fortress by millennia, Kael found it: a **tree**.

Not a normal tree. Not even a sentient Briar-tree. Something older. Something that made the Briar look like a houseplant. Its trunk was black and smooth as obsidian, and its roots extended through the floor into the earth below. Its branches pushed through the ceiling above, and Kael realized with a start that this tree was the *foundation* of Thornwall — the fortress had been built around it, not the other way around.

And carved into its trunk, so old the grooves had nearly grown over: the **spiral-crack** symbol.

Kael pressed his branded wrist against it.

The tree *spoke*.

Not in words. In images. In feelings. Kael saw the world as the tree saw it: from below, from the roots, from a perspective of patience so deep it made centuries feel like heartbeats. He saw the Briar, not as an enemy, but as a *symptom* — the forest's desperate, mindless attempt to reach the tree, to touch it, to connect with the pattern it carried. The Briar was not malicious. It was *drawn*. Like iron to a magnet. Like moths to a flame. The spiral-crack in the tree emitted a frequency that plants could feel and humans couldn't, and the forest had spent thousands of years trying to reach its source.

And the crack was growing.

"What are you?" Kael whispered to the tree.

The tree showed him. The tree showed him everything.

It showed him the **Pattern** — the original, unbroken spiral that had been the template for all reality. It showed him the moment the crack had appeared. It showed him the crack spreading — through the Pattern, through reality, through every world. It showed him the Hollow — the nothing that fed on the crack, that grew with every fracture, that would consume everything if the crack wasn't healed.

And it showed him himself. Kael Ashworth. Standing at the center of the Pattern, at the moment of the crack, a fragment of the original spiral embedded in his soul.

*You are the seed*, the tree said, in its wordless, root-deep language. *You are what the Pattern grew to heal itself. Every world you visit, you leave a fragment behind. A piece of the spiral, buried in the reality of that place. Growing. Slowly. The way a seed grows in the dark.*

"How do I heal it?" Kael asked.

*You gather the fragments. You carry them. And when you have enough — when you have touched enough worlds, left enough pieces behind — you return to the center. To the crack. And you **become** the new Pattern.*

"What happens to me?"

The tree didn't answer. Trees are patient. Trees know that some questions can only be answered by living them.

---

Kael emerged from the lower chambers to find Thornwall under full assault. The Briar had broken through the outer wall. Vines the size of ship masts were coiling around the fortress towers, crushing stone. The soldier who'd pulled Kael inside lay in the courtyard, pinned by a thorn as long as a spear.

Maren was dragging children toward the inner keep, her massive arms scooping up two at a time.

Kael felt the shift building — the wave beneath his feet. He could leave. He could fold away and escape to another world, leaving Thornwall to its fate.

He could. He didn't want to.

He pressed his hand against the floor — against the roots of the ancient tree beneath — and he reached for the spiral. Not the cracked one on his wrist, but the original one, the whole one, the pattern that the tree had shown him. He reached for it the way Ashael had taught him in Aethyr. He reached for it the way he'd reached for the submerged door in Shenzhen-9.

And the Briar **stopped**.

Every vine, every branch, every root — frozen. The forest went still. Not dead. *Listening*. The way a congregation falls silent when the preacher steps to the pulpit.

Kael could feel the spiral in the roots. His fragment. The piece he'd left behind by touching the ancient tree. It was already growing — a tiny, fragile thread of pattern winding through the root system, spreading through the earth, reaching toward the Briar like an open hand.

"Go back," Kael said, his voice barely a whisper but somehow carrying through every root and vine in the forest. "I'll fix it. I'll fix the crack. But I need time."

The Briar retreated.

Not all at once. Reluctantly. The way a dog backs away from food it's been told not to touch. But it retreated, uncoiling from the towers, releasing the walls, pulling back to the edge of the Held Lands.

Maren stared at Kael across the courtyard, a child under each arm, her face unreadable.

"The kitchen needs peeling," she said finally. "Get to work."

Kael almost laughed. But the wave was building, and he couldn't stop it. He had to go. More worlds to touch. More fragments to leave.

"I'll come back," he said.

"You'd better," Maren said. "I don't train peeling boys lightly."

The world folded.

## CHAPTER C-III: ORBITAL GRAVEYARD

### *Cyberpunk World Three: Station Erebus*

---

Space was silent. Kael had always known this, intellectually, but experiencing it — floating in a maintenance corridor with no sound but his own breathing and the distant thrum of recyclers — made it viscerally real.

He was on a space station. He'd never been on a space station before.

Station Erebus was old. Kael could feel it in the way the metal groaned, in the corrosion on the bulkheads, in the flickering emergency lighting that cast everything in shades of amber and shadow. He was twenty in this world, wearing a faded jumpsuit with a patch on the shoulder that read **EREBUS SALVAGE — SECTOR 7**.

Erebus orbited a dead star — a white dwarf that cast everything in pale, ghostly light. The station had been built as a research outpost, then abandoned when the star died, then repopulated by the desperate, the criminal, and the insane, who turned it into a salvage hub. Ships that wandered too close to the dead star's gravity well got trapped in orbit, and Erebus's inhabitants harvested them for parts, cargo, and occasionally survivors.

Kael was a salvager.

He knew this the way he knew things in every world — not through memory, but through the body's muscle and habit. His hands knew how to cut hull plating. His eyes knew how to spot a pressure breach. His fingers knew the interface codes for ship systems that hadn't been manufactured in a century.

The spiral-crack mark on his wrist was a **tattoo** in this world — blue ink, professional quality, clearly deliberate.

He found Lira in the station's central hub, which served as a market, a bar, and a morgue, depending on which level you were on. She was a **data archaeologist** — someone who specialized in extracting information from dead ships' computers. Her silver hair was tied back with a cord, and her eyes were unaugmented but sharp as scalpels. She wore a hazard suit stripped to the waist, the arms tied around her hips, and her forearms were covered in the tiny cuts that came from handling degraded circuitry.

"You're the new salvager from Sector 7," she said, not looking up from the data pad she was repairing. "Word is you arrived on a ship that wasn't there the day before. Just... appeared in orbit."

"That sounds right," Kael said.

"Now why would a ship appear out of nowhere in orbit around a dead star?"

"I don't know. But I have a feeling you're going to tell me it has something to do with a spiral."

Lira looked up. Her eyes locked on his. "Who told you?"

"Nobody. It's... a long story. Involving you, actually. Multiple versions of you."

She set down the data pad. "Sit down. Start from the beginning."

---

Lira had found the spiral in the data of every dead ship she'd ever excavated.

It was buried in the code — not as a file or a program, but as a *pattern*. A mathematical structure that repeated across every system, every architecture, every language. It was in the navigation code, the life support algorithms, the entertainment databases. It was everywhere, and it was always the same: a self-referencing spiral with a fault line running through it.

"I thought it was a virus," Lira said. "A really old one. Maybe the oldest. But viruses propagate to cause harm. This pattern doesn't do anything. It just... *is*. Like a signature. Like a watermark."

She showed him the data. Kael recognized it immediately — not the code itself, but the *shape* of it. It was the same pattern he'd seen in Shenzhen-9's old architecture, the same geometry that underlay Aethyr's Weaving, the same spiral carved into the ancient tree in Briar.

"It's not a virus," Kael said. "It's a foundation. It's what reality is built on."

"You sound like a religious nut."

"I sound like someone who's seen it in four different worlds."

Lira studied him. "Prove it."

Kael held out his wrist. The blue spiral tattoo seemed to darken as she looked at it. He could feel the pattern inside him — the fragment, the seed, whatever the ancient tree had called it. He reached for it, let it pulse once, and the data pad on the table between them lit up.

Every scrap of spiral-coded data on the station converged on the pad's screen, arranging itself into a three-dimensional model. A spiral. A crack. And something else — something Kael hadn't seen before.

A **point of light** at the center of the crack.

"That's new," Lira breathed. "That's not in any of the data I've extracted. That's..." She leaned closer. "That's a signal. An active signal. Coming from inside the crack."

"Inside the crack in the pattern," Kael said. "Not inside the data."

"Where, then?"

Kael looked out the viewport, past the station's hull, to the dead star below. Its light was pale and cold and ancient. And there, visible only when he looked *through* the spiral fragment inside him, he saw it: a **door**. Floating in orbit around the dead star. A wooden, iron-banded door, identical to the one beneath Shenzhen-9's Flood.

"That's where I need to go," Kael said.

"That's a door in space," Lira said reasonably.

"I know."

"You can't open a door in space."

"I think I can."

---

He couldn't. Not alone.

Getting to the door required a ship, and every ship in Erebus's salvage yard was dead. But Lira — brilliant, pragmatic, impossible Lira — had spent years rebuilding a single vessel from salvaged parts. It was ugly, unreliable, and its navigation system was held together with solder and prayer, but it could fly.

They flew toward the dead star, toward the door that only Kael could see.

"It's not on any sensor," Lira said, checking her instruments for the fiftieth time. "No mass, no energy signature, no electromagnetic emission. It's not *there*, Kael. Not physically."

"It's there," Kael said. "Just not in a way your instruments can measure."

"What's it made of?"

"Pattern. The same pattern that's in your data. The same pattern that's in my wrist. The same pattern that's in the roots of every world."

Lira was quiet for a moment. "You're not afraid," she observed.

"I'm terrified," Kael said. "I'm just getting used to being terrified."

They reached the door. Through the cockpit viewport, it looked exactly as Kael had described: wooden, iron-banded, impossible. It hung in the vacuum of space like a painting on an invisible wall.

Lira cut the engines. The ship drifted toward the door, silent except for the hum of the life support.

"If you open that," Lira said, "and something comes through—"

"Something will come through," Kael said. "But it's not coming *through*. It's already *here*." He pointed at the dead star. "It's been here all along. The crack. The Hollow. It's inside everything. I'm just... opening a door to see it clearly."

He pressed his hand against the cockpit glass. The spiral fragment in his wrist pulsed.

The door opened.

Behind it was not a room or another world. It was the **crack** itself — the fracture in the pattern, seen from the inside. And looking into it, Kael finally understood what the Hollow was.

It wasn't a creature. It wasn't an entity. It was **absence**. The nothing that existed where the pattern had been broken. And it was *growing* — fed by every crack in every world, every fracture in every reality, slowly expanding, slowly consuming, because a pattern with a crack in it will always, eventually, come apart.

The Hollow wasn't evil. It was simply what remained when the pattern was gone.

*Kael.*

The voice came from everywhere and nowhere. It was the voice of the tree in Briar, the voice of the spiral code in Shenzhen-9, the voice of the ancient Pattern itself — broken but not dead, fractured but still speaking.

*Not yet. You are not ready. More worlds. More fragments. More seeds.*

"I'm trying," Kael whispered.

*Try faster. The crack grows. The Hollow feeds. And in every world, the walls are falling.*

The door closed. The ship drifted. Lira stared at Kael with eyes that held more questions than a single conversation could answer.

"Take me back to Erebus," Kael said. "I have to go to another world."

"I know," Lira said. And somehow, she did.

The wave gathered. The world folded. Space creased like paper, and Kael fell through the crack.

## CHAPTER M-IV: THE TOURNAMENT OF ASH AND GOLD

### *Medieval World Four: The Realm of Caldera*

---

He arrived to the sound of trumpets.

Not the mournful horns of Greyath or the living song of Aethyr — these were triumphant, brazen, obnoxious trumpets, the kind that announced something grand and slightly ridiculous.

Kael was standing at the edge of a tournament ground. He was sixteen, dressed in fine clothes he was certain he hadn't earned — a doublet of deep red and gold, fitted trousers, soft leather boots, and a cape that was frankly excessive. A circlet sat on his head. A circlet.

"My lord!" A squire — an actual squire, a boy of maybe twelve with a face full of freckles and enthusiasm — ran up to him. "The Herald has called your name! You're next in the lists!"

"Next for what?"

"The Grand Tournament, my lord! For the throne of Caldera!" The squire looked at him with the earnest alarm of someone whose job depended on another person's competence. "You... *are* Sir Kael of the Crossing, aren't you?"

"I'm Kael," he said carefully. "And I'm from a crossing, I suppose, if you mean the kind between worlds."

"Perfect! The Herald is waiting! Your horse is ready! Your lance is sharpened! Your armor is— oh. Oh no." The squire looked at Kael's unarmored body with dawning horror. "My lord, where is your armor?"

---

The Realm of Caldera was ruled by **tournament**.

Not metaphorically. Literally. The kingdom had no hereditary monarchy, no elected government, no ruling council. Instead, every five years, a Grand Tournament was held, and the winner became **Caldera's sovereign** for the next five years. The tournament was open to anyone — noble or commoner, soldier or farmer, man or woman — and the competitions ranged from jousting to swordplay to archery to strategic board games to, in one memorable year, competitive poetry.

It was, Kael realized, the most absurd system of government he'd ever encountered. It was also, somehow, the most stable. Caldera had been ruled this way for three hundred years, and in that time, it had never fought a war with a neighboring kingdom (it had no neighboring kingdoms; the realm was an island continent), never suffered a famine (the land was impossibly fertile), and never had a sovereign who was genuinely terrible (the tournament format, surprisingly, filtered out both cowards and sadists, who tended to lose early).

Kael was entered in the tournament as "Sir Kael of the Crossing," a name he hadn't chosen but apparently couldn't change. His squire, Tomas, was deeply loyal and deeply concerned about his lord's apparent amnesia.

"You hit your head during practice yesterday," Tomas explained, as he helped Kael into armor that weighed approximately as much as a small car. "The healer said your memories would return. I didn't think they'd be *this* gone."

"Just walk me through the basics," Kael said.

Tomas did. The basics were: (1) sit on horse, (2) hold lance, (3) don't die.

Kael had never joustied in his life. But he'd fought Ashen soldiers in Greyath, he'd faced sentient forests in Briar, and he'd opened a door in the vacuum of space. How hard could riding a horse at another armed man be?

*Very hard*, it turned out.

---

He won his first match by accident. His opponent — a massive knight in black armor who went by "The Harrow" — charged with the confidence of someone who had won forty consecutive bouts. Kael, who had barely managed to stay on his horse during the approach, dropped his lance at exactly the right moment, tripping The Harrow's horse and sending the giant knight tumbling into the dirt.

"Lucky!" The Harrow bellowed, being helped up by his squires. "That was luck!"

"The best kind," Kael said, and the crowd roared.

He won his second match by remembering Ashael's Weaving lessons. Not that he could Weave in this world — magic didn't work the same way here — but the *principle* was the same: see the pattern, adjust the variables. In a sword fight, that meant reading your opponent's stance, anticipating their strikes, and moving to where they weren't. His opponent, a skilled swordswoman named Dame Vera, was better than him in every technical sense. But Kael had fought across multiple realities, and his reflexes were sharpened by experiences that Dame Vera couldn't imagine.

"You fight like someone who expects the world to fold around him," she said afterward, graciously accepting defeat.

"You have no idea," Kael said.

He won his third match — the semi-final — by forfeit. His opponent, a young lord named Rennick, took one look at Kael's face across the tournament ground and withdrew.

"I've seen you in a dream," Rennick said, when Kael asked why. "You were standing in a crack in the sky, and you were holding something together — a pattern, a shape — and it was burning you alive." He shook his head. "I don't want to fight someone who's already burning."

---

The final match was not a fight.

The current sovereign of Caldera — a woman named Queen Morvaine, who had won the last three tournaments and ruled for fifteen years — did not compete in the tournament in the traditional sense. Instead, the finalist faced her in a **challenge of her choosing**, and she had never chosen the same challenge twice.

For Kael, she chose: "Tell me a true story."

"A true story?"

"A story that is true in every world. Not just this one."

Kael sat across from her in the throne room. The court watched. Tomas fidgeted. And Kael told her a story.

He told her about Greyath, and the Bonewall, and Sera Vane. He told her about Aethyr, and the floating kingdom, and Brother Ashael. He told her about Shenzhen-9, and Lira, and the submerged door. He told her about Briar, and the ancient tree, and the seed. He told her about Station Erebus, and the dead star, and the crack in the pattern.

He told her everything.

When he finished, Queen Morvaine was silent for a long time.

"That is a true story," she said. "I don't know how I know, but I do. Every word of it is true — not just in this world, but in all of them." She removed her crown. "And a person who carries that much truth deserves a throne more than I do."

"I don't want a throne," Kael said.

"No one who deserves one ever does." She placed the crown on the table between them. "But you need it. You need authority, resources, a power base. Because whatever is coming — whatever that crack in the pattern is — you're going to need more than a lance and a lucky disposition to face it."

She was right. Kael knew she was right. But the wave was already building, and the world was already folding, and he didn't have time to be a king.

"Tomas," Kael said, grabbing his squire's shoulder as reality began to crease. "You're in charge. Don't let anyone change the tournament rules. And if a girl with silver hair shows up — help her. Whatever she needs."

"My lord, where are you going?"

Kael smiled. "To be a seed."

## CHAPTER C-IV: THE DESERT OF MIRRORS

### *Cyberpunk World Four: The Ashlands*

---

Heat hit him first. Then light. Then sand.

Kael was lying on his back in a desert that stretched to the horizon in every direction. The sky was white — not cloudy white, but *blinding* white, as if the sun had expanded to fill the entire sky. The sand was not sand but **glass** — fine, crystalline grains that crunched under his fingers and reflected the white sky in a million tiny mirrors.

He was eighteen in this world, shirtless, sunburned, and cybernetically enhanced in ways he discovered only gradually. His left arm from the elbow down was mechanical — not a crude prosthetic but a sleek, chrome-and-carbon-fiber limb with built-in tools: a cutting laser in the index finger, a data port in the palm, and a series of small compartments hidden beneath synthetic skin panels. His eyes had been augmented with telescopic lenses that clicked and whirred as they focused.

And on his chrome forearm, etched into the metal itself: the spiral-crack.

A vehicle crested the nearest dune and came toward him. It was a sand-rail — a stripped-down, weaponized dune buggy built from salvaged tech and held together with welding skill and optimism. The driver was a woman in her thirties, weathered and scarred, with a mechanical jaw that clicked when she spoke.

"Got a live one," she said into a radio. "Sector Twelve. Looks military-grade augmentation." She looked down at Kael. "You Corp or Nomad?"

"Neither," Kael said. "I'm... lost."

"Lost in the Ashlands. That's a new one." She extended a hand. "Name's Jes. Get in. Sun'll cook your brain in about twenty minutes if you're not under cover."

---

The Ashlands were the remains of a civilization that had incinerated itself.

In this world, a global conflict — the **Resource Wars** — had ended with the deployment of atmospheric weapons that had vitrified the planet's surface. The sand beneath Kael's feet was the Earth itself, melted and reformed into a sea of glass. The sky was white because the atmosphere was still saturated with reflective particles — a deliberate geoengineering attempt to cool the planet that had worked *too well*, plunging the world into a permanent, blinding daylight.

The survivors had divided into two factions: the **Corps** (who had retreated into underground arcologies and continued to wage proxy wars through automated drone armies) and the **Nomads** (who had stayed on the surface and adapted, building a culture of scavenging, survival, and radical self-reliance).

Jes was a Nomad. Her clan — the Mirror Clan — roamed the Ashlands in a convoy of modified vehicles, salvaging pre-War technology from the glass dunes and trading it with other clans.
