# A Detailed History of Mount Gretna, Pennsylvania

---

## I. Founding and Origins

The story of Mount Gretna begins not with a town charter or a colonial land grant, but with iron ore and a railroad. In 1740, Peter Grubb established an iron furnace among the ore-rich hills of Cornwall, Pennsylvania, taking advantage of abundant hardwood forests and limestone. This furnace, soon joined by others at Colebrook and Brickerville along with a complex of mills and forges, formed the industrial backbone of the region. By 1798, these operations had been consolidated under the ownership of the Coleman family, whose patriarch, Robert Coleman, would become one of Pennsylvania's most prominent industrialists [3][4].

Nearly a century later, Coleman's descendant, **Robert H. Coleman**, extended the **Cornwall and Lebanon Railroad (C&LRR)** in 1883, building a 17.5-mile line from Cornwall to Conewago. A station was placed in what was then an unnamed mountain wilderness in Lebanon County. **Hugh Maxwell**, a member of the C&L Railroad committee tasked with siting stations, credited his wife with naming the area **"Gretna"** — likely after Gretna Green, the famous Scottish border village [4].

In **May 1884**, Coleman purchased 6.5 acres of mountain land for $100 per acre and established **Mount Gretna Park**. The *Lebanon Daily News* described Coleman's transformation of a "howling wilderness" into "a paradise for visitors" [4]. The park officially opened on **June 19, 1884**, featuring a dance floor for 128 dancers, a restaurant, and spring water captured in stone cisterns. Within its first season, between **40,000 and 50,000 visitors** arrived, many of them urban dwellers seeking respite from city pollution and heat [4].

Robert Coleman's personal life left its mark as well. His first wife, Lilly, died while they were in Paris in May 1880. According to the Mount Gretna Area Historical Society, Coleman ordered work on his first mansion stopped on May 30th and directed that it be demolished without a trace. He then took direct charge of his estate's affairs, a determination that ultimately shaped Mount Gretna's development [3].

## II. The Unique Subdivisions of Mount Gretna

Unlike most American towns, Mount Gretna was never incorporated as a single municipality. Instead, it evolved as a constellation of distinct subdivisions and communities, each with its own governance, character, and purpose. The major subdivisions include:

### Mount Gretna Park (1884)
The original recreational development established by Robert Coleman along the Cornwall & Lebanon Railroad. This was the commercial and entertainment heart of the community, featuring dining halls, dancing platforms, a roller-skating pavilion, bowling alley, shooting gallery, and Lake Conewago for boating. By 1890, the park boasted **16 large dancing and dining platforms** and an auditorium seating **3,500 to 4,000 people** [4].

### The Pennsylvania Chautauqua (1891)
In 1891, the **Pennsylvania Chautauqua** selected Mount Gretna as the site for its yearly educational and arts assembly, establishing a community grounded in the national Chautauqua movement that blended adult education, the arts, religion, and cultural enrichment. This subdivision became a community of charming summer cottages surrounding a central auditorium and program grounds, and it remains one of the most distinctive cultural enclaves in Pennsylvania [3][4].

### The Mount Gretna Campmeeting (1892)
In 1892, the **Mount Gretna Campmeeting Association** held its first religious campmeeting in the park, establishing another distinct community organized around faith and spiritual retreat. The Campmeeting's iconic **tabernacle**, constructed in **1899**, still stands today as a focal point for worship and community gatherings [1][4].

### Timber Hills and Other Cottage Communities
Over time, additional cottage communities and residential enclaves developed, including areas known as **Timber Hills** and other neighborhood clusters. The Mount Gretna Area Historical Society actively documents these through its **"Cottage Histories"** program (originally titled "If Cottages Could Talk"), which encourages property owners to research and share the histories of their summer homes [3].

## III. Cultural History

Mount Gretna's cultural identity was shaped by several powerful forces: the Chautauqua movement, the culinary arts, the performing arts, and the seasonal rhythm of summer life.

### The Chautauqua Movement
The Pennsylvania Chautauqua brought nationally known speakers, educators, musicians, and performers to Mount Gretna each summer. Modeled after the original Chautauqua Institution in western New York, it provided a program of lectures, concerts, and classes that made Mount Gretna a hub of intellectual and cultural life in central Pennsylvania [3][4].

### Sarah Tyson Rorer and the Rorer Hall of Cookery
One of Mount Gretna's most notable cultural figures was **Sarah Tyson Rorer** (1849–1937), an early resident of Mount Gretna and Colebrook. Described as an "early Martha Stewart," "America's first dietitian," and a "pioneer of domestic science," Rorer was an internationally known figure who exhibited at two World Fairs and pioneered Home Economics and Family and Consumer Science education in schools. Her work was closely tied to the Mount Gretna Chautauqua, where the **Rorer Hall of Cookery** stood as a testament to the movement's commitment to practical education [3].

### The Gretna Theatre
Live theater became a cornerstone of Mount Gretna's cultural offerings. The **Gretna Theatre**, a summer stock playhouse, entertained generations of visitors with professional theatrical productions. Along with concerts and art shows, it helped establish Mount Gretna as a destination for the performing arts as well as natural beauty [4].

### The Mount Gretna Area Historical Society
In **2004**, community historians **Jack Bitner** and **Fred Buch** founded the **Mount Gretna Area Historical Society** to safeguard the community's unique heritage. The society maintains a museum in the heart of the Pennsylvania Chautauqua — the **Anne Brossman Sweigart Center for History** — housing artifacts, oral history recordings, and a 15-minute virtual museum tour. The society has drawn membership from across Pennsylvania and as far as California, Texas, Florida, and New England [3].

## IV. Religious History

Religion has been intertwined with Mount Gretna's identity from the community's earliest organized years.

### The Campmeeting Association (1892–Present)
The Mount Gretna Campmeeting Association was established in 1892, organizing annual religious campmeetings that drew faithful from across the region. These campmeetings were part of a broader 19th-century Protestant tradition of outdoor revival meetings and spiritual retreats. The **tabernacle**, built in **1899**, served as the architectural and spiritual centerpiece of the Campmeeting community and remains standing today [1][4].

### Camp Mount Gretna (1949–1970)
For more than two decades, **Camp Mount Gretna** operated as a Christian summer camp for thousands of youngsters. Its main building, **Otterbein Lodge**, housed the dining room where campers ate meals with their counselors. Female campers resided in **Hershey Hall**, the former Kauffman mansion. The camp repurposed existing Mount Gretna infrastructure: the former **Mellinger's Store** was converted into a craft area and office space, with its second floor providing housing for camp employees [1].

Spiritual activities centered around the Campmeeting's tabernacle. **Friday night campfires** held on **"The Island"** behind Otterbein Lodge were the spiritual highpoint of each session. The camp's theme song captured its ethos:

> *"Camp Mount Gretna, we all love you, with our heart and soul and mind. You inspired and led us upward in Christian love divine."* [1]

For recreation, campers regularly hiked to the tower atop **Governor Dick Mountain** and dressed in their "Sunday best" on the final day of camp [1].

## V. Military History

Mount Gretna's military connections run deep. In **June 1885**, just one year after the park's founding, **120 acres were cleared** for the **National Guard of Pennsylvania's annual encampment**. This military training ground operated for **five decades**, bringing thousands of soldiers to the mountain each summer for drills, maneuvers, and training exercises [3][4].

The Mount Gretna Area Historical Society acknowledges this legacy through its direct association with the **28th Infantry Division Association**, reflecting the area's longstanding connection to the Pennsylvania National Guard. The military encampment was one of the earliest and most significant institutional uses of the Mount Gretna landscape, predating both the Chautauqua and the Campmeeting [3].

## VI. Summer Recreation and Attractions

### The Early Boom (1884–1900)
Mount Gretna's appeal as a summer destination was immediate and explosive. After its 1884 opening, the park rapidly expanded:

- **1884**: Dance floor, restaurant, spring water in stone cisterns
- **1885**: Roller-skating pavilion, dining hall, shooting gallery, bowling alley, and **Lake Conewago** for boating
- **1889**: A **4-mile narrow-gauge railway** completed, carrying passengers up **Governor Dick** mountain
- **1890**: 16 large dancing and dining platforms; an auditorium seating 3,500–4,000
- **1890–1916**: The **Mount Gretna Farmer's Encampment**, an agricultural and political fair, operated out of what is now the **historic Mount Gretna Roller Rink** [4]

### The Jigger Shop (1895–Present)
Perhaps no single business embodies Mount Gretna's history more than **The Jigger Shop**, founded in **1895** at 202 Gettysburg Avenue. The shop's signature item, **The Jigger** sundae, was created in the early 1900s when two girls attending a Mount Gretna campmeeting asked a waitress to make them "something different." The girls returned to order "one of those jiggers" — slang for "thingamajig" — and the shop's then-owners, the **Kettering sisters**, officially named their business The Jigger Shop by the 1920s [5][6].

The famous Jigger sundae features **2½ scoops of buttery French vanilla ice cream**, chocolate or butterscotch syrup, whipped marshmallow, and secret-recipe **Jigger nuts** [6]. Over the decades, the menu expanded to include soups, salads, burgers, black bean burgers, Eastern Shore crab cakes, specialty flambéed sundaes, a Fireside S'mores Sundae, and freshly squeezed orangeade, limeade, and lemonade [5][6].

**Chuck and Charlotte Allwein** purchased the establishment in **1963**, and their son **Drew** and wife **Linda** ran it for 22 years. In **2018**, after more than 50 years under the Allwein family, the shop was sold to the **Seyfert family** — three couples (Paul and Beth Seyfert, Leon Seyfert and his wife, and Kyle and Kortni Seyfert) who collectively brought 35 years of experience working at the shop [5][6].

The Jigger Shop remains open seasonally from May through September, drawing visitors from Lebanon, Lancaster, Dauphin, and York counties and beyond. As Drew Allwein once noted: "Couples come for their anniversaries. It's just that kind of place" [6].

### Modern Attractions
Today, Mount Gretna continues to thrive as a summer destination with:
- **Mt. Gretna Lake** for swimming, canoeing, and kayaking
- **Rails-to-Trails** system for hiking and biking
- **Governor Dick Environmental Center** and observation tower
- **State game lands** for hiking and nature observation
- The historic **Mount Gretna Roller Rink**
- **Art shows and craft fairs** throughout the season
- Live concerts and performances at the **Gretna Theatre**
- Walking tours of distinctive **arts-and-crafts and gingerbread cottages** [4][5][6]

Over its nearly 140-year history, Mount Gretna has hosted a variety of seasonal and year-round businesses:

## VIII. Legacy

From a railroad executive's vision in 1884 to a beloved cultural and recreational landmark, Mount Gretna reflects the broader arc of American leisure history. What began as a simple picnic ground at the end of a rail line evolved into a multifaceted community encompassing religious devotion, military service, educational aspiration, culinary innovation, and the simple pleasures of summer. Its unique structure — an unincorporated patchwork of distinct communities sharing a common landscape and heritage — makes it unlike any other place in Pennsylvania.

As the Mount Gretna Area Historical Society observes, the town's heritage shows "how a little town reflected the growth of the nation as a whole from the iron boom to military installments, to a thirst for culture and ideas to a spiritual awakening" [3].
