## SECTION 1: THE THREAT LANDSCAPE — WHO IS ATTACKING AIRLINES AND WHY

### 1.1 The Scale of the Problem

The aviation sector experienced at least **18 publicly confirmed cyber attacks in 2024 alone**, impacting aircraft manufacturers, airlines, airports, and air traffic control systems. Critically, industry researchers note that this represents only a fraction of actual incidents, as many companies suppress or minimize public reporting.

Global airline IT spending reached a record estimated **$37 billion in 2024** (SITA Air Transport IT Insights), yet modernization gaps persist. The U.S. Government Accountability Office (GAO) documented **34 IT incidents** over three years across 12 airlines, with **85% causing flight delays or cancellations**. The industry's digital transformation — while necessary — is outpacing its security maturation.

### 1.2 Who Are the Threat Actors?

Airlines face a three-front cyber war:

- **Ransomware Cartels:** Organized criminal enterprises that encrypt airline systems and demand payment. Airlines are prime targets because operational downtime is catastrophically expensive — as one industry CEO noted, "When assets can't operate, airlines lose money very quickly and are incentivized to pay a ransom," yielding seven- to eight-figure payouts.
- **Nation-State Actors:** Advanced Persistent Threat (APT) groups, such as the suspected Russian group APT28 that targeted German air traffic control (DFS) in August 2024. These actors seek to map critical infrastructure, pre-position for future conflict, and test capabilities.
- **Hacktivists:** Politically motivated groups, including pro-Russian collectives that launched coordinated DDoS attacks against Copenhagen Airport (February 2024) and Milan airports (December 2024).

### 1.3 Common Attack Methods

| Attack Vector | How It Works | Aviation Impact |
|---|---|---|
| **Ransomware** | Malware encrypts systems; payment demanded for decryption keys | Grounds operations, disrupts baggage, check-in, and crew scheduling |
| **DDoS (Distributed Denial of Service)** | Floods servers with traffic until they crash | Disables booking, check-in, and customer-facing websites |
| **Supply Chain Compromise** | Attacker infiltrates a trusted vendor to access airline systems | Massive blast radius — one vendor compromise affects dozens of airlines |
| **Phishing & Social Engineering** | Deceptive emails/messages trick employees into revealing credentials | Entry point into internal corporate and operational networks |
| **Exploitation of Public Infrastructure** | Compromising airport Wi-Fi, kiosks, or passenger-facing systems | Interception of passenger data, pivot into corporate networks |

## SECTION 2: ANATOMY OF A BREACH — CASE STUDIES FROM OUR INDUSTRY

### 2.1 The Swissport Ransomware Attack (February 2023)

**What happened:** Switzerland-based Swissport, the world's largest ground-handling company, was hit by ransomware. The company was forced to take affected infrastructure offline and rely on manual workarounds.

**The impact:** At Zurich Airport alone, 22 flights were delayed. Swissport manages **282 million passengers and 4.8 million tons of cargo annually across 310 airports in 50 countries**. The potential for global disruption was enormous.

**The lesson:** A single vendor in the aviation supply chain — one that physically handles bags, fueling, and ramp operations — can be crippled by a standard ransomware attack, and there is no airline in the world that doesn't depend on such vendors.

### 2.2 The SITA / Air India Data Breach (2021)

**What happened:** A vulnerability in the SITA Passenger Service System — a shared backend data platform used by airlines worldwide — was exploited by attackers.

**The impact:** **4.5 million Air India passenger records** were compromised, including names, passport numbers, credit card details, phone numbers, email addresses, and full travel itineraries. SITA confirmed the breach on March 25, but Air India did not disclose it until May 21 — nearly two months of delayed notification.

**The lesson:** An airline's cybersecurity is only as strong as its weakest third-party vendor. Shared infrastructure creates shared risk. The two-month disclosure delay exacerbated passenger exposure to identity theft and fraud.

### 2.3 Seattle-Tacoma International Airport Attack (August 2024)

**What happened:** Sea-Tac Airport — Alaska Airlines' home hub — was hit by a cyber attack that severely disrupted regional travel.

**The lesson:** This was not a distant, theoretical incident. It happened at our primary operational base. Any similar attack during peak travel season could cascade across our entire route network.

### 2.4 The Collins Aerospace MUSE System Failure (September 2025)

**What happened:** A breach targeting the widely used MUSE check-in and baggage software hosted by Collins Aerospace caused mass flight cancellations at major European hubs including Heathrow, Brussels, and Berlin.

**The impact:** Airlines were forced to revert to manual check-in processes, resulting in severe delays, flight cancellations, and logistical backlogs that lasted through the weekend.

**The lesson:** Operational security is only as strong as third-party vendors. A single compromised software platform grounded check-in across an entire continent.

### 2.5 Japan Airlines DDoS Attack (December 26, 2024)

**What happened:** Japan Airlines suffered a DDoS attack that directly delayed flights — one of the clearest examples of a purely digital attack causing physical operational disruption.

### 2.6 Alaska Airlines' Own IT Ground Stop (October 2024)

**What happened:** A primary data center failure — explicitly stated as *not* a cyber attack — caused a systemwide ground stop, resulting in **229+ flight cancellations** across Alaska and Horizon Air.

**The critical lesson:** Even a non-malicious infrastructure failure revealed the brittleness of our operational technology stack. A deliberate attack exploiting the same single-points-of-failure would be far more damaging. Alaska Airlines experienced a similar critical hardware failure in July 2025 as well, suggesting systemic vulnerability.

## SECTION 3: WHERE WE ARE MOST VULNERABLE

### 3.1 IT/OT Convergence — The Blurring Line

Historically, operational technology (OT) — baggage systems, fuel routing, weight-and-balance calculations, flight operations — was physically isolated ("air-gapped") from corporate IT networks. Today, digital interconnectedness means a breach in passenger ticketing, in-flight Wi-Fi, or an employee email account can serve as a conduit to disrupt flight operations, crew scheduling, and navigation.

**The C-suite must understand:** A phishing email clicked by a marketing employee can, in a poorly segmented network, cascade into grounded flights.

### 3.2 Single Points of Failure

Many airlines' operational cores still feature **monolithic architectures** and legacy OT interfaces. Even when migrating to cloud or SaaS, dependencies shift rather than disappear. Alaska Airlines' data center failures in October 2024 and July 2025 both demonstrate that our critical systems have single points of failure that can halt the entire network.

### 3.3 Supply Chain — Our Weakest Link

The aviation supply chain is deeply interconnected:

- **Passenger Service Systems (PSS):** Platforms like SITA's system handle ticketing, check-in, and baggage for multiple airlines. One breach compromises millions of records across dozens of carriers.
- **Ground Handling:** Companies like Swissport physically move bags and fuel aircraft. A ransomware attack on Swissport delayed flights across 50 countries.
- **Check-in/Baggage Software:** The Collins Aerospace MUSE system serves dozens of airports. Its compromise grounded European travel.
- **IT Service Providers:** Turkish airline SunExpress lost data on 250,000 customers due to a cyberattack on its IT service provider — not its own systems.

**The harsh truth:** Alaska Airlines could have perfect internal cybersecurity and still be brought down by a compromised vendor.

### 3.4 Recovery Complexity

Even after an IT fault or cyber attack is "fixed," airlines must physically reposition aircraft and crews while navigating duty-time limits, creating rolling delays and hub backlogs. Delta Air Lines took **more than five days** to fully recover from the CrowdStrike outage. A targeted ransomware attack could take even longer.

## SECTION 4: THE REGULATORY HORIZON

### 4.1 Current and Emerging Requirements

The regulatory environment is tightening rapidly:

- **TSA Cybersecurity Directives:** The Transportation Security Administration has been issuing increasingly specific cybersecurity requirements for airports and airlines, including mandatory incident reporting, vulnerability assessments, and access controls.
- **FAA Oversight:** The Federal Aviation Administration is expanding its focus from physical safety to digital infrastructure security, recognizing that cyber threats to aviation constitute safety threats.
- **ICAO Standards:** The International Civil Aviation Organization (itself a victim of unauthorized access in 2024) is developing global cybersecurity frameworks for civil aviation.
- **GAO Scrutiny:** The Government Accountability Office has flagged that there is **no consistent, public reporting taxonomy** for airline IT disruptions, and has recommended standardized reporting — which will likely become mandatory.

### 4.2 What Compliance Will Require

Airlines will need to demonstrate:
- Documented cybersecurity risk management programs
- Regular penetration testing and vulnerability assessments
- Incident response plans that are tested and rehearsed
- Vendor risk management programs with contractual security requirements
- Employee cybersecurity awareness training
- Network segmentation between IT and OT environments

## SECTION 5: THE FINANCIAL STAKES

### 5.1 Direct Costs

| Cost Category | Description | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Ransom Payments** | Seven- to eight-figure demands common in aviation | Industry norm for critical infrastructure |
| **Remediation & Forensics** | Emergency incident response teams, system rebuilding | Often $10M+ for major incidents |
| **Regulatory Fines** | Penalties for data breaches and non-compliance | GDPR fines can reach 4% of global revenue |
| **Passenger Compensation** | EU261, DOT mandates, goodwill gestures | Delta faced mass compensation after CrowdStrike |
| **Legal Liability** | Class-action lawsuits from affected passengers | Common after data breaches |

### 5.2 Indirect Costs

- **Operational Disruption:** GAO found 85% of IT incidents caused flight delays/cancellations. Each grounded aircraft costs roughly $10,000–$75,000 per hour depending on aircraft type.
- **Crew Repositioning:** After system recovery, aircraft and crews must be physically repositioned while complying with duty-time regulations, extending disruptions for days.
- **Brand & Trust Erosion:** Consumer confidence is fragile. The SITA breach exposed passport numbers and credit cards — the kind of data that permanently damages customer relationships.

### 5.3 The Insurance Dimension

Cyber insurance premiums for aviation are rising sharply as insurers reassess systemic risk. Alaska Airlines should ensure coverage is based on scenario-based risk assessments that account for supply chain and OT-specific incidents.

## SECTION 6: A STRATEGIC ROADMAP — WHAT ALASKA AIRLINES MUST DO NOW

### 6.1 Immediate Priorities (0–6 Months)

| Action | Description |
|---|---|
| **Network Segmentation** | Isolate critical flight-safety OT systems from corporate IT networks. A breach in the passenger Wi-Fi must never reach crew scheduling or weight-and-balance systems. |
| **Vendor Risk Audit** | Conduct immediate security assessments of all critical third-party providers (PSS, ground handling, check-in systems, cloud providers). Mandate contractual requirements for breach notification within 24–48 hours. |
| **Incident Response Plan** | Develop, document, and rehearse a cyber-specific Incident Response Plan — including manual fallback procedures for check-in, boarding, and baggage handling. |
| **Employee Training** | Deploy mandatory, recurring cybersecurity awareness training focused on phishing, social engineering, and password hygiene. |

### 6.2 Medium-Term Initiatives (6–18 Months)

| Action | Description |
|---|---|
| **Zero Trust Architecture** | Transition to a security model where every user, device, and connection — internal or external — is continuously verified before access is granted. No implicit trust based on network location. |
| **OT-Specific Security Controls** | Standard IT firewalls are insufficient for operational technology. Deploy monitoring and protection designed specifically for baggage systems, fuel routing, flight operations, and fleet assets. |
| **Software Bill of Materials (SBOM)** | Require all software vendors to provide a complete inventory of components, enabling rapid identification of vulnerable dependencies. |
| **Cyber Insurance Review** | Ensure policies cover supply chain attacks, OT incidents, and business interruption from cyber events — not just data breaches. |

### 6.3 Long-Term Strategic Investments (18+ Months)

| Action | Description |
|---|---|
| **Architecture Modernization** | Decouple monolithic legacy systems via APIs and cloud-enabled orchestration, as McKinsey recommends — building security and resilience into the architecture rather than bolting it on. |
| **AI-Powered Threat Detection** | Deploy AI-driven tools that can dynamically predict attacker behavior, automate threat triage, and orchestrate containment before lateral movement occurs. |
| **Industry Information Sharing** | Participate in aviation-specific threat intelligence sharing organizations to identify emerging threats targeting fleet assets. |
| **Redundancy & Fail-Safe Design** | Implement hybrid manual/automatic systems with regular stress testing to ensure operations can continue during any IT outage — whether caused by hardware failure or cyber attack. |

### 6.4 Governance — The C-Suite Role

Cybersecurity cannot be delegated entirely to IT. Effective aviation cybersecurity requires:

- **A Transformational Cybersecurity Leader** to drive culture change from the board level
- **An Operational Cybersecurity Executive** to manage daily defense
- **A Cyber Risk Officer** to quantify cyber risk in financial terms for the board and investors

The board should receive quarterly cyber risk briefings with quantified financial exposure, just as it reviews operational and financial risk.

## SECTION 7: KEY DEFINITIONS FOR THE NON-TECHNICAL EXECUTIVE

| Term | Plain-English Definition |
|---|---|
| **Ransomware** | Malicious software that locks your systems and demands payment to unlock them |
| **DDoS Attack** | Overwhelming your servers with fake traffic so real users can't access them |
| **Zero Trust** | A security approach that trusts no one by default — every access request is verified, every time |
| **OT (Operational Technology)** | The computers and networks that control physical operations — baggage systems, fuel lines, flight systems |
| **IT (Information Technology)** | Standard business computing — email, booking systems, customer databases |
| **Network Segmentation** | Dividing your network into isolated zones so a breach in one area can't spread to others |
| **Phishing** | Fraudulent emails or messages designed to trick employees into revealing passwords or clicking malicious links |
| **Supply Chain Attack** | When hackers target a vendor or partner to indirectly attack your systems |
| **APT (Advanced Persistent Threat)** | A sophisticated, usually nation-state-backed hacking group that embeds in networks for long periods |
| **SBOM (Software Bill of Materials)** | A complete ingredients list for software — so you know exactly what's inside and can quickly find vulnerable components |
| **MFA (Multi-Factor Authentication)** | Requiring more than just a password to log in (e.g., a code sent to your phone) |
