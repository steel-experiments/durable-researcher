## I. EXECUTIVE SUMMARY

Haiti is experiencing a catastrophic convergence of state collapse, armed insurgency, and economic freefall that now directly threatens U.S. regional interests. Gangs control an estimated 90% of Port-au-Prince, approximately 8,700 people have been killed since early 2024, and 1.3 million are internally displaced. A fragile transitional government lacks the capacity or legitimacy to project authority, while the Kenyan-led Multinational Security Support Mission (MSS) remains critically under-resourced at roughly 1,000 of its 2,500 authorized personnel. Current U.S. policy — simultaneously scaling back immigration protections while failing to lead on security assistance — is incoherent and counterproductive.

---

## II. INTERCONNECTED POLITICAL, ECONOMIC, AND SECURITY FAILURES

### A. Security Collapse
The Haitian state has lost its monopoly on violence. Approximately 200 heavily armed gangs now operate across 63 communes, controlling ports, oil terminals, and critical roadways through extortion and protection rackets [1][5]. In March 2024, gangs stormed two major prisons, freeing over 4,700 inmates, a single event that crystallized the complete erosion of state authority [5]. The Haitian National Police (HNP) has as few as 3,000 active officers — vastly outnumbered and outgunned [3]. Self-defense brigades (*brigades de vigilance*) formed by desperate residents have themselves devolved into perpetrators of extrajudicial violence [1]. Haiti manufactures no firearms or ammunition domestically; an estimated 500,000 small arms are in circulation, the vast majority flowing from the United States through Florida [2][7].

### B. Political Vacuum
Haiti has had no sitting legislators since 2019 and no elected president since the assassination of Jovenel Moïse in 2021. Prime Minister Ariel Henry resigned on March 12, 2024, under direct gang pressure [5]. A Transitional Presidential Council, brokered by CARICOM and backed by the U.S., was established in 2024 but lacks capacity to govern, faces organized violent opposition, and has no democratic mandate [1][4]. The country has not held elections in nearly a decade.

### C. Economic Devastation
Haiti has suffered five consecutive years of GDP contraction — shrinking 8.5% over the past decade — with inflation at 26.9% [2]. The World Bank estimates national losses exceeding $9 billion since 2019. A proposed $1.3 billion World Bank recovery plan for Port-au-Prince is now only 50% executable due to violence [2]. The IDB's alternative strategy targets the Greater North (Cap-Haïtien), which hosts Haiti's only functioning international airport, but experts warn that no economic plan can succeed without basic security [2]. The private sector in the capital has been hollowed out; unemployed youth are fodder for gang recruitment.

**Key Insight:** These three failures form a self-reinforcing cycle. Security collapse prevents economic activity and governance; political vacuum prevents coordinated security response; economic desperation fuels gang recruitment and further insecurity.

## III. KEY DOMESTIC ACTORS

### A. G9 an fanmi / Viv Ansanm — Led by Jimmy "Barbecue" Chérizier
The G9 coalition, led by former police officer Jimmy Chérizier, represents the most dangerous fusion of organized crime and political power in Haiti. Originally tied to ruling elites, the G9 demonstrated its political leverage by directly forcing Henry's resignation through coordinated violence [5]. Chérizier has styled himself as a revolutionary leader and retains documented links to political and business elites who have historically used gangs as proxies. The G9 coordinates with other gang alliances (the broader "Viv Ansanm" coalition) to control territory, extort commercial traffic, and resist state and international forces [1][5].

### B. Guy Philippe and the BSAP
Guy Philippe — a convicted money launderer repatriated from U.S. federal prison in November 2023 — represents a distinct and potent threat. A former police commander who helped lead the 2004 coup against President Aristide, Philippe now leads the "Réveil National" political party and is actively campaigning nationwide [3]. His operational muscle comes from the Brigade of the Security of Protected Areas (BSAP), estimated at 2,000–6,000 heavily armed members including former soldiers from his 2004 coup network [3]. The BSAP has repeatedly clashed with the HNP. Philippe has publicly expressed solidarity with Chérizier and called for "revolution," though he positions himself as an anti-elite stabilizer — a narrative that resonates amid the state vacuum [3][5]. Philippe is a wildcard: neither fully aligned with nor controlled by the gangs, but capable of destabilizing any transitional arrangement.

## IV. KEY INTERNATIONAL ACTORS

### A. CARICOM
CARICOM has been the most active regional diplomatic actor, brokering the 2024 transitional governance framework and pressuring Henry to resign to facilitate a political opening [4][5]. CARICOM's legitimacy as a mediator is anchored in shared regional identity, which partially insulates its efforts from the anti-imperialist backlash that accompanies U.S. or French interventions. However, CARICOM lacks the financial and military resources to enforce its agreements.

### B. United Nations / MSS Mission
The UN Security Council authorized the Kenya-led Multinational Security Support Mission in October 2023. One year after the first Kenyan contingent deployed (June 25, 2024), the mission has approximately 1,000 of 2,500 promised personnel and is chronically underfunded [1][2][7]. It requires an estimated $600 million to sustain operations. UN Secretary-General Guterres has recommended transitioning the MSS into a full peacekeeping mission, which would provide stable assessed funding and a more robust mandate [1]. Benin and other nations have pledged personnel but deployment has been slow. European partners have increased financial contributions but cite U.S. diplomatic disengagement at the Security Council as a major obstacle [1].

### C. United States (Current Posture)
U.S. policy is contradictory. On the security front, Washington has backed the MSS and transitional council rhetorically but has not contributed troops or sufficient funding. On immigration, the Trump administration moved to terminate Temporary Protected Status (TPS) for over 500,000 Haitians in June 2025 — a decision blocked by federal courts as of July 2025, with Supreme Court arguments scheduled for April 2026 [7][8]. DHS claimed conditions in Haiti had "improved," a characterization directly contradicted by the State Department's own travel warnings and UN data showing 4,864 killed from October 2024 through June 2025 [7]. The House Foreign Affairs Committee has reintroduced the Haiti Criminal Collusion Transparency Act (H.R. 2643), which would mandate annual reporting on gang-elite ties and authorize targeted sanctions [7].

## V. THREE ACTIONABLE U.S. POLICY OPTIONS

### OPTION 1: Lead a UN Security Council Transition to a Full Peacekeeping Mission + Restore TPS

**Action:** The U.S. should champion a UNSC resolution to convert the MSS into a formal UN Peacekeeping Operation (PKO), providing access to assessed (mandatory) funding, a stronger mandate for civilian protection, and a clearer command structure. Simultaneously, the administration should reverse its termination of TPS for Haitian migrants, restoring the Biden-era extension through at least February 2026 and refraining from appealing the federal court injunction.

**Benefits:**
- A PKO would unlock stable funding and enable force generation beyond the current 2,500-person MSS ceiling, directly addressing the gangs' 90% territorial control.
- Restoring TPS aligns domestic policy with empirical reality, avoids injecting 500,000+ displaced persons into an active conflict zone, and preserves U.S. credibility as a honest broker.
- The combined approach addresses both the proximate security crisis and the humanitarian dimension, signaling coherent U.S. commitment to hemispheric partners.

**Risks:**
- Securing a UNSC resolution requires persuading Russia and China, who may veto or extract concessions. This demands significant diplomatic capital.
- A PKO implies a multi-year financial and political commitment. The U.S. would be expected to bear a substantial share of assessed costs (~27% of the peacekeeping budget).
- Restoring TPS will face fierce domestic political opposition regardless of the merits.
- Any large international force risks the perception of foreign occupation in a country with a fraught history of interventions, potentially galvanizing anti-international sentiment that Philippe and Chérizier can exploit.

---

### OPTION 2: Fully Fund and Arm the MSS + Florida Arms Interdiction + Targeted Sanctions

**Action:** Provide bilateral U.S. funding (estimated $200–300 million) to bring the MSS to its full 2,500-person mandate, supplemented by U.S. intelligence-sharing, logistical support, and armored vehicles — without deploying U.S. combat troops. Concurrently, mandate X-ray inspections for all cargo departing Florida ports bound for Haiti to disrupt the arms pipeline. Implement targeted Treasury sanctions under the Haiti Criminal Collusion Transparency Act against political and business elites colluding with gang leadership, and designate Guy Philippe's BSAP as a sanctioned entity.

**Benefits:**
- Works within existing legal frameworks (the MSS mandate) without requiring a new UNSC resolution, enabling faster action.
- Directly degrades gang capabilities by cutting the weapons supply — the single greatest force multiplier enabling gang territorial control.
- Targeted sanctions against Philippe's network and elite enablers signal that the U.S. will not tolerate paramilitarism or gang-political collusion, without incentivizing direct confrontation.
- Avoids the political costs and risks associated with U.S. troop deployment.

**Risks:**
- Even at full strength, 2,500 MSS personnel may be structurally insufficient to retake and hold Port-au-Prince against ~200 gangs with 500,000 weapons in circulation. A failed mission would be worse than no mission — it would discredit international intervention and embolden armed groups.
- Florida cargo inspections face legal challenges from shipping companies and Second Amendment advocacy groups.
- Sanctions against Philippe and the BSAP could provoke violent retaliation against the transitional government, MSS forces, or U.S. diplomatic assets. Philippe has demonstrated willingness to use force (repeated BSAP-HNP clashes) and could align more closely with the G9 if cornered.
- This option is a half-measure that may delay but not prevent full collapse, requiring escalation to Option 1 within 12–18 months.

---

### OPTION 3: Support CARICOM-Led Political Process + Conditioned Security Sector Reform + Regional Humanitarian Corridor

**Action:** Empower CARICOM as the lead mediator for Haiti's political transition — backing its efforts to establish a credible, inclusive transitional government with a fixed electoral timeline — while conditioning U.S. security assistance on verifiable reform benchmarks for the HNP (anti-corruption vetting, chain-of-command restructuring, human rights training). Establish a U.S.-funded humanitarian corridor through Cap-Haïtien (the only secure international airport) in partnership with the IDB and World Bank, channeling aid, food, and medical supplies to displaced populations while bypassing gang-controlled Port-au-Prince.

**Benefits:**
- Centers regional actors (CARICOM, IDB) rather than the U.S., reducing anti-imperialist backlash and building sustainable regional capacity.
- Conditioned security sector reform addresses the root cause of state incapacity — a corrupt, under-resourced police force — rather than relying solely on international forces.
- The northern humanitarian corridor provides immediate relief to displaced populations and creates economic alternatives to gang recruitment, leveraging the IDB's existing Grand North development plan (2025–2030) [2].
- A fixed electoral timeline gives the transitional government legitimacy and a clear exit strategy, addressing the political vacuum.

**Risks:**
- CARICOM has limited enforcement capacity and may be unable to compel compliance from armed actors. Without a credible security deterrent, the G9 and BSAP may simply refuse to participate or escalate violence to disrupt the process.
- Conditioned aid may be too slow. HNP reform is a multi-year endeavor; the current crisis demands immediate action.
- A northern corridor strategy effectively cedes Port-au-Prince to gang control in the near term, entrenching territorial fragmentation and potentially formalizing a de facto partition of the country.
- The transitional government may lack the political will or institutional capacity to meet reform benchmarks, creating an impasse where U.S. assistance is neither disbursed nor the security situation improved.

---

## VI. RECOMMENDATION

None of these options is sufficient alone. The recommended approach is a **sequenced combination of Options 2 and 3 in the immediate term (0–12 months)**, with **Option 1 as the medium-term objective (12–24 months)**. Specifically:

1. **Now:** Fund the MSS, interdict arms, and sanction rogue actors (Option 2) while empowering CARICOM mediation and opening a northern humanitarian corridor (Option 3).
2. **Next:** Use improved security conditions to push for a UNSC-mandated PKO and credible elections (Option 1).
3. **Throughout:** Maintain TPS protections and refrain from deportations that would destabilize both Haiti and the region.
