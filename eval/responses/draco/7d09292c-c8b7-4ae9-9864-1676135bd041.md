# Women's Labor Force Participation: A Comparative Analysis of Saudi Arabia, South Korea, Iceland, and India (1970–2025)

---

## Executive Summary

Women's labor force participation across these four nations reveals a stark divergence shaped by policy, culture, and economic structure. Iceland has sustained rates above 66% for over three decades through comprehensive parental leave, subsidized childcare, and legal equality. Saudi Arabia has undergone the most dramatic transformation—rising from ~15% in the early 1990s to over 34% by 2024—driven by Vision 2030 reforms. South Korea has steadily climbed from ~47% to ~56% but remains constrained by persistent cultural norms. India has fluctuated between 26–35%, with recent upticks masked by deep rural-urban, educational, and informal-sector disparities. Each country's trajectory demonstrates that legal reform and social infrastructure are the decisive levers for female economic inclusion.

### Key Trajectory Patterns

**Iceland** has maintained the most consistently high female participation rate in the world. After peaking at 73.42% in 2000, it experienced a gentle decline to ~70% by 2024—still nearly double the rate of many developed economies. The small dip since 2000 reflects an aging population and marginal shifts toward part-time work rather than regression.

**South Korea** shows a steady upward trajectory from ~47% in the early 1990s to ~56% in 2024, gaining roughly 9 percentage points over three decades. The 1998 minimum of 47.12% coincides with the Asian Financial Crisis. Growth has been consistent but gradual, reflecting both expanding educational attainment and incremental policy reforms.

**Saudi Arabia** has experienced the most dramatic shift of any country studied. From a baseline of ~15% in 1990–1991, participation has more than doubled to 34.41% in 2024. The acceleration has been particularly steep post-2017, coinciding with Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman's social liberalization agenda. Despite this, at 34.41%, Saudi Arabia still sits well below the global average of 51.13%.

**India** presents the most paradoxical case. Despite rapid economic growth, female participation hovered around 30–35% from 1990 to 2010, declined sharply to a nadir of 26% in 2020, and has rebounded to 32.80% by 2024. The long decline from its 2005 peak of 34.85% remains one of the most puzzling phenomena in development economics, attributed to measurement issues, the transition from agriculture to services, rising education enrollment, and cultural restrictions on women's mobility.

## 2. Policy Interventions and Their Impact

### 2.1 Iceland: The Gold Standard of Gender Policy

Iceland's sustained ~70% female participation rate is the product of a coherent, decades-long policy architecture:

- **Parental Leave:** Iceland offers 12 months of paid parental leave, divided into three equal portions—one for the mother, one for the father, and one that either parent can use. Each parent receives 80% wage compensation. The "use-it-or-lose-it" father's quota has been critical in normalizing men's caregiving and reducing employer discrimination against women of childbearing age.

- **Childcare Subsidies:** Universal, heavily subsidized preschool (leikskóli) from age 1–6 ensures that the gap between the end of parental leave and school entry does not force women out of the labor market. Municipalities are legally obligated to provide childcare.

- **Legal Framework:** The Icelandic Equal Pay Certification (2018) requires companies with 25+ employees to prove they pay men and women equally for equal work. Gender-based wage discrimination has been illegal since 1976, and the country has had the world's highest gender parity rankings for over a decade.

- **Cultural Infrastructure:** High social trust, small population homogeneity, and deeply embedded norms of gender equality create reinforcing feedback loops—policies normalize women's work, and women's high participation normalizes the policies.

### 2.2 South Korea: Incremental Reform Against Deep Cultural Resistance

South Korea's rise from ~47% to ~56% reflects important but incomplete policy interventions:

- **Parental Leave:** South Korea offers 1.5 years of parental leave (extended from 1 year in 2019) with wage compensation. However, take-up by fathers remains extremely low (~2–3% as of recent estimates) due to workplace stigma and cultural expectations. The "father's quota" was introduced in a limited form but lacks the enforcement mechanisms of the Icelandic model.

- **Childcare:** Free childcare for children under age 5 was introduced in 2013. This has helped increase participation among women in their 30s and 40s but has not fully resolved the labor market's structural barriers.

- **The M-Curve Problem:** South Korea's female participation by age historically formed an "M-curve"—women entered the workforce in their 20s, exited during childbearing years (30s), and returned in their 40s–50s. While the dip has flattened somewhat, it persists, indicating that childcare infrastructure alone has not eliminated the career penalty for motherhood.

- **Glass Ceiling:** South Korea has the largest gender pay gap in the OECD (~31%), reflecting vertical segregation. Women remain underrepresented in management and leadership positions despite having higher average educational attainment than men.

### 2.3 Saudi Arabia: Top-Down Social Revolution

Saudi Arabia's doubling of female participation from ~15% to ~34% is primarily attributable to state-directed reform under Vision 2030:

- **Mobility Reforms:** The lifting of the driving ban for women (June 2018) was the single most symbolic and practical reform. Without the ability to drive, women's access to employment was fundamentally constrained by the availability of male drivers or (previously) male guardians' permission to use ride services.

- **Guardianship System Erosion:** Since 2019, women no longer need male guardian approval to obtain a passport, travel, or register a marriage. Previous guardianship requirements meant that women's ability to work, open bank accounts, or sign contracts was legally dependent on a male relative.

- **Sector-Specific Push:** The government has mandated female representation on corporate boards, required certain retail sectors to employ women, and actively recruited women into the growing entertainment, tourism, and technology sectors.

- **Education Pipeline:** Saudi women now outnumber men in university enrollment (roughly 55–60% of graduates are female), creating a highly educated female population with growing expectations of employment.

- **Persistent Gaps:** Female unemployment remains extremely high at 12.5% (vs. 1.79% for men), suggesting that while more women are seeking work, structural and cultural barriers to hiring persist. Women constitute only 19.66% of the total labor force.

### 2.4 India: Fragmented Progress in a Complex Federation

India's policy landscape is complicated by federalism, informal economy dominance, and enormous regional variation:

- **Legal Reforms:** The Maternity Benefit (Amendment) Act of 2017 increased paid maternity leave from 12 to 26 weeks—one of the most generous globally. However, it applies only to the formal sector, which employs fewer than 10% of Indian women. The 2005 Right to Information Act and evolving inheritance rights (the Hindu Succession Act was amended in 2005 to give daughters equal inheritance rights in ancestral property) have incrementally improved women's legal standing.

- **Mobility and Safety:** The paradox of rising education but declining participation is partly explained by safety concerns. High-profile cases of gender-based violence and persistent norms around women's mobility restrict labor market access, particularly in urban areas and northern states.

- **Informal Employment Dominance:** India's female informal employment rate is approximately 88.80%, meaning most women work in agriculture, home-based piecework, or domestic service without formal contracts, social protection, or labor law coverage. This makes policy interventions difficult to implement and measure.

- **Regional Divergence:** Southern states like Kerala and Tamil Nadu have significantly higher female participation (~35–45%) than northern states like Bihar and Uttar Pradesh (~10–20%), reflecting deep cultural, educational, and industrial differences.

## 3. Breakdowns by Education Level, Sector, and Employment Type

### 3.1 Education Level

- **Iceland:** Female participation is high across all education levels, though women with tertiary education have marginally higher rates. Iceland's near-universal secondary and tertiary education system means the educational gradient is relatively flat.

- **South Korea:** The U-shaped relationship between education and participation has largely disappeared. Women with tertiary education now have the highest participation rates (~65%), while those with only secondary education remain more concentrated in service and care work.

- **Saudi Arabia:** The education-participation gap is striking. Saudi women have among the highest university enrollment rates in the region, but this has not yet translated into proportionate employment. The mismatch is slowly closing as the economy diversifies beyond oil.

- **India:** The relationship is U-shaped: women with no education (agricultural labor) and women with graduate degrees (professional employment) have higher participation than women with intermediate education, for whom cultural restrictions on "appropriate" work are most binding.

### 3.3 Part-Time vs. Full-Time Employment

- **Iceland:** Part-time work among women (~35–40%) is common, often by choice, facilitated by strong labor protections that ensure pro-rata benefits. Part-time work does not carry the stigma or penalty seen elsewhere.

- **South Korea:** Part-time and non-regular employment among women is significant (~35% of employed women), but it is frequently involuntary and carries substantial wage penalties, limited benefits, and career dead-ends. This is a key driver of the gender pay gap.

- **Saudi Arabia:** Full-time employment dominates for women, partly because part-time work arrangements are less culturally accepted and partly because the formal sector is the primary gateway for female employment. Government employment is overwhelmingly full-time.

- **India:** The formal/informal distinction is more relevant than part-time/full-time. Among formal-sector women, most work full-time. In the informal sector, women's work is often intermittent, seasonal, and invisible to official statistics.

## 4. Demographic Projections and Future Participation

### Iceland (Population ~390,000)
- **Aging pressure:** Iceland's fertility rate has fallen to ~1.7, below replacement. An aging population will increase demand for care workers (a female-dominated sector) while reducing the working-age population. Immigration may partially offset this.
- **Projection:** Female participation likely remains in the 68–72% range through 2035, with potential modest increases if older women's participation rises further.

### South Korea (Population ~52 million)
- **Demographic crisis:** With the world's lowest fertility rate (~0.72 in 2023), South Korea faces a shrinking working-age population that could compel higher female participation. By 2050, the working-age population is projected to decline by over 30%.
- **Projection:** Female participation could reach 60–63% by 2035 if policy reforms (expanded paternity leave, glass-ceiling legislation) accelerate. Without deeper cultural change, the ceiling remains constrained by persistent gender norms.

### Saudi Arabia (Population ~36 million)
- **Youth bulge and Vision 2030:** Over 60% of the population is under 35, and female university graduates are entering the workforce in unprecedented numbers. Vision 2030 targets female participation of 30% (already exceeded at 34%).
- **Projection:** Continued growth to 40–45% by 2035 is plausible if private-sector hiring accelerates and cultural norms continue liberalizing. The gap between male (80.75%) and female (34.41%) participation remains enormous, indicating substantial headroom.

### India (Population ~1.44 billion)
- **Working-age dividend:** India has the world's largest working-age population, but the female dividend remains largely untapped. If female participation could reach even 50%, GDP growth could accelerate by 1–1.5 percentage points annually (McKinsey Global Institute estimates).
- **Urbanization and education:** As more women complete secondary and tertiary education and as urbanization continues, participation rates are expected to rise. The recent uptick from 26% (2020) to 33% (2024) suggests momentum.
- **Projection:** Female participation could reach 38–42% by 2035 if accompanied by expanded childcare infrastructure, safety improvements, and formalization of the economy. Regional disparities will persist, with southern states leading.

## 5. Synthesis: What Drives Female Labor Force Participation?

The comparative analysis reveals several cross-cutting themes:

1. **Legal autonomy is necessary but not sufficient.** Saudi Arabia's guardianship reforms and India's inheritance laws are foundational, but without cultural shifts and childcare infrastructure, their impact is muted. Iceland demonstrates that comprehensive legal frameworks work best when paired with enforcement and social buy-in.

2. **Childcare is the single most impactful policy lever.** Every country that has achieved high female participation—Iceland being the exemplar—has ensured affordable, universal childcare. South Korea's free childcare (2013) helped flatten the M-curve but arrived too late for older cohorts.

3. **Fathers matter as much as mothers.** Iceland's "daddy quota" has been more effective than South Korea's paternal leave policies because it creates social expectation, not just legal permission. Where fathers do not take leave, employers discriminate against women of childbearing age, and the career penalty for motherhood persists.

4. **Education without employment infrastructure creates frustration.** Both Saudi Arabia and India have rapidly educated female populations but lack the labor market structures to absorb them. Saudi female unemployment at 12.5%—seven times the male rate—exemplifies this mismatch.

5. **Demographic urgency will accelerate change.** In South Korea, population decline makes female labor force participation an existential economic question, not merely a rights issue. In India, the demographic dividend is running out. These pressures will drive policy innovation even where cultural resistance remains strong.
