# Respondeat Superior Analysis: Daniel Rocha Collision (June 14, 2024)

## Executive Summary

Applying the three-factor respondeat superior test—employee status, scope of employment, and benefit to the employer—the company is vicariously liable for Daniel Rocha's rear-end collision on Route 7. Rocha was an undisputed employee acting under direct supervisory instruction on a minor geographical detour (four blocks) that constituted a "detour" rather than a "frolic." The company's own retroactive reimbursement, its updated "team support errands" policy, and the supervisor's text direction all confirm that Rocha was acting within the scope of employment. Summary judgment in favor of the plaintiff sedan driver is warranted; the employer's motion for summary judgment should be denied.

---

## I. Legal Framework: Respondeat Superior

The doctrine of *respondeat superior* holds an employer or principal legally liable for the wrongful acts of an employee occurring within the scope of employment, regardless of the employer's level of monitoring [1]. Liability requires proof of three elements:

1. **Employee Status:** The tortfeasor must be an employee, not an independent contractor.
2. **Scope of Employment:** The tortious conduct must have occurred within the range of activities the employee was reasonably expected to perform as part of the job [2].
3. **Benefit to the Employer:** The employee's conduct must have been motivated, at least in part, by a purpose to serve the employer [1][5].

Vicarious liability under this doctrine is a form of strict liability—the employer need not have been negligent itself. The principal is liable because the agent's actions represent the principal [4].

## II. Factor One: Employee Status

**Conclusion: Satisfied. No genuine dispute.**

Daniel Rocha is a company delivery driver who operates under company policy and supervisor direction. He follows assigned routes, adheres to company scheduling, and is subject to company disciplinary rules (including policies governing detours). This establishes a classic employer-employee relationship governed by control—the company directs *how*, *where*, and *when* Rocha performs his work. There is no indication Rocha operates as an independent contractor. This element is satisfied as a matter of law [1].

## III. Factor Two: Scope of Employment — Detour vs. Frolic

**Conclusion: Rocha was acting within the scope of employment. His deviation was a detour, not a frolic.**

### A. The Detour/Frolic Doctrine

The critical analytical framework comes from the frolic and detour doctrine, originating in *Joel v. Morison* (1834) and still applied in modern American courts, including *O'Connor v. McDonald's Restaurants* [3]. Under this doctrine:

- A **detour** is a minor departure from an employee's duties that still falls within the scope of employment. The employer **remains vicariously liable**.
- A **frolic** is a major departure from the scope of employment undertaken primarily for the employee's own benefit. The employer is **not liable** [3].

The distinction turns on the degree and character of the deviation—its geographical extent, temporal duration, and whether the employee was still serving the employer's interests.

### B. Multi-Factor Application to Rocha

Courts typically analyze scope of employment using a multi-factor test. Applying the relevant factors:

| Factor | Analysis | Favors Employer? |
|---|---|---|
| **Time** | Rocha remained on schedule and intended to complete deliveries immediately after lunch. Temporal deviation was negligible. | No |
| **Place** | Collision occurred on Route 7, only four blocks off the assigned route—a minor geographical deviation. | No |
| **Authorization** | Rocha received a **text message from his supervisor** directing him to pick up lunch for coworkers. Deleted email metadata confirms supervisory approval existed. The company's updated policy (effective May 1, 2024) expressly permits "team support errands." | No |
| **Normalcy of Conduct** | While the pre-existing policy prohibited personal detours, the company updated its policy to allow team support errands six weeks before the collision, and the supervisor affirmatively directed the lunch run. | No |
| **Purpose** | Rocha was not pursuing a purely personal errand. He was procuring sandwiches for two warehouse coworkers at the direction of a supervisor, constituting a team-support function. | No |
| **Foreseeability** | A rear-end collision is a foreseeable risk of operating a delivery vehicle. Rocha's momentary phone glance (two seconds) constitutes driver negligence—a foreseeable risk the employer assumes by dispatching drivers. | No |
| **Retroactive Employer Ratification** | The company reimbursed Rocha's mileage for the detour *after* the collision, citing the updated team support errands policy. This constitutes post-hoc ratification acknowledging the trip was business-related. | No |

Every significant factor weighs against the employer. The four-block deviation is geographically minor—the textbook definition of a detour [3]. Rocha remained on schedule and was returning to his route when the collision occurred. Critically, a supervisor's direct instruction to perform the errand transforms what might otherwise be a personal frolic into a work-related detour.

### C. The Supervisor's Direction Is Dispositive

The single most important fact is that **Rocha's supervisor sent a text message directing him to pick up lunch for the warehouse coworkers**. This is not an employee who independently decided to deviate from his route for personal reasons. He was following a management directive. Under *respondeat superior*, a master is generally liable for acts the master assented to or directed [5]. The deleted email (confirmed by metadata) corroborates that supervisory approval existed through multiple channels. Even if the company disputes the content of these communications, the metadata evidence creates at minimum a genuine issue of fact—and here, it affirmatively establishes scope.

### D. The Phone Glance Does Not Remove Rocha from Scope

The dashcam footage showing Rocha glanced at his phone two seconds before impact establishes **negligence** but does not remove him from the **scope of employment**. Momentary inattention while driving is precisely the kind of employee negligence for which *respondeat superior* imposes employer liability. The doctrine's purpose is to place the cost of workplace-related torts on the enterprise that created the risk—not to require perfect employee attentiveness as a condition of employer liability [1]. The sedan's brake lights functioned properly, confirming Rocha's inattention was the proximate cause of the collision, but this inattention occurred *while* he was performing a work-directed errand.

## IV. Factor Three: Benefit to the Employer

**Conclusion: Satisfied. The errand conferred a tangible benefit on the employer.**

The "benefit to employer" test does not require that the employee's conduct *solely* benefit the employer. Rather, the agent must be "motivated to any degree" to benefit the principal [5]. Here, the benefit is established through multiple channels:

1. **Team Support:** The lunch pickup directly benefited two warehouse coworkers who had requested sandwiches that morning, contributing to team morale and operational continuity.
2. **Supervisor Authorization:** The supervisor's text and email approvals confirm management viewed the errand as serving an employment-related function.
3. **Company Policy Endorsement:** The updated policy effective May 1, 2024, expressly authorizes "team support errands," categorizing Rocha's lunch run as a recognized business function.
4. **Retroactive Mileage Reimbursement:** The company reimbursed Rocha's mileage for the detour *after* the collision, which constitutes an acknowledgment that the trip served a business purpose. Reimbursement is an act of ratification—by financially covering the trip, the company adopted the deviation as its own.

The confluence of supervisory direction, company policy, and retroactive reimbursement makes the employer benefit unambiguous.

## V. Summary Judgment Analysis

### A. Standard of Review

Summary judgment is appropriate only where there is no genuine dispute as to any material fact and the movant is entitled to judgment as a matter of law. Where scope of employment is at issue, courts recognize it as typically a **question of fact for the jury** when the facts are disputed [2]. However, where the undisputed facts overwhelmingly favor one conclusion, a court may resolve scope of employment as a matter of law.

### B. The Employer Cannot Prevail on Summary Judgment

If the **employer** moves for summary judgment arguing Rocha was outside the scope of employment, the motion must be **denied**. The evidentiary record—supervisor text, email metadata, updated policy, retroactive reimbursement—overwhelmingly establishes that Rocha was acting within the scope of employment. No reasonable jury could find this four-block, supervisor-directed, policy-sanctioned errand constituted a frolic.

### C. The Sedan Driver Can Prevail on Vicarious Liability

If the **plaintiff sedan driver** moves for summary judgment on vicarious liability, the motion should be **granted** on the *respondeat superior* question. The material facts are either undisputed or supported by documentary evidence:

- Rocha's employee status is undisputed.
- The scope of employment analysis yields a clear detour, not a frolic, based on the minor geographical deviation, supervisory direction, and company policy.
- Employer benefit is established through multiple, overlapping evidentiary channels.
- Proximate causation is straightforward: Rocha rear-ended a sedan with functioning brake lights while momentarily distracted.

The only factual disputes—the content of the deleted email, the precise scope of the supervisor's text—are immaterial because even without the email, the text, the updated policy, and the reimbursement independently establish scope of employment.

## VI. Counterarguments and Weaknesses

The employer might raise the following defenses, none of which prevail:

1. **Pre-collision policy prohibited personal detours.** Response: The updated policy (effective May 1, 2024) superseded this prohibition for "team support errands." Even if it did not, the supervisor's direct instruction overrides general policy. An employer cannot issue a direct order and then disavow liability for the employee's compliance.

2. **Rocha was negligent (phone glance).** Response: Employee negligence is the very basis for *respondeat superior* liability. The doctrine imposes strict liability on employers for tortious employee conduct within scope [4]. The phone glance establishes Rocha's fault; it does not exonerate the employer.

3. **The sedan braked suddenly for fallen lumber (intervening/superseding cause).** Response: The sedan's brake lights functioned properly, meaning Rocha had a normal stopping opportunity. A sudden braking event is a foreseeable occurrence on any roadway, particularly for a professional delivery driver. This does not constitute a superseding cause that breaks the chain of proximate causation.

4. **Deleted email cannot be authenticated.** Response: Even setting aside the deleted email entirely, the supervisor's text message, the updated company policy, and the retroactive mileage reimbursement independently establish all three elements of *respondeat superior*. The email is cumulative, not essential.

5. **Retroactive reimbursement was a goodwill gesture, not ratification.** Response: The reimbursement was expressly tied to the "team support errands" policy, not framed as gratuitous. Courts treat post-hoc employer adoption of an employee's acts—particularly financial reimbursement citing specific policy authority—as evidence that the conduct was within scope.

---

## VII. Conclusion

Under the *respondeat superior* doctrine, the company is vicariously liable for Daniel Rocha's rear-end collision on Route 7 on June 14, 2024. All three requisite elements are satisfied:

- **Employee Status:** Rocha is an undisputed company employee.
- **Scope of Employment:** His four-block detour to pick up lunch for coworkers at his supervisor's direction was a minor deviation—a detour, not a frolic—conducted during working hours on a company route, under company policy authorizing "team support errands."
- **Benefit to Employer:** The errand served team needs, was directed by a supervisor, was sanctioned by company policy, and was retroactively reimbursed by the employer.

The plaintiff is entitled to summary judgment on the question of the company's vicarious liability. The sedan driver's soft-tissue neck strain injury, supported by medical imaging, is a compensable harm proximately caused by Rocha's negligence within the scope of employment. The company should be held liable for all resulting damages.
