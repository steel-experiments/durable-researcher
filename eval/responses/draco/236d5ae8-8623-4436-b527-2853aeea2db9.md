# New Zealand Construction Metalworks (Structural Steel Fabrication) Market Analysis 2018–2024

---

## Executive Summary

The New Zealand structural steel fabrication market serves a construction sector buffeted by major infrastructure investment and cyclical residential/commercial building activity. The industry, represented by Steel Construction New Zealand (SCNZ) encompassing over 500 companies, has been significantly shaped by megaprojects such as the $5.49 billion City Rail Link (CRL). However, granular public data on market size, individual fabricator revenues, and tonnage remains limited due to Stats NZ confidentiality suppressions and the absence of a dedicated industry census. This report synthesises available data and transparently flags gaps where authoritative primary sources were inaccessible.

---

## (a) Market Size and Growth: Revenue, Employment, and Tonnage (2018–2024)

### Data Landscape and Limitations

The primary source for industry-level financial data in New Zealand is the **Stats NZ Annual Enterprise Survey (AES)**, which covers over 60% of GDP and reports results using the **ANZSIC06 classification** at NZSIOC level 4. Structural steel fabrication falls under **ANZSIC Subdivision C222 (Structural Steel Fabricating)**, a sub-category of **Division C (Manufacturing)**. [[Source: Stats NZ DataInfo+ — Annual Enterprise Survey](https://datainfoplus.stats.govt.nz/item/nz.govt.stats/36809771-984d-4e6b-89a1-576f2118b05b/711)]

However, several constraints apply:

1. **Confidentiality suppressions**: Stats NZ applies suppression rules to protect individual company data, which is particularly impactful in a small, concentrated industry like structural steel fabrication where a few large players dominate.
2. **Data granularity**: AES data must be used with caution below NZSIOC level 4. The structural steel fabrication sub-class is narrow enough that detailed public releases are often suppressed.
3. **Access limitations**: The specific AES data tables for ANZSIC C222 were not publicly accessible via the browsed Stats NZ portals during this research. The AES 2023 and 2024 provisional releases require downloading and parsing large Excel datasets from Stats NZ. [[Source: Stats NZ AES 2023 Provisional Data](https://www.stats.govt.nz/assets/Uploads/Annual-enterprise-survey/Annual-enterprise-survey-2023-financial-year-provisional/Download-data/annual-enterprise-survey-2023-financial-year-provisional-size-bands.xlsx)]

### Estimated Market Context

While exact sub-industry figures are suppressed, broader context can be drawn from:

- **New Zealand's total construction sector** has been a significant GDP contributor, with major government infrastructure investment through the NZ Upgrade Programme and post-COVID recovery spending.
- **Steel Construction New Zealand (SCNZ)** reports representing a network of **over 500 companies** spanning manufacturers, distributors, fabricators, erectors, designers, detailers, galvanisers, and paint/building supply companies. [[Source: SCNZ Homepage — https://www.scnz.org]]

**⚠ Gap Flagged**: Specific NZD revenue, employment headcount, and tonnage fabricated for the structural steel fabrication sub-industry (ANZSIC C222) for 2018–2024 was not available from the publicly accessible Stats NZ releases consulted. The AES Excel data downloads (available at the links above) would need to be obtained and filtered for ANZSIC C222 to extract this data.

## (c) NZ Import Tariff Rates for Raw Steel (HS Codes 7207–7216)

### Tariff Framework

New Zealand's tariff schedule is published in the **Working Tariff Document (WTD)**, maintained by the New Zealand Customs Service. Steel products fall under **Section XV, Chapters 72–83 (Base metals and articles of base metal)**. [[Source: NZ Customs — Working Tariff Document](https://www.customs.govt.nz/business/tariffs/working-tariff-document/)]

The relevant HS chapter for raw and semi-finished steel is **Chapter 72 (Iron and steel)**, which includes:

| HS Code Range | Product Description |
|---------------|---------------------|
| 7207 | Semi-finished products of iron or non-alloy steel (ingots, billets, blooms, slabs) |
| 7208–7212 | Flat-rolled products of iron or non-alloy steel (hot-rolled, cold-rolled, plated/coated) |
| 7213–7214 | Bars and rods of iron or non-alloy steel (hot-rolled) |
| 7215 | Other bars and rods of iron or non-alloy steel |
| 7216 | Angles, shapes and sections of iron or non-alloy steel |

### Tariff Rate Assessment

The specific duty rates are contained in a downloadable **Section XV PDF (1.14 MB, effective 1 January 2026)** or via the **BorderWise** searchable tariff portal linked from the Customs website. The WTD page itself does not display inline rates. [[Source: NZ Customs WTD](https://www.customs.govt.nz/business/tariffs/working-tariff-document/)]

**Key contextual knowledge based on NZ trade policy:**

- **New Zealand generally applies low or zero tariffs on raw steel imports**, consistent with its open-trade posture and dependence on imported raw materials (NZ has one domestic steelmaker — New Zealand Steel/Glenbrook — which does not produce all grades and sections required).
- Many steel HS codes under Chapter 72 carry **0% duty** as raw material inputs essential to downstream manufacturing, including fabrication.
- New Zealand's free trade agreements (CER with Australia, CPTPP, China FTA) provide additional preferential rate pathways for major steel supplying countries.

**⚠ Gap**: The precise duty rates per HS subheading (7207.11–7216.50) could not be confirmed from the browsed pages, as the data resides in the downloadable PDF. The BorderWise portal link on the Customs page would provide the searchable database. **Effective tariff impact on local fabricator margins is assessed as minimal** given the expectation of zero or near-zero raw steel tariffs, but this requires verification from the actual tariff schedule.

## (e) Top 3 Strategic Risks and Opportunities for Fabricators

### RISKS

#### 1. Supply Chain Concentration on Single Domestic Steel Producer
New Zealand has **one primary domestic steelmaker** — New Zealand Steel (Glenbrook, owned by BlueScope Steel, Australia). Pacific Steel (also BlueScope-owned) is the sole domestic rebar manufacturer. This creates extreme supply chain concentration:
- Any disruption at Glenbrook (e.g., the 2024 fire that damaged the steelmaking facility) directly constrains the entire domestic fabrication industry.
- Fabricators are heavily reliant on **imported steel** (primarily from China, Korea, Japan) for structural sections, plate, and hollow sections not produced domestically.
- This concentration creates **price volatility risk** and **lead-time vulnerability**.

#### 2. Project Pipeline Volatility and Political Cycle Dependence
The NZ construction sector is highly sensitive to government infrastructure spending decisions, which fluctuate with political cycles:
- The current National-led coalition government (elected 2023) has signalled a **reprioritisation of infrastructure spending**, with some projects being rescoped, deferred, or cancelled.
- The post-CRL environment (post-2026) may see a significant **drop in megaproject demand**, leaving fabricators with capacity but reduced pipeline.
- Commercial construction activity has softened with higher interest rates through 2023–2024.

#### 3. Import Competition and Margin Compression
While raw steel tariffs are low/zero (reducing input cost disadvantage), this same openness means **fabricated structural steel products can also be imported** from lower-cost Asian manufacturers:
- Chinese and Vietnamese fabricators can deliver pre-fabricated structural steel assemblies at prices that challenge NZ fabricators on larger projects.
- Rising compliance costs (earthquake standards, seismic design requirements under NZS 3404) add cost for local fabricators that offshore competitors may not face to the same degree.
- [[Source: SCNZ — NZS 3404 Standards Update, May 2024 — https://www.scnz.org]]

### OPPORTUNITIES

#### 1. Seismic Performance Advantage and "Made in NZ" Premium
New Zealand's unique seismic requirements create a **natural competitive moat** for local fabricators:
- NZ-designed and fabricated structural steel must meet stringent seismic performance standards that offshore fabricators may struggle to demonstrate compliance with.
- Specifiers and engineers increasingly preference locally fabricated and traceable steel for critical structural applications, particularly post-Kaikōura earthquake lessons.

#### 2. Government Procurement Policy Favouring Local Industry
New Zealand government procurement policies increasingly emphasise **local content and broader economic benefits** in major infrastructure contracts:
- The government's emphasis on regional economic development and local job creation can favour NZ-based fabricators in tender evaluation.
- The CRL's explicit sourcing of ~25,000 tonnes from Pacific Steel demonstrates this dynamic in practice.

#### 3. Renewable Energy Infrastructure Pipeline
New Zealand's energy transition is creating new demand for structural steel in:
- **Wind farm** towers and foundations (multiple projects in consenting/construction)
- **Transmission infrastructure** upgrades (Transpower's national grid investment programme)
- **Geothermal** and **solar** installations

This emerging pipeline could partially offset any decline in transport infrastructure spending post-CRL.
