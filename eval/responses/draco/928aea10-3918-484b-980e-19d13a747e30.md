# UCC Acceptance and Cure Analysis: Vendor v. Buyer — Summary Judgment Memorandum

## I. Issue Presented

Whether Buyer rightfully rejected 500 reams of legal-size copy paper on November 30, 2024 — twenty-two days after delivery and after consuming 180 reams and customizing 320 more with company letterhead — or whether Buyer's conduct constitutes acceptance under UCC Article 2, such that Vendor's proposed cure (exchange of remaining stock within five business days) must be honored on summary judgment.

## II. Governing Legal Framework

### A. Conforming Goods and the Perfect Tender Rule (§§ 2-106, 2-601)

UCC § 2-106(2) defines goods as "conforming" when they are "in accordance with the obligations under the contract" [1]. The purchase order specified letter size; the contract used the undefined term "standard office paper." This creates a threshold ambiguity. If the purchase order controls as the more specific expression of the parties' intent, legal-size paper is non-conforming. If the contract's ambiguous "standard office paper" language controls, legal-size paper could arguably conform — since legal size (8.5" × 14") is a standard office paper format.

Under the perfect tender rule (UCC § 2-601), if goods "fail in any respect to conform to the contract, the buyer may (a) reject the whole; or (b) accept the whole; or (c) accept any commercial unit or units and reject the rest" [2]. However, this right is not unlimited — it is conditioned on timely action and extinguished by acceptance.

### B. Acceptance of Goods (§ 2-606)

UCC § 2-606 provides three independent bases for acceptance [3][4]:

1. **§ 2-606(1)(a):** The buyer signifies that the goods are conforming or that they will retain them despite non-conformity, after a reasonable opportunity to inspect.
2. **§ 2-606(1)(b):** The buyer fails to make an effective rejection — but only after a reasonable opportunity to inspect.
3. **§ 2-606(1)(c):** The buyer does "any act inconsistent with the seller's ownership."

Critically, **§ 2-606(2) states: "Acceptance of a part of any commercial unit is acceptance of that entire unit"** [3]. All 500 reams were delivered as one commercial order; using 180 reams likely constitutes acceptance of the entire 500-ream unit.

### C. Rejection Requirements (§ 2-602)

Rejection must occur "within a reasonable time after delivery or tender" and requires "seasonable notification" to the seller [5]. Failure to reject promptly constitutes a waiver. Under § 2-605, a buyer who fails to particularize defects in a rejection notice may lose the right to rely on those defects.

### D. Right to Cure (§ 2-508)

The seller's right to cure operates through two pathways [6][7]:

- **§ 2-508(1) — Pre-deadline cure:** If the tender is rejected and the contract time has not expired, the seller may seasonably notify the buyer of intent to cure and make a conforming delivery within the contract time.
- **§ 2-508(2) — Post-deadline cure:** Even after the deadline, the seller may cure if it "had reasonable grounds to believe" the non-conforming goods "would be acceptable with or without money allowance." The seller must seasonably notify the buyer and receives a "further reasonable time to substitute a conforming tender."

### E. Revocation of Acceptance (§ 2-608)

A buyer who has accepted goods may revoke only if the non-conformity "substantially impairs the value" of the goods, and only if the revocation occurs within a reasonable time "before any substantial change in condition of the goods not caused by their own defects" [5].

### F. Notice of Breach After Acceptance (§ 2-607)

A buyer who has accepted must notify the seller of breach "within a reasonable time" or be barred from remedy [5].

## III. Application to the Facts

### A. The Goods Were Non-Conforming

On the present facts, the legal-size paper likely fails to conform. The purchase order specified letter size, and even though the contract used the broader term "standard office paper," the purchase order's specific dimensional requirement controls as the more precise expression of buyer expectations. This is especially true where — as here — the difference between letter size (8.5" × 11") and legal size (8.5" × 14") has practical significance for office printing. The goods thus trigger the perfect tender rule under § 2-601.

**However, non-conformity alone does not entitle the Buyer to reject.** The right to reject is extinguished by acceptance.

### B. Buyer's Conduct Constitutes Acceptance Under All Three Bases

**1. Failure to Make an Effective Rejection — § 2-606(1)(b):** The office manager accepted delivery on November 8 and stored the goods without objection. No complaint was raised for 22 days. Paper size is immediately apparent on visual inspection — there is no latent defect argument. A "reasonable time" to reject a visible size discrepancy is measured in days, not weeks. The 22-day silence alone constitutes failure to make an effective rejection.

**2. Conduct Inconsistent with Seller's Ownership — § 2-606(1)(c):** Buyer's staff consumed 180 reams over three weeks for internal drafts and overflow projects. This is not de minimis use — it represents 36% of the total delivery put into active commercial use. More critically, Buyer printed company letterhead and logos on the remaining 320 reams (64% of the delivery). Printing on paper is an irreversible act of ownership — it permanently commits the goods to Buyer's business use and destroys their marketability as blank paper. Courts consistently hold that such use constitutes an act "inconsistent with the seller's ownership" [4][8].

**3. Acceptance of Part = Acceptance of Whole — § 2-606(2):** Since the 500 reams were a single commercial unit under one purchase order, acceptance of the 180 reams consumed is acceptance of the entire unit [3].

### C. Buyer's Rejection Was Untimely

Even if the court found that acceptance had not yet occurred by conduct, Buyer's November 30 rejection was untimely. Under § 2-602, rejection must occur within a reasonable time after delivery. For goods with an obvious size discrepancy, 22 days — during which the goods were extensively used — far exceeds any reasonable rejection window. This delay, combined with the failure to notify Vendor of any problem, constitutes waiver under § 2-605.

### D. Revocation of Acceptance Is Unavailable

Even if Buyer could reframe its November 30 action as revocation of acceptance under § 2-608, two barriers exist:

1. **Substantial impairment:** Buyer claims the wrong size disrupted workflow and wasted staff time, but cannot quantify hours lost. Wrong-size paper is inconvenient, not value-destroying. Legal-size paper retains its essential function as copy paper. The non-conformity does not "substantially impair the value" of the goods under § 2-608(1).

2. **Substantial change in condition:** Buyer printed letterhead and logos on 320 reams — an irreversible alteration. Revocation is barred under § 2-608(2) because it must occur "before any substantial change in condition of the goods not caused by their own defects." The printing was not caused by the size defect; it was Buyer's voluntary act. This independently bars revocation for the 320 customized reams.

### E. Vendor's Cure Right Is Valid and Timely

Even assuming *arguendo* that Buyer's rejection was timely and acceptance had not occurred, Vendor's cure right under § 2-508(2) is independently compelling:

1. **Reasonable grounds to believe goods acceptable:** The contract stated only "standard office paper" without defining dimensions. Legal-size paper is a standard office product with equivalent market value. The ambiguous contract language gave Vendor reasonable grounds to believe the goods would be acceptable, satisfying the § 2-508(2) threshold [6][7].

2. **Seasonable notification:** Vendor offered to cure on November 30, the same day Buyer demanded return of payment. This is seasonable notification.

3. **Further reasonable time:** The proposed five-business-day exchange window is commercially reasonable for a paper order. No evidence suggests Vendor was unable or unwilling to perform [6][7].

4. **Vendor's incomplete delivery records are irrelevant:** The missing packing slips for four other accounts go to Vendor's recordkeeping, not to the legal question of whether cure was timely and in good faith. The specific delivery to Buyer is undisputed — the dispute is about paper dimensions, not whether delivery occurred.

### F. Buyer Cannot Refuse a Valid Cure Offer

Under UCC principles, a buyer cannot refuse a valid, timely offer to cure to escape a contract. The buyer's obligation is to act in good faith [7]. Buyer's blanket refusal to accept the cure — demanding instead a full return of payment — is inconsistent with good faith where the goods have been extensively used and modified.

## IV. Summary Judgment Analysis

### A. Vendor's Motion for Summary Judgment: Likely Granted

On the undisputed facts, no genuine issue of material fact exists regarding acceptance. The record establishes:

- Delivery accepted by Buyer's authorized agent (office manager).
- 22-day delay before any objection (visible defect).
- 180 reams consumed in commercial use.
- 320 reams permanently customized with Buyer's proprietary printing.
- No quantifiable damages alleged.
- Vendor's same-day cure offer, commercially reasonable in scope and timeline.

All three independent bases for acceptance under § 2-606 are satisfied. Revocation under § 2-608 is barred by both the lack of substantial impairment and the irreversible alteration of goods. Vendor's cure right under § 2-508(2) is independently valid. A court would likely grant Vendor's motion for summary judgment (or deny Buyer's), holding that Buyer accepted the goods and must honor Vendor's proposed cure.

### B. Buyer's Limited Remedies After Acceptance

Buyer is not without remedy entirely. Under § 2-607, Buyer may pursue damages for breach of warranty under § 2-714 — but only if Buyer gave timely notice of breach, which is itself doubtful given the 22-day delay. Even if notice were timely, damages under § 2-714 are limited to "the difference between the value of the goods as accepted and as warranted." Given that legal-size paper has equivalent market value and functional utility, this difference would be nominal. Buyer's unquantified claims of "workflow disruption" and "wasted staff time" are consequential damages that would require proof under § 2-715 and are unlikely to succeed without quantification.

---

## V. Conclusion

**Buyer did not rightfully reject and cannot prevail on summary judgment.** The undisputed facts establish acceptance under all three prongs of UCC § 2-606. The 22-day delay in objection, combined with the consumption of 180 reams and permanent customization of 320 reams, constitutes conduct so clearly inconsistent with rejection that no reasonable jury could find otherwise. Vendor's proposed cure under § 2-508(2) is timely, reasonable, and supported by the contract's ambiguous "standard office paper" language. Buyer must accept the cure or, alternatively, may pursue limited damages under § 2-714 — though such damages would be negligible given the equivalent value and functionality of the goods delivered.

The appropriate outcome on summary judgment is: **Buyer accepted the goods and is bound by the contract, subject to Vendor's right to substitute letter-size paper for the remaining stock within five business days.**
