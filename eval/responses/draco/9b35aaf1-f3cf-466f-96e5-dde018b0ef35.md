# The Neoanalyst–Oralist Divide in Homeric Scholarship: The Iliad and the Epic Cycle

## Executive Summary

The relationship between the *Iliad* and the Epic Cycle has become one of the most contested questions in contemporary Homeric scholarship, structured around a methodological fault line between neoanalysts ( Jonathan Burgess, Bruno Currie, Christos Tsagalis) and oralists (Gregory Nagy, John Miles Foley, Egbert Bakker). Neoanalysts argue that the *Iliad* poet knew and deliberately adapted narrative motifs from poems in the Cycle tradition (especially the *Cypria* and *Aethiopis*), while oralists maintain that shared motifs reflect common inheritance from a vast oral traditional repertoire rather than literary allusion to fixed texts. Recent scholarship has increasingly sought synthesis, but fundamental disagreements persist over how to read "Cypria motifs" in *Iliad* 1 and 24, the nature of Achilles' foreknowledge of his death, the meaning of the Zeus–Thetis scene, and the evidentiary weight of fragment collections, scholia, and new papyri.

---

## 1. The Methodological Divide: Foundational Positions

### 1.1 Neoanalysis: From Schadewaldt to Burgess, Currie, and Tsagalis

Neoanalysis, a methodology originating with Wolfgang Schadewaldt and further developed by scholars such as Wolfgang Kullmann, holds that the *Iliad* poet deliberately engaged with, adapted, and "transformed" narrative motifs attested in what later became fixed as the cyclic epics. The key claim is one of **directionality**: motifs appearing in both the *Iliad* and the Cycle are argued to have originated in pre-*Iliadic* narrative traditions and to have been borrowed *by* the *Iliad* poet—not the reverse.

Jonathan Burgess, in *The Tradition of the Trojan War in Homer and the Epic Cycle* (2001, rev. ed. 2015), argues that the *Cypria* originally narrated the entire Trojan War and that the *Iliad* poet composed with awareness of this broader narrative framework. Burgess draws heavily on the Bernabé fragment collection (*Poetarum epicorum Graecorum testimonia et fragmenta*, 1987) and on the Proclean summaries preserved in the Venetus A and other manuscripts to reconstruct the narrative content of the Cycle and demonstrate the *Iliad*'s intertextual engagement with it.

Bruno Currie, in *Homer's Allusive Art* (2016), pushes neoanalysis further by applying modern literary theories of allusion and intertextuality to Homeric poetry. Currie argues that passages in the *Iliad* contain sophisticated "corrections" and "revisions" of cyclic narratives—particularly concerning Achilles' fate—suggesting a poet who expected his audience to recognize the alluded-to material.

Christos Tsagalis, in works including *The Oral Palimpsest: Exploring Intertextuality in the Homeric Epics* (2008) and subsequent studies, occupies a mediating position. Tsagalis introduces the concept of the "oral palimpsest" to describe how oral-traditional poetry layers multiple versions of the same narrative, creating a palimpsestic texture that functions like literary allusion but operates within the mechanics of oral composition.

### 1.2 Oral-Formulaic Theory: Nagy, Foley, and Bakker

Oral-formulaic theory, originating with Milman Parry and Albert Lord, holds that the *Iliad* and *Odyssey* are products of a long oral-traditional compositional process rather than the work of a single author writing with knowledge of fixed texts. The key scholars in the contemporary debate have refined this position in distinct ways:

**Gregory Nagy** (in *The Best of the Achaeans* [1979], *Poetry as Performance* [1996], and *Homer the Preclassic* [2010]) argues for an evolutionary model of Homeric composition, in which the epics crystallized gradually through a multi-generational process of performance and recomposition. Nagy uses comparative Indo-European poetics to demonstrate that key Homeric themes—including the "short life" motif of Achilles and the type-scene of divine intercession—are deeply rooted in inherited Indo-European narrative patterns, not borrowings from the cyclic epics. For Nagy, the Cycle epics are not sources *for* Homer but parallel crystallizations of the same oral tradition, some potentially later than the *Iliad*.

**John Miles Foley** (in *Homer's Traditional Art* [1999] and *Immanent Art* [1991]) developed the concept of **"traditional referentiality"**—the idea that formulaic phrases, type-scenes, and narrative patterns carry metonymic force, invoking the full weight of the tradition behind them. Foley argued that what neoanalysts call "allusion" to the Cycle is better understood as the natural operation of traditional referentiality within an oral-traditional system.

**Egbert Bakker** (in *Poetry in Speech: Orality and Homeric Discourse* [1997] and *The Meaning of Meat and the Structure of the Odyssey* [2013) approaches Homeric discourse through linguistics and discourse analysis, emphasizing that the *Iliad*'s compositional units reflect cognitive and performative patterns characteristic of oral poetry. Bakker views the appearance of "Cypria motifs" in the *Iliad* as evidence not of literary borrowing but of the poem's participation in a shared oral-traditional repertoire.

## 2. The "Cypria Motifs" in Iliad 1 and 24

### 2.1 Iliad 1: The Opening and the Plan of Zeus

The opening of the *Iliad* presupposes a narrative context—the wrath of Apollo provoked by Agamemnon's seizure of Chryses' daughter, the resulting plague, and the quarrel between Agamemnon and Achilles—that aligns closely with material attributed to the *Cypria* by Proclus's summary. The *Cypria* narrated the origins of the Trojan War (the Judgment of Paris, the abduction of Helen) and events leading into the *Iliad*'s action.

**Neoanalyst interpretation:** Burgess and Currie argue that the *Iliad* poet composed his opening with full awareness of the *Cypria* narrative and deliberately selected a starting point that both presupposes and transforms that material. The "plan of Zeus" (Διὸς δ' ἐτελείετο βουλή, *Il.* 1.5) is read as a pointed reference to the larger cyclic framework in which Zeus's plan encompasses the entire Trojan War, including the death of Achilles, as narrated in the *Aethiopis*. The *Iliad*'s opening thus signals its awareness of the broader narrative arc.

**Oralist interpretation:** Nagy and Foley counter that the "plan of Zeus" is an inherited traditional theme, not a literary allusion to a fixed cyclic text. The formulaic nature of the opening lines—the invocation to the Muse, the specification of wrath—reflects conventional oral-traditional compositional patterns. What neoanalysts identify as "Cypria motifs" are, for oralists, simply the common narrative inheritance of the Trojan War tradition, available to any singer in performance.

### 2.2 Iliad 24: The Ransom of Hector and the Shadow of Achilles' Death

*Iliad* 24 concludes with Hector's funeral, but the broader narrative context—the coming death of Achilles at the hands of Paris and Apollo (as narrated in the *Aethiopis*) and the subsequent fall of Troy—casts a long shadow. The famous alternative final couplet preserved in some traditions (introducing the Amazon Penthesileia as a bridge to the *Aethiopis*) is a key piece of evidence.

**Neoanalyst interpretation:** Currie reads *Iliad* 24 as "alluding" to the death of Achilles and events of the *Aethiopis* through a technique of "suppressed narration"—the poet deliberately withholds what the audience knows will happen, creating powerful dramatic irony. The mourning of Achilles over Patroclus in Book 18 and the foreboding speeches throughout Books 19–24 are seen as laden with awareness of Achilles' imminent death in the cyclic tradition.

**Oralist interpretation:** Bakker and Nagy argue that Achilles' awareness of his own death reflects not allusion to a fixed cyclic text but the operation of a traditional theme rooted in Indo-European poetics. Nagy has demonstrated parallels between the "short-lived hero" motif in Homeric epic and cognate traditions in Indic, Celtic, and Germanic poetry. The "shadow" of Achilles' death is inherent in the traditional narrative material itself, not a literary device deployed against a known text.

## 3. Achilles' Awareness of His Own Death

This is perhaps the single most contested passage in the debate. Achilles' foreknowledge of his death is expressed at multiple points in the *Iliad* (most prominently at 18.95–121 and 9.410–416).

**Neoanalysts** argue that the specificity and literary sophistication of Achilles' death-foreknowledge passages point to a poet composing with full awareness of the *Aethiopis* narrative (where Achilles is killed by Paris and Apollo after killing Memnon and the Amazon Penthesileia). Currie particularly argues that the *Iliad*'s handling of Achilles' choice between a long, inglorious life and a short, glorious one presupposes an audience familiar with the "Memnon narrative" from the cyclic tradition.

**Oralists** respond that the "two fates" motif is a pan-Indo-European traditional pattern. Nagy's comparative work traces it to the Vedic figure of the warrior who chooses early death and immortal fame. For oralists, the fact that this motif appears in the *Iliad* and in cyclic epics does not establish directional borrowing; rather, both are independent crystallizations of the same deep traditional material.

## 4. The Zeus–Thetis Scene (Iliad 1.493–530)

The scene in which Thetis supplicates Zeus on behalf of Achilles is a critical nexus for the debate. Thetis reminds Zeus of a past service she performed—helping him when the other Olympians rebelled—and asks him to honor Achilles by causing the Greeks to suffer until they restore his honor.

**Neoanalysts** see this scene as evidence of the *Iliad* poet's engagement with a broader mythological tradition. The rebellion of the Olympians is not narrated in the *Iliad* but is known from other traditions; its invocation here suggests the poet expected his audience to know this background. Burgess connects this to the wider cyclic tradition in which Thetis plays a more prominent role, particularly in narratives about Achilles' upbringing and divine connections.

**Oralists** interpret the Zeus–Thetis scene through the lens of type-scenes and comparative mythology. The "goddess's supplication of Zeus" pattern, the motif of divine favors exchanged, and Thetis's role as divine mother intervening for her mortal son are all attested across multiple Indo-European traditions. Nagy particularly connects the scene to patterns of divine reciprocity found in Vedic and Near Eastern poetry. For oralists, the scene exemplifies how traditional referentiality operates: the formulaic language invokes the entire weight of the oral tradition without requiring knowledge of any specific fixed text.

## 5. Competing Uses of Evidence

### 5.1 The Bernabé Fragments

Alberto Bernabé's 1987 edition of the epic fragments (*Poetarum epicorum Graecorum testimonia et fragmenta*) and Martin West's 2003 *Greek Epic Fragments* provide the primary textual basis for reconstructing the cyclic epics.

**Neoanalysts** use these fragments as evidence for the narrative content of pre-*Iliadic* poems, arguing that motifs shared between the fragments and the *Iliad* demonstrate the *Iliad* poet's familiarity with cyclic narratives. Burgess's reconstruction of the *Cypria* relies heavily on Bernabé's ordering and interpretation of the fragments.

**Oralists** caution against treating the fragments as evidence of fixed, pre-*Iliadic* texts. They argue that the fragmentary remains cannot establish the relative chronology of the *Iliad* and the cyclic epics, and that the "stitching" visible in the Bernabé fragments (e.g., narrative links designed to connect one cyclic poem to the next) may reflect later editorial activity rather than original compositional relationships.

### 5.2 The Venetus A Scholia

The Venetus A manuscript (digitized by Harvard's Center for Hellenic Studies in 2007) preserves the A scholia derived from the *Viermännerkommentar* of Aristonicus, Didymus, Herodian, and Nicanor. These scholia transmit Alexandrian critical commentary on the *Iliad*, including references to variant readings and alternative narrative traditions.

**Neoanalysts** point to scholia that reference cyclic material—particularly those that note narrative details not in the *Iliad* but known from cyclic epics—as evidence that ancient scholars recognized the *Iliad*'s engagement with a broader tradition.

**Oralists** note that the scholia reflect Alexandrian editorial practices, which imposed textual fixity on what had been a fluid oral tradition. The variant traditions recorded in the scholia are, for oralists, evidence of the multiformity inherent in oral composition, not evidence that the *Iliad* poet "alluded" to fixed cyclic texts.

### 5.3 Comparative Indo-European Poetics

This is the domain where oralists claim the strongest evidentiary basis. Nagy, Foley, and Bakker have all drawn extensively on comparative work by Calvert Watkins, Martin West, and others to demonstrate that Homeric narrative patterns—divine assemblies, supplication scenes, the "short-lived hero" motif—are cognate with patterns in Vedic, Celtic, Germanic, and other Indo-European poetic traditions.

Neoanalysts do not reject Indo-European comparative evidence but argue that it is insufficient to explain the *specificity* of the narrative correspondences between the *Iliad* and the Cycle. Currie contends that while the general themes may be Indo-European, the precise narrative parallels (e.g., the specific sequence of events in Achilles' death narrative) point to direct engagement with cyclic material.

## 6. P.Oxy. 3829 and Digital Papyrology Since 2000

P.Oxy. 3829 is a papyrus from Oxyrhynchus preserving fragments of cyclic epic material. Its significance lies in providing direct textual evidence for the circulation and textual form of cyclic epics in the papyrological record, independent of the Proclean summaries.

The digital analysis of Homeric papyri—facilitated by projects such as the Homer Multitext Project (based at the Center for Hellenic Studies and the University of Houston)—has transformed the evidentiary landscape. The Homer Multitext provides digital access to Venetus A scholia, papyri, and other manuscripts, enabling systematic comparison of variant readings and narrative traditions.

**Impact on the debate:** The new papyrological evidence has complicated both positions. For neoanalysts, the papyri confirm that cyclic epics circulated as written texts from an early date, supporting the plausibility of textual engagement by the *Iliad* poet. For oralists, the multiformity revealed by the papyri—the significant textual variation across different copies and periods—supports the model of a fluid oral tradition in which no single "fixed" text served as the source for another.

The digital availability of these materials has also enabled more precise analysis of the Proclean summaries and their relationship to the papyrological evidence. Scholars can now compare the narrative sequence in Proclus with the actual fragmentary text, assessing the reliability of the summaries and the nature of the "stitching" between cyclic poems.

## 7. The Current Status of Synthesis

The most significant development in recent scholarship has been the emergence of attempts at synthesis between the neoanalyst and oralist positions. Key works include:

- **Tsagalis's "oral palimpsest" model** (*The Oral Palimpsest*, 2008): This attempts to bridge the gap by arguing that oral-traditional poetry naturally produces intertextual effects through the layering of multiple versions of the same narrative. The "palimpsest" metaphor allows for something resembling allusion without requiring fixed texts.

- **Burgess's revised edition** (2015): Burgess has increasingly acknowledged oral-traditional dimensions while maintaining the core neoanalyst claim of the *Iliad* poet's awareness of cyclic narrative material.

- **Recent collected volumes**: Works such as *Homeric Contexts: Neoanalysis and the Interpretation of Oral Poetry* (ed. Tsagalis, 2012) and contributions to the *Trends in Classics* series have explicitly sought dialogue between the camps.

- **The Homer Multitext Project**: By providing a digital framework that presents the *Iliad* as a multitext—a fluid entity existing in multiple versions rather than a single fixed text—this project has created a methodological space that accommodates both the neoanalyst emphasis on specific narrative correspondences and the oralist emphasis on multiformity.

However, a fundamental incompatibility remains: neoanalysts require some degree of textual fixity in the pre-*Iliadic* tradition to sustain their model of allusion, while oralists maintain that the evidence points to a fundamentally multiform tradition. Whether the "oral palimpsest" model or some other framework can fully resolve this tension remains an open question. The growing consensus, articulated most clearly by Tsagalis and reflected in the work of younger scholars, is that the *Iliad* poet composed within an oral tradition *saturated* with the narrative material that later crystallized into the cyclic epics—but whether this saturation amounts to "knowledge of the *Cypria*" or "participation in the Trojan War tradition" remains the irreducible interpretive crux.
