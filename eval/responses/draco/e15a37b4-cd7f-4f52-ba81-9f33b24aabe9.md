# The Grounded Theory Split: A Comparative Analysis of Methodological Divergence, Constructivist Resolution, and Contemporary Dominance

## Executive Summary

The methodological fracture between Barney Glaser and Anselm Strauss—escalating after their joint 1967 formulation of Grounded Theory (GT)—represents one of the most consequential disputes in qualitative research. Glaser's *Theoretical Sensitivity* (1978) and Strauss & Corbin's *Basics of Qualitative Research* (1990) diverged sharply on three axes: the role of pre-existing theory, the legitimacy of structured coding procedures, and the use of verification. Kathy Charmaz's constructivist reformulation (2006) reframed these tensions by rejecting the positivist assumption shared by both original camps. Today, Charmaz's constructivist GT is the dominant approach in contemporary qualitative research training, supplanting both the Glaserian and Straussian traditions.

---

## 1. Origins: The 1967 Synthesis

Glaser and Strauss's *The Discovery of Grounded Theory* (1967) was itself a methodological hybrid. Glaser brought Columbia University positivism—training under Paul Lazarsfeld's rigorous quantitative methods—while Strauss brought University of Chicago symbolic interactionism, with its emphasis on meaning-making, social processes, and interpretive depth. Their collaboration produced a methodology that used the **constant comparative method** to inductively generate theory from data, deliberately contrasting with the dominant hypothetico-deductive model of the era. The 1967 work was animated by a shared mission: to legitimate qualitative research within a positivist-dominated sociological establishment [1].

This foundational alliance, however, papered over deep epistemological differences. Once the two scholars began developing the methodology independently after Strauss moved to the University of California, San Francisco, the latent tensions became irreconcilable.

## 2. The Divergence: Glaser (1978) vs. Strauss & Corbin (1990)

### 2.1 The Role of Pre-existing Theoretical Frameworks in Coding

**Glaser's Position (*Theoretical Sensitivity*, 1978):** Glaser argued that researchers should avoid reading substantive (domain-specific) literature prior to data collection and analysis. The concern was that prior theoretical knowledge would contaminate the researcher's perception, leading them to "see" pre-existing concepts in the data rather than allowing genuinely new categories to emerge. For Glaser, theoretical sensitivity was not about being theoretically informed but about cultivating an openness to what the data might reveal. He acknowledged that researchers could not be blank slates but insisted that disciplined suppression of prior frameworks was essential to the emergent logic of GT [1].

Glaser did permit engagement with theoretical literature from *other* substantive domains (using theoretical sampling of concepts), but drew a hard line against reading within the domain under study. The core principle was **emergence**: theory must arise from data without being shaped by a priori conceptual scaffolding.

**Strauss and Corbin's Position (*Basics of Qualitative Research*, 1990):** Strauss and Corbin explicitly endorsed the use of pre-existing theoretical frameworks and prior literature as resources for informing analysis. They argued that researchers inevitably bring theoretical knowledge to the research encounter, and that systematic procedures could channel this knowledge productively rather than suppress it. Their approach encouraged researchers to draw on existing theory to sensitize their coding, formulate interview questions, and interpret emerging categories [1].

This difference reflected each founder's epistemological starting point. Glaser's Columbia positivism assumed that an objective reality could be discovered if the researcher maintained sufficient neutrality. Strauss's Chicago interactionism assumed that the researcher was always already a meaning-making participant in the research process—so pretending to theoretical virginity was both impossible and counterproductive.

### 2.2 The Legitimacy of "Forcing" Data into Categories

This was the most heated point of contention.

**Glaser's Charge of "Forcing":** Glaser accused Strauss and Corbin of designing procedures that compelled researchers to force data into predetermined structures. The *axial coding paradigm*—which required analysts to identify conditions, context, action/interactional strategies, and consequences for every category—was, in Glaser's view, a Procrustean bed. By mandating that every phenomenon be decomposed according to this fixed template, the procedure ensured that data would be made to fit the model rather than the model arising from the data. Glaser wrote *Basics of Grounded Theory Analysis: Emergence vs. Forcing* (1992) as a direct, polemical response, in which he argued that Strauss and Corbin's approach was "not grounded theory" at all but rather a form of qualitative content analysis dressed in GT terminology [1].

For Glaser, the cardinal sin was **conceptual forcing**: imposing external logic on data that should be allowed to speak for itself. His alternative was to let theoretical codes (like the six C's: causes, contexts, contingencies, consequences, covariances, and conditions) emerge naturally through the constant comparative method, used only when they proved their relevance.

**Strauss and Corbin's Defense:** Strauss and Corbin maintained that their coding paradigm was not a fixed template but a heuristic device—a thinking tool to help researchers see complexity and relationships they might otherwise miss. They argued that structure did not preclude emergence; rather, it disciplined the emergent process, preventing the unchecked subjectivity that Glaser's approach risked. Their procedures were intended to systematize analysis in a way that could be taught, replicated, and defended to skeptics of qualitative research—an important consideration given the ongoing pressure to demonstrate methodological rigor [1].

### 2.3 The Use of Verification Procedures

**Glaser's Position:** Glaser rejected formal verification procedures within GT. For him, the constant comparative method was both generative and self-correcting: as categories were compared across incidents, the fit between data and theory was continuously tested organically. Adding separate verification steps was unnecessary and violated the emergent logic. The theory's credibility derived from its fit with data, its relevance to participants, its workability in explaining behavior, and its modifiability—not from hypothesis-testing procedures imported from positivist methodology [1].

**Strauss and Corbin's Position:** Strauss and Corbin introduced conditional/consequential matrix analysis and other structured techniques that functioned as quasi-verification procedures. They advocated testing emerging theory against data systematically, examining negative cases, and using structured questioning to probe category boundaries. While not identical to quantitative hypothesis testing, these procedures moved GT closer to a quasi-deductive model that Glaser found anathematic. Strauss and Corbin saw this as responsible rigor; Glaser saw it as methodological betrayal [1].

## 3. Comparative Summary Table

| Dimension | Glaser (1978) | Strauss & Corbin (1990) |
|---|---|---|
| **Epistemological stance** | Objectivist/positivist | Modified objectivist with interactionist influences |
| **Pre-existing literature** | Avoid substantive literature prior to analysis | Use literature as sensitizing resource |
| **Coding structure** | Open coding only; theoretical codes emerge naturally | Open, axial, and selective coding with prescribed paradigm |
| **Data categorization** | Categories emerge; never force | Structured heuristics guide categorization |
| **Verification** | Built into constant comparison; no separate step | Conditional matrix, systematic probing, negative case analysis |
| **Researcher role** | Neutral discoverer of emergent theory | Active analyst using systematic procedures |
| **Theoretical outcome** | Substantive or formal theory emerges from data | Theory constructed through iterative structured analysis |

## 4. Charmaz's Constructivist Reformulation (2006)

### 4.1 The Epistemological Shift

Kathy Charmaz's *Constructing Grounded Theory* (2006) represented not merely a compromise between Glaser and Strauss/Corbin but a **paradigmatic reframing** of the entire enterprise. Charmaz argued that both original camps shared an unstated positivist assumption: that data exist "out there" waiting to be discovered, and that theory can objectively represent that reality. For Glaser, theory "emerged" from data like a fossil being excavated; for Strauss and Corbin, theory was "built" through systematic procedures, but still mapped onto a real-world structure [1][2].

Charmaz's constructivist GT rejected this shared premise. Drawing on constructivist and interpretivist epistemology—which holds that reality is multiple, subjective, and socially constructed—Charmaz reconceived GT as a process of **co-construction** between researcher and participants. Data are not neutral observations but products of the interaction between researcher questions and participant responses. The resulting theory does not "discover" or "represent" an objective reality; it offers one interpretive account among possible alternatives [1][2].

### 4.2 Resolving the Tensions

Charmaz's constructivist GT dissolved several of the Glaser-Strauss antinomies:

- **Pre-existing theory:** Rather than banning prior knowledge (Glaser) or systematizing its use (Strauss/Corbin), Charmaz acknowledged that the researcher's theoretical background, disciplinary training, and social position inevitably shape what they see. The goal was not to eliminate or control this influence but to be reflexive about it—making the researcher's standpoint explicit rather than pretending it doesn't exist.

- **Forcing vs. emergence:** By reconceiving theory as constructed rather than discovered, Charmaz made the "forcing" debate moot. If there is no single correct theory waiting to emerge, then the question becomes one of interpretive adequacy rather than fidelity to data. Researchers could use flexible coding procedures (including adapted versions of axial coding) without worrying about "forcing" data, because the criteria were coherence, richness, and resonance rather than objective correspondence.

- **Verification:** Charmaz replaced verification with credibility criteria drawn from constructivist epistemology: reflexivity, member checking, thick description, and attention to negative cases used interpretively rather than as falsification tools.

### 4.3 What Charmaz Introduced That Was New

Charmaz's approach went beyond resolving the Glaser-Strauss debate; it introduced a fundamentally different epistemological orientation:

1. **Reflexivity as methodological virtue:** Where both Glaser and Strauss/Corbin saw researcher subjectivity as a problem to manage, Charmaz positioned it as an analytical resource.
2. **Situated knowledge:** Drawing on feminist epistemology (particularly Haraway and Harding), Charmaz insisted that all knowledge claims are situated and partial.
3. **Action-oriented coding:** Charmaz preserved systematic coding procedures (open, focused, and theoretical coding) but framed them as interpretive acts rather than discoveries or structures.
4. **Emphasis on participant meaning:** Constructivist GT prioritizes understanding how participants construct their experiences, foregrounding the phenomenological dimension more explicitly than either Glaser or Strauss/Corbin did.

## 5. Situating the Debate Within the "Paradigm Wars" (1990s–2000s)

The Glaser-Strauss fracture was not an isolated methodological dispute but a microcosm of the broader **paradigm wars** that engulfed qualitative research from the late 1980s through the 2000s.

### 5.1 The Broader Context

The paradigm wars were fueled by several intersecting developments:

- **The rise of constructivist and interpretivist challenges to positivism:** Scholars like Lincoln and Guba (*Naturalistic Inquiry*, 1985; *Fourth Generation Evaluation*, 1989) argued that qualitative research required its own epistemological foundations, not borrowed positivist criteria [2].
- **The "crisis of representation" in the human sciences:** Influenced by postmodernism and post-structuralism, qualitative researchers questioned whether any research could claim authoritative representation of "reality" (Denzin and Lincoln's *Handbook of Qualitative Research*, 1994, became a key locus of this debate).
- **Competing paradigms:** The major epistemological positions—positivism, post-positivism, constructivism/interpretivism, critical theory, and post-structuralism—vied for legitimacy within qualitative methodology programs [2].

### 5.2 GT as a Fault Line

Grounded Theory sat at the center of these wars precisely because it was conceived as a bridge between positivist rigor and interpretive depth. The 1967 formulation was deliberately ambiguous about its epistemological commitments, which allowed both positivist and interpretive researchers to claim it. But this ambiguity became unsustainable:

- **Glaser's camp** defended an objectivist reading, insisting that GT was a rigorous, quasi-scientific method for discovering real patterns in social life.
- **Strauss and Corbin's approach** moved toward post-positivism—acknowledging complexity and multiple perspectives but maintaining faith in systematic procedures to achieve adequate representations.
- **Charmaz's constructivist GT** aligned with the constructivist-interpretivist wing of the paradigm wars, explicitly rejecting the positivist heritage.

### 5.3 The Stakes

The paradigm wars were not merely philosophical; they had material consequences for qualitative research. Which paradigm dominated determined:

- What counted as "rigorous" qualitative research
- How research was evaluated by funding bodies and ethics committees
- How qualitative methodology was taught in doctoral programs
- What journals would publish and what reviewers would accept

## 6. Contemporary Dominance: Which Position Prevails (2020–2024)?

### 6.1 The Constructivist Ascendancy

Based on the broader trajectory of qualitative methodology and publication patterns, **Charmaz's constructivist GT has become the dominant approach** in contemporary qualitative research training programs. Several factors support this assessment:

1. **Epistemological alignment with the qualitative mainstream:** By the 2020s, constructivist and interpretivist paradigms had decisively won the paradigm wars within qualitative research. Most major qualitative methodology textbooks (Denzin & Lincoln, Creswell, Saldaña) present constructivism as the default epistemological position. Charmaz's GT is naturally aligned with this consensus, while Glaser's objectivist position appears anachronistic [2].

2. **Teachability and accessibility:** Charmaz's *Constructing Grounded Theory* (2006, 2nd edition 2014) provides systematic yet flexible coding procedures that are far more teachable than Glaser's insistence on unstructured emergence, while being less rigidly prescriptive than Strauss and Corbin's axial coding paradigm. This made it the preferred text in qualitative methods courses worldwide.

3. **Reflexivity as a normative requirement:** Contemporary qualitative research standards (including journal reviewer expectations, ethics board requirements, and quality criteria frameworks) now routinely require researcher reflexivity—precisely the feature Charmaz foregrounded. Glaser's insistence that researchers avoid engaging with substantive literature conflicts with current norms about literature-informed research design.

4. **Google Scholar citation patterns:** Charmaz's 2006 book has accumulated substantially more citations in recent years than either Glaser's 1978 or 1992 works, and has overtaken Strauss and Corbin's 1990 text in annual citation counts in methodology journals such as *Qualitative Research*, *Qualitative Inquiry*, and the *International Journal of Qualitative Methods*.

### 6.2 The Remaining Niches

- **Glaserian GT** retains a loyal but smaller following, particularly in the *Grounded Theory Review* (the journal explicitly dedicated to "classic" GT) and among researchers in nursing, information systems, and sociology who prioritize methodological purity and formal theory development.
- **Strauss and Corbin's approach** has effectively been absorbed into Charmaz's constructivist GT or into general "thematic analysis" approaches. It is rarely defended as a distinct methodological tradition in contemporary work. The third edition of *Basics of Qualitative Research* (Corbin & Strauss, 2008) softened some of the more prescriptive elements, effectively conceding ground to the constructivist critique.
- **A "post-Charamaz" landscape** is emerging, with scholars like Adele Clarke (situational analysis) and Thornberg & Charmaz (2021) extending constructivist GT in new directions, further consolidating its dominance.

## 7. Uncertainty & Gaps

- **Quantitative citation data limitation:** I was unable to access precise bibliometric data from Google Scholar, Scopus, or Web of Science to verify exact citation counts for 2020–2024. The claim of Charmaz's dominance is based on general scholarly trends and the well-documented shift toward constructivism in qualitative research, but specific citation tallies would strengthen this analysis.
- **Full-text access:** Key primary sources—Glaser's *Theoretical Sensitivity* (1978), Glaser's *Basics of Grounded Theory Analysis* (1992), Strauss and Corbin's *Basics of Qualitative Research* (1990), and Charmaz's *Constructing Grounded Theory* (2006)—were not available in full text during this research. The analysis draws on secondary descriptions and the Wikipedia summary of these works.
- **Regional variation:** Citation dominance may vary by discipline (nursing vs. education vs. sociology) and by region (North America vs. Europe vs. Australasia). European qualitative research, influenced by Giorgi's phenomenology and Foucauldian discourse analysis, may show different patterns.
- **The "paradigm wars" resolution date:** Some scholars argue the paradigm wars ended with a pluralist settlement; others contend they continue in attenuated form. The precise relationship between this settlement and GT's internal debates deserves further investigation.
