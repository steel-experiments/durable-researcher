# Monitoring Strategy and Disposition for Recurrent Near-Syncope with Symptomatic Bradycardia and AV Block

## Executive Summary

This 68-year-old man meets clinical criteria for **hospital admission with inpatient telemetry monitoring** rather than expedited outpatient evaluation. His presentation — recurrent near-syncope with witnessed episodes of pallor and inability to speak, sinus bradycardia with dropped beats on ECG, and a likely medication-exacerbated second-degree AV block — represents symptomatic bradycardia with evidence of cerebral hypoperfusion. The combination of failed outpatient monitoring (twice-denied Holter, 6-week cardiology wait) and progressively concerning symptoms makes discharge unsafe.

---

## 1. ECG Interpretation and Diagnostic Framework

### Likely Mobitz Type I (Wenckebach) — but Cannot Definitively Exclude Higher-Grade Block

The ECG shows sinus bradycardia (HR 52) with a **varying PR interval (180–240 ms)** and **occasional dropped beats**. This pattern is characteristic of **Mobitz Type I (Wenckebach) second-degree AV block**, defined by progressive PR prolongation culminating in a non-conducted P wave and dropped QRS complex [1].

While Mobitz I is generally described as benign in isolation — common in athletes, young healthy individuals, and the elderly — the critical clinical distinction is with **Mobitz Type II**, which features constant PR intervals with sporadically dropped beats, carries a high risk of progression to complete heart block, and mandates pacemaker implantation [1]. In 2:1 conduction patterns, differentiation between the two can be difficult on a single surface ECG.

**Key concern:** A single 12-lead ECG snapshot cannot reliably characterize the full burden or severity of this patient's conduction disease. The daughter's description of episodes where he "went pale and couldn't talk" for 30 seconds suggests **complete AV block or prolonged sinus pauses** that are not captured on the resting ECG.

## 2. Recommended Monitoring Strategy Before Discharge

### Primary Recommendation: Admit to Inpatient Telemetry

Given the constellation of risk factors (detailed below), the recommended monitoring strategy is **admission to an inpatient bed with continuous cardiac telemetry** for a minimum of **24–48 hours**, with the following objectives:

1. **Continuous real-time rhythm monitoring** to capture the rhythm during any symptomatic episode, determine whether dropped beats represent Mobitz I, Mobitz II, or higher-degree block, and identify prolonged pauses (>3 seconds) or complete heart block
2. **Medication adjustment under observation** — hold metoprolol and monitor for improvement in heart rate and conduction
3. **Expedited inpatient cardiology consultation** to evaluate for pacemaker implantation, bypassing the 6-week outpatient wait
4. **Bridge to outpatient mobile cardiac telemetry (MCT)** if inpatient monitoring is non-diagnostic and the patient stabilizes after medication adjustment

### Why Not Outpatient Holter or Event Monitor?

| Monitoring Modality | Duration | Data Review | Suitability for This Patient |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Holter monitor** | 24–48 hours (up to 14 days) | Retrosive (analyzed after return) | Denied twice by insurance; 24–48h likely non-diagnostic given intermittent episodes [2] |
| **Event recorder** | Up to 30 days | Patient-triggered or auto-triggered; retrospective | May miss asymptomatic pauses; requires patient activation during episodes |
| **Mobile cardiac telemetry (MCT)** | Up to 30 days | **Real-time** transmission with instant alerts for abnormalities | Best outpatient option for intermittent symptoms [2, 3] — but insurance barriers likely similar to Holter |
| **Inpatient telemetry** | Continuous | **Real-time** with immediate clinical response | **Most appropriate** — allows medication adjustment, immediate intervention, and cardiology consultation [4] |

The Holter monitor has already been denied twice, and the pending appeal creates an unsafe gap. Even if approved, a 24–48 hour Holter is likely to be non-diagnostic given that episodes occur every 1–2 weeks and data would not be reviewed until the device is returned — potentially weeks later [3]. If inpatient telemetry is non-diagnostic and the patient improves with medication adjustment, **MCT should be arranged at discharge** as it provides real-time data transmission and 30-day monitoring, making it superior for capturing intermittent arrhythmias [2, 3].

## 3. Clinical Thresholds: Admission vs. Expedited Outpatient Evaluation

### Thresholds Warranting Admission

Based on the evidence gathered, the following clinical features in this patient **meet admission criteria** [4, 5]:

| Admission Criterion | This Patient |
|---|---|
| **Symptomatic bradycardia** (near-syncope, syncope, altered consciousness) | ✅ 3 ED visits for near-syncope; daughter reports episodes of pallor + inability to speak for 30 seconds |
| **Documented conduction system disease** on ECG | ✅ Sinus bradycardia with dropped beats, varying PR interval (180–240 ms) |
| **Medication-induced AV block** with ongoing symptoms | ✅ Metoprolol 50mg BID — identified as a primary reversible cause of AV nodal conduction delay [5, 6] |
| **Failed outpatient workup/diagnostic uncertainty** | ✅ Holter denied twice; cardiology unavailable for 6 weeks |
| **Suspected >3-second pauses or high-grade block** (based on clinical episodes) | ✅ Episodes of unresponsiveness lasting 30 seconds are consistent with prolonged cerebral hypoperfusion from complete AV block or sinus arrest |
| **Age ≥65 with cardiac syncope features** | ✅ Age 68, structural heart disease risk, ECG abnormalities |
| **Recurrence and escalation** (3 ED visits in 1 month + 2 home episodes) | ✅ Progressive symptom burden over 4 weeks |

**Heart rate thresholds:** Technically, bradycardia is HR <60 bpm; clinically significant bradycardia is typically HR <50 bpm [4]. The patient's HR of 52 is near this threshold and must be interpreted in context — it is measured at rest in the ED, and his resting rate on metoprolol likely underestimates the severity of pauses during symptomatic episodes.

### Thresholds That Could Support Expedited Outpatient Evaluation (If Met)

Discharge with expedited outpatient monitoring would be defensible **only if all** of the following were true:

- Symptoms resolved completely after holding metoprolol
- Inpatient telemetry captured no pauses >3 seconds, no Mobitz II, no high-grade AV block
- A reliable outpatient MCT or extended Holter was **confirmed and scheduled** (not pending insurance appeal)
- Expedited cardiology follow-up was **arranged within 1–2 weeks** (not 6 weeks)
- Reliable family member available for continuous observation at home
- Patient educated on red-flag symptoms and has rapid access to emergency care

None of these conditions are currently met.

## 4. Immediate Management Steps

### In the ED / Upon Admission

1. **Hold metoprolol immediately.** Beta-blockers are explicitly identified as a primary reversible cause of AV nodal conduction block [5, 6]. Given that potassium (4.2), magnesium (2.0), and TSH (2.4) are all within normal limits, metoprolol is the most likely reversible precipitant.
2. **Admit to telemetry bed** for continuous real-time cardiac monitoring (minimum 24–48 hours).
3. **Obtain inpatient cardiology consultation** for pacemaker evaluation — this bypasses the 6-week outpatient wait.
4. **Monitor for symptomatic improvement** after metoprolol washout (beta-blocker half-life ~3–7 hours; clinical effect wanes over 24–48 hours).
5. **If bradycardia/symptoms persist despite holding metoprolol:** This indicates intrinsic conduction system disease, and the patient meets criteria for permanent pacemaker evaluation [5, 6].

### If Hemodynamic Instability Develops

- **Atropine 0.5 mg IV** (repeat every 3–5 minutes, max 3 mg) as first-line therapy [4]
- **IV glucagon** for beta-blocker–induced bradycardia refractory to atropine [4]
- **Epinephrine or dopamine infusion** as second-line [4]
- **Transcutaneous pacing** if unstable; transition to transvenous pacing if needed >12 hours [4]
- **ICU transfer** if continuous pacing or vasopressor support is required [4]

---

## 5. Discharge Planning (After Inpatient Evaluation)

If inpatient telemetry is non-diagnostic and the patient stabilizes after metoprolol adjustment:

1. **Discontinue or reduce metoprolol** (substitute with alternative antihypertensive that does not affect AV nodal conduction, e.g., ACE inhibitor or calcium channel blocker — avoiding verapamil/diltiazem which also slow AV conduction)
2. **Arrange MCT for 14–30 days** at discharge — with real-time monitoring and auto-detection of bradyarrhythmias
3. **Expedited cardiology follow-up** within 1–2 weeks (arranged by inpatient consulting team, not as a new outpatient referral)
4. **Educate patient and daughter** on immediate return precautions for recurrence of symptoms
5. **Document the insurance denial history** to support medical necessity for MCT or any future prior authorization

However, the management principles gathered from secondary sources are consistent with these guidelines.

These tools incorporate age ≥65, ECG abnormalities, history of heart failure, and other features — all of which this patient possesses — and generally would classify him as high-risk.

Practical experience suggests that inpatient-initiated MCT orders carry higher approval rates than outpatient orders, and documentation of failed Holter appeals strengthens medical necessity claims.
- The **exact PR interval pattern** (truly progressive Wenckebach vs. 2:1 block with varying conduction) cannot be definitively determined from the case description and has major implications for pacing decisions. This underscores the need for inpatient telemetry to characterize the rhythm disturbance fully.
