# Delta Ranges for Collar Strategy (Protective Collar on Long Stock)

## Executive Summary

The protective collar strategy overwhelmingly uses **0.20–0.30 delta for the sold call** and **-0.20 to -0.35 delta for the bought put**. Due to the persistent volatility skew in equity options (puts are more expensive than equidistant calls), most practitioners recommend **asymmetric strike selection** to achieve a zero-cost structure — the call is placed further OTM (percentage-wise) than the put, resulting in different absolute deltas.

---

## Detailed Findings

### 1. Sold Call Delta: 0.20 to 0.30

Across virtually every source examined, the sold call in a collar falls in the **0.20–0.30 delta range**, corresponding to roughly 5–10% out-of-the-money.

| Delta | Assignment Probability | Use Case |
|-------|----------------------|----------|
| **0.30** | ~30% chance of finishing ITM | Collects more premium; easier to fund the put; standard for zero-cost collars |
| **0.25** | ~25% chance | Balanced approach; moderate upside room |
| **0.20** | ~20% chance | More room for stock appreciation; harder to fully fund the put at zero cost |

**Reasoning:** The sold call must generate enough premium to offset (or at least subsidize) the cost of the protective put. A 0.30 delta call captures more extrinsic value due to its proximity to the money, making it the workhorse for zero-cost constructions. A 0.20 delta is used when the investor prioritizes upside capital appreciation over zero-cost execution [1][2][3][4][5].

Sources: TradingBlock [1], OptionAlpha [2], Framework Investing [5], QuantStrategy.io [6], OIC [7]

---

### 2. Bought Put Delta: -0.20 to -0.35

The bought put shows more variation depending on the investor's protection priority:

| Delta | Protection Level | Cost | Use Case |
|-------|-----------------|------|----------|
| **-0.35 to -0.40** | Strong floor (~5% OTM) | Expensive | Risk-averse holders; institutional capital preservation |
| **-0.30** | Moderate floor (~8-10% OTM) | Moderate | Standard retail collar; balanced protection |
| **-0.20** | Wider floor (~12-15% OTM) | Cheap | Cheaper insurance; larger unprotected gap |

**Reasoning:** A -0.30 delta put is the most commonly cited "default" — it provides a reliable structural floor without overpaying for insurance. For zero-cost collars, the put delta often lands between -0.25 and -0.35, because the put must be closer to the money (higher absolute delta) than the call to match premiums given the volatility skew [3][4][8][9].

Sources: TradingBlock [1], Strike.money [8], FasterCapital [9], educoptions [4]

---

### 3. Symmetric vs. Asymmetric: **Asymmetric is Recommended**

This was the most consistent and emphatic finding across sources. **Do not aim for equal absolute deltas.**

**Why asymmetric?**

1. **Volatility Skew**: In equity markets, OTM puts consistently trade at higher implied volatility than equidistant OTM calls (the "crashophobia" effect). A put that is 10% OTM will cost significantly more than a call that is 10% OTM. To achieve zero-cost, you must either:
   - Sell the call *closer* to the money (higher delta, e.g., 0.30), OR
   - Buy the put *further* from the money (lower absolute delta, e.g., -0.20), OR
   - Both

2. **Put-Call Parity Reality**: As OptionAlpha [2] and Framework Investing [5] explicitly state: *"Puts equidistant from the stock price cost more than calls. Therefore, the call strike must generally be set closer to the underlying stock price than the strike price of the put."*

3. **Typical Asymmetric Structure**: Sell a 0.25–0.30 delta call, buy a -0.20 to -0.30 delta put — but the *percentage distances* will differ, not the deltas. For example, the call might be 7% OTM while the put is 10% OTM to equalize premiums [5][6].

**When to use symmetric:** Only when the investor specifically wants equal upside cap and downside floor distances (e.g., 10% up / 10% down) and is willing to accept a net debit because the put will cost more than the call [1][7].

### 4. Percentage Distance from Current Price

| Source | Call Strike OTM | Put Strike OTM | Net Cost |
|--------|----------------|---------------|----------|
| TradingBlock [1] | 5% | 5% | Zero-cost (symmetric) |
| OptionAlpha [2] | 5% | 5–6% | Zero-cost (slight asymmetry) |
| QuantStrategy.io [6] | 12.5% | 10% | Near zero-cost |
| Framework Investing [5] | 5–15% | 5–15% | Varies; wider in high IV |
| Institutional (LEAPS) [4] | 30–70% | 5–10% | Zero-cost (deep OTM call) |
| Fidelity [3] | 5% | 5% | Net credit possible |

**Standard retail collar**: 5–10% OTM on both sides.
**Institutional collar**: Can be much wider on the call side (30–70% OTM using LEAPS), accepting a very high cap in exchange for zero-cost downside protection [4].

---

### 5. Institutional Collar Structures

Institutional holders (pension funds, concentrated stock positions, corporate executives) use collars differently than retail traders:

- **Primary Goal**: Capital preservation on large concentrated positions without triggering a taxable sale event [4][6].
- **Duration**: Often use **LEAPS** (long-dated options, 1–2 year expirations) rather than the 30–90 day retail standard [4][6].
- **Call Strike**: Can be set very far OTM (30–70% above current price), accepting an extremely high cap to fully finance the put at zero cost [4].
- **Put Strike**: Typically closer to the money (5–15% OTM) for robust downside protection; institutions prioritize the floor over the cap [4][9].
- **Example from QuantStrategy.io [6]**: A stock at $400 with a $50 cost basis — collar with $360 put (10% floor) and $430 call (7.5% cap) to preserve accrued wealth.

## Uncertainty & Gaps

- **Exact delta values are context-dependent**: The delta corresponding to a given percentage OTM varies with underlying volatility, time to expiration, and interest rates. A 10% OTM call on a high-volatility tech stock may carry a 0.35 delta, while the same distance on a utility stock might yield a 0.15 delta.
- **Institutional data is limited**: Pension fund and corporate hedging programs are proprietary. The 30–70% OTM call range cited for institutional LEAPS collars comes from secondary educational sources rather than primary fund disclosures.
- **No single "correct" answer**: Collar construction is inherently a personal risk-reward tradeoff. The 0.20–0.30 delta range is a widely cited convention, not a hard rule. Traders should adjust based on their specific stock, volatility environment, and risk tolerance.
