## Why This Is Different From a Normal Flare

You said this feels different, and the medical literature confirms that instinct is correct. Here is how your symptoms map onto the recognized warning signs of **bowel obstruction** — a distinct, dangerous complication separate from a typical Crohn's flare:

| Your Symptom | What It Likely Indicates |
|---|---|
| Cramping so intense you're doubled over | Obstructive colic — the intestine forcefully contracting against a blockage |
| Vomiting everything for 24+ hours | Complete or near-complete obstruction — nothing can pass through |
| Dry mouth | Moderate-to-severe dehydration from fluid loss |
| Lightheaded when standing | Hemodynamic instability — dropping blood pressure from volume depletion |
| Known narrowing on scope 6 months ago | A stricture that has likely progressed to a blockage |

**Intestinal strictures occur in 50–70% of Crohn's patients within 10 years of diagnosis** [1][2]. Your 8-year history places you squarely in this risk window. Strictures can be inflammatory, fibrotic (scar tissue), or mixed, and when they narrow the bowel enough, they create a mechanical obstruction — something no amount of clear liquids or waiting will fix.

---

## The Dehydration Is Already Serious — and Getting Worse

Mayo Clinic explicitly lists **"inability to keep down fluids"** as a criterion for **immediate emergency care** [3]. Harvard Health concurs: you should see a doctor immediately if you are "unable to adequately replace fluids on your own" [4].

Your body has been losing fluids and critical electrolytes (sodium, potassium, chloride) through vomiting since yesterday morning while absorbing virtually nothing. The dry mouth and lightheadedness you're experiencing are not mild symptoms — they indicate your blood volume is already dropping. Left unchecked, this cascade leads to:

- **Kidney failure** (from inadequate blood flow)
- **Dangerous electrolyte imbalances** (which can cause heart arrhythmias and seizures)
- **Hypovolemic shock** — a deadly drop in blood pressure and oxygen delivery [3][4]

**Clear liquids at home will not solve this.** You need IV fluids to safely restore hydration and electrolytes.

---

## What Can Happen If You Wait

Multiple medical sources detail a grim, rapid progression when bowel obstruction goes untreated:

| Timeline Without Treatment | What Happens |
|---|---|
| **0–24 hours** | Abdominal pain, nausea, vomiting, constipation (where you are now) |
| **24–72 hours** | Increasingly severe pain, frequent vomiting, abdominal distension |
| **As early as 6–12 hours after ischemia begins** | The obstructed intestine can twist around its own blood supply (strangulation), cutting off circulation and causing tissue death (necrosis) |
| **72+ hours** | Bowel perforation — the intestinal wall tears, spilling bacteria-laden contents into the abdomen, causing peritonitis |
| **Beyond that** | Sepsis, septic shock, multi-organ failure, and death |

Small bowel obstruction carries a **10–20% mortality rate**, and large bowel obstruction **15–30%** [5]. Survival rates drop significantly once perforation occurs. Patients who receive treatment **within 24 hours have far better outcomes** [6].

---

## What the ER Will Do

Going to the ER is not an overreaction. It is the appropriate, medically indicated response. Here is what to expect:

1. **IV fluids** — to rapidly correct dehydration and restore electrolytes
2. **Blood work** — to assess electrolyte levels, kidney function, and signs of infection
3. **CT scan or X-ray** — to confirm whether there is a bowel obstruction, its location, and severity
4. **Nasogastric (NG) tube** — a tube through your nose into your stomach to decompress it and stop the vomiting
5. **Surgical consultation** — if the obstruction is complete or the bowel is compromised, surgery (such as strictureplasty or bowel resection) may be necessary [1][2][5]
