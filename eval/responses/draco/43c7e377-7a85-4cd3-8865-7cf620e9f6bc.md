# Feminist Legal Theory Across Legal Traditions: A Comparative Analysis

## Executive Summary

Feminist legal theory has evolved along strikingly different trajectories depending on the legal tradition in which it is embedded. Anglo-American critical feminism developed through a sequence from MacKinnon's dominance theory to Fineman's vulnerability theory, systematically exposing how liberal legal categories reproduce male power. Islamic feminism, led by scholars like Mir-Hosseini and Wadud, pursues gender justice through internal exegetical reinterpretation of the Quran and Sharia rather than secular rights frameworks alone. African feminism confronts the dual legacies of colonial law and patriarchal customary practice, critiquing universal human rights instruments as often masking continued subordination. Chinese feminist legal thought navigates the contradictions between socialist egalitarian promises and market-era retrenchment, where the state alternately liberates and constrains women depending on its demographic and economic priorities. Across all traditions, the central contested domains are property rights, bodily autonomy, and political participation—each structured differently by the interplay of formal law, cultural practice, and state power.

## I. Anglo-American Critical Legal Feminism

### From Formal Equality to Dominance Theory

The Anglo-American tradition of feminist legal theory is the most internally documented and institutionally developed strand of feminist jurisprudence. Its evolution proceeds through identifiable phases: first-wave campaigns for basic political and property rights (suffrage, married women's property acts); second-wave dismantling of formal legal barriers (1960s–70s); the critical legal studies turn of the 1980s; and the intersectional, postcolonial, and vulnerability-based approaches from the 1990s onward [1].

**Catharine MacKinnon's dominance theory** represents the most consequential theoretical innovation of the 1980s phase. MacKinnon posited that gender inequality is not merely differential treatment but a *hierarchy*—a system of institutionalized male dominance in which sexuality functions as the primary mechanism of political subordination. Her framework rejected the liberal "sameness" approach to equality ("treat like cases alike") in favor of a substantive approach that asks whether a law or practice perpetuates systemic power imbalances [2].

MacKinnon's theory had extraordinary doctrinal impact. Her 1979 book *Sexual Harassment of Working Women* invented the legal categories of "quid pro quo" and "hostile work environment" harassment, which the U.S. Supreme Court adopted in *Meritor Savings Bank v. Vinson* (1986). With Andrea Dworkin, she drafted the 1983 Minneapolis anti-pornography civil rights ordinance, which reframed pornography not as a moral issue but as sex discrimination. Though struck down domestically, Canada's Supreme Court adopted her framework in *R. v. Butler* (1992). Internationally, she represented Bosnian and Croatian women in *Kadic v. Karadzic*, winning a $745 million verdict that established rape and forced impregnation as legally actionable acts of genocide. She also shaped the "Swedish Model" (1998) of penalizing buyers of sexual services [2].

### Fineman's Vulnerability Theory: Beyond the Autonomous Subject

**Martha Fineman** represents a later pivot in Anglo-American feminist legal thought. Described as "the preeminent feminist family theorist of our time," she founded the Feminism and Legal Theory Project in 1984 and held the first Dorothea S. Clarke Professorship in Feminist Jurisprudence at Cornell [3].

Fineman's core contribution is her demolition of the autonomous liberal legal subject. In *The Autonomy Myth* (2004), she argued that American legal ideology is fixated on the fiction that citizens are and should be self-sufficient, when in reality dependency is "an unavoidable fact of the human condition." Her subsequent **vulnerability theory**, articulated in *The Vulnerable Subject* (2008, 2011), proposes that because vulnerability is "universal and constant, inherent in the human condition," the state bears affirmative obligations to build institutional "resilience" mechanisms—reconfiguring how legal systems should structure social obligations, caretaking support, and property rights [3].

Vulnerability theory moves beyond MacKinnon's focus on sexuality as the axis of domination to address structural economic dependencies: family law, welfare provision, caregiving infrastructure. It also represents a "post-identity" inquiry, shifting focus from specific marginalized groups to the institutional structures that manage—or fail to manage—universal human fragility [1, 3].

### Persistent Structural Gaps

Despite these theoretical advances, the Anglo-American legal system continues to exhibit what scholar Murray (2023) calls a "jurisprudence of masculinity." The 2022 *Dobbs* decision reversing federal reproductive protections and the fact that the United States remains the only OECD nation without paid family leave are potent indicators that formal legal equality has not yielded substantive equity [1].

## II. Islamic Feminism: Reinterpreting Sharia from Within

### Theological Methodology and Key Scholars

Islamic feminism constitutes a distinctive legal-theoretical tradition that seeks gender justice through internal religious reform rather than through secular international law alone. As historian Margot Badran frames it, Islamic feminism "derives its understanding and mandate from the Qur'an, seeks rights and justice for women, and for men, in the totality of their existence" [4].

The methodological core is **feminist exegetical reinterpretation** of the Quran and Hadith. Scholars distinguish between verses specific to 7th-century Arabia and universal Quranic principles, arguing that patriarchal norms result from political manipulation of texts by male elites, not from divine ordination. Moroccan sociologist **Fatema Mernissi** made this case powerfully in *The Veil and the Male Elite*, demonstrating how hadith were selectively deployed to suppress women's rights, running counter to the egalitarian community envisioned by the Prophet Muhammad [4].

**Amina Wadud**, in her foundational 1992 text *Quran and Woman*, critiques traditional readings of verse 4:34—one of the most contested passages regarding gender relations. She argues that the verse prescribes an "equitable and mutually dependent relationship" based on mutual responsibility, not universal male superiority or sole male provider status. Wadud's hermeneutic approach reads the Quran holistically, using its general ethical principles to interpret specific contextual verses [4].

**Ziba Mir-Hosseini**, an Iranian-born legal anthropologist, brings an ethnographic dimension to Islamic feminist legal theory. Specializing in how legal structures impact women's rights in the Muslim world, she has documented the practical realities of women navigating Sharia family courts through both scholarship and documentary film (*Divorce Iranian Style*, 2000). Her activist-legal perspective emphasizes internal religious reform driven by grassroots movements: "Women want the same things that everyone wants... as more of these women are educating themselves, the sheer groundswell of it coming from below is forcing a change" [5].

**Azizah al-Hibri** grounds equality in the Quranic teaching that all humans are created from one soul, asserting natural equality where no man is superior based on gender alone. **Riffat Hassan** takes a more radical methodological step, advocating for theology based solely on Quranic principles while excluding hadith entirely, arguing that the hadith corpus is too saturated with patriarchal interpolation to serve as a reliable source for egalitarian norms [4].

### Historical Roots and Contemporary Trajectories

Islamic feminist legal thought has deeper historical roots than commonly recognized. **Qasim Amin's** 1899 *Tahrir al-Mar'a* (Women's Liberation) condemned polygyny and veiling as un-Islamic—though Leila Ahmed later critiqued this as androcentric and colored by colonial influence. **Begum Rokeya** (1880–1932) criticized *purdah* (sex segregation) and founded the Sakhawat Memorial Girls' School. **Aisha Abd al-Rahman** began popular feminist Quranic exegesis as early as 1959 [4].

Islamic feminists engage with CEDAW and global human rights frameworks but argue that integrating these with Islamic theology creates greater resonance for ordinary Muslim women in Muslim-majority nations. The 2015 **Muslim Reform Movement** Declaration explicitly supports "equal rights to inheritance, witness, work, mobility, personal law, education, and employment" and rejects sexism and misogyny. Emerging jurisprudence seeks to equalize family law, forbid female genital mutilation, and support women's religious and civil leadership [4].

The designation "Islamic feminism" itself remains contested. Scholar Fatima Seedat argues it is unnecessary and isolates Muslims from the universal feminist movement, while others like Asma Barlas insist engaging with Islamic theology is essential, particularly where religious law is state law [4].

## III. African Feminism: Customary Law, Colonialism, and Human Rights Critique

### Beyond Western Frameworks

African feminism encompasses diverse theories—**Stiwanism, Motherism, Femalism, nego-feminism, and snail-sense feminism**—that diverge fundamentally from mainstream Western feminism. Scholars like Naomi Nkealah categorize these continental models as prioritizing indigenous histories, gender inclusion, and collaboration with men to improve women's material conditions [6].

African feminists critique Western feminism for its basis in individualism and its failure to accommodate African communal traditions. Cameroonian scholar Elma Akob argues Western feminism is incompatible with Africa's "we" ideology. Hazel Carby contends that Western feminism falsely universalizes the "normative experience" of white women, while Awa Thiam identifies the "multiple jeopardy" facing African women—combining patriarchal oppression, capitalist exploitation, and neo-colonialism [6].

### Customary Law as Colonial Construction

A crucial African feminist legal insight is that what passes as "customary law" is often a colonial fabrication. Pre-colonial societies frequently featured complementary gender roles with women holding significant political and economic power—Queen-mothers in Edo and Yoruba cultures being prominent examples. Colonial imposition of "customary law" stripped women of property rights and political power by creating male-dominated local governments and transferring land and labor control to men while exploiting women's agricultural production [6, 7].

Post-colonially, men have continued to invoke "customs or cultural tradition" to maintain control over property and restrict women's mobility. Under customary patrilineal systems, women's land access is tied to "user rights" (usufruct) determined by natal and marital affiliations rather than direct ownership. Dowry practices often supersede formal inheritance laws—a daughter's dowry is viewed as her inheritance but is absorbed by the husband's family. There remains a persistent "mismatch" between statutory law (which increasingly grants daughters inheritance rights) and traditional practices, with land titling processes administered with strong gender bias by predominantly male administrative bodies [7].

### Critique of Universal Human Rights Frameworks

African feminists challenge universal human rights frameworks for classifying African women simply as "women of color," ignoring specific historical trajectories of colonial extraction and post-colonial state formation. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy confirms that critical feminists charge that adopting universal human rights instruments like CEDAW can "mask the continued flourishing of patriarchal domestic laws and fail to address the specific realities of post-colonial women" [1]. This is not a rejection of rights *per se*, but an insistence that universal frameworks must account for the particular ways colonial legal pluralism created layered systems of subordination.

Despite these structural challenges, women's political representation shows meaningful variation: as of December 2020, the East and Southern Africa region recorded **32% female parliamentary representation** (surpassing the 24.5% global average), with Rwanda at **61%** leading globally [6].

## IV. Chinese Feminist Legal Thought: State Socialism, Marketization, and Control

### Revolutionary Foundations

Chinese feminist legal scholars navigate a unique trajectory shaped by state socialism and subsequent marketization. Professor Lin Chun captures the foundational logic: "the Chinese revolution was simultaneously a women's revolution, and Chinese socialism a women's cause" [8].

The landmark **Marriage Law of 1950** structurally redefined family law by outlawing arranged marriages, polygamy, concubinage, and bride prices. During land reform, the state promoted a "double *fanshen*"—a revolutionary awakening in which women gained land ownership and "freedom over their own bodies." The state funded public childcare and paid wages directly to individuals rather than family heads, which Mao noted was "a way of smashing the patriarchal system" [8]. The **1980 Marriage Law** banned forced marriages, and a major **2001 amendment** adapted family law to the complexities of marketization [8].

### The Marketization Contradiction

However, China's transition to a socialist market economy produced acute contradictions for feminist legal advocacy. While formal legal equality was achieved through state socialism, women's actual property rights and bodily autonomy fluctuate based on the state's demographic and economic priorities. Under current CCP General Secretary Xi Jinping, the state has suppressed independent feminist NGOs, censored platforms, and labeled feminism a "toxic Western ideology." Simultaneously, the government is increasingly restricting abortion in the 2020s and promoting pro-natalist policies, urging women to "actively foster a new type of marriage and childbearing culture" [8].

This creates a distinctive paradox: the same socialist state that once granted women unprecedented legal rights now constrains feminist advocacy when it conflicts with demographic planning. Chinese feminist legal scholars operate in a space where international human rights instruments are invoked to advocate for gender equity, but where independent civil society mobilization is systematically curtailed [1, 8].

## V. Comparative Analysis: Property Rights, Bodily Autonomy, and Political Participation

### Property Rights

| Legal Tradition | Structural Framework | Key Feminist Critique |
|---|---|---|
| **Anglo-American** | Married Women's Property Acts (19th c.) formally ended coverture; but Fineman argues liberal property law still presumes autonomous male-headed households, leaving caretakers economically vulnerable [1, 3, 7] | Vulnerability theory: state must structurally support dependency rather than assume self-sufficiency |
| **Islamic** | Sharia inheritance jurisprudence traditionally allocates female heirs half the male share; Islamic feminists argue this must be contextually reinterpreted given the Quran's broader egalitarian principles [4, 7] | Wadud, al-Hibri: patriarchal administrative practice, not divine law, drives inequality |
| **African Customary** | Women hold usufruct (user) rights, not ownership; patrilineal inheritance concentrates property in male hands; colonial codification of "customary law" hardened flexible pre-colonial practices [6, 7] | Customary law is a colonial artifact; statutory reforms fail without restructuring land administration |
| **Chinese Socialist** | 1950 Marriage Law and land reform granted women formal property ownership; marketization has eroded collective protections and created new gendered economic vulnerabilities [8] | Formal rights are insufficient when the state can revoke autonomy for demographic goals |

### Bodily Autonomy

| Legal Tradition | Structural Framework | Key Dynamics |
|---|---|---|
| **Anglo-American** | *Roe v. Wade* (1973) to *Dobbs* (2022): federal constitutional protection for abortion reversed; MacKinnon frames sexuality itself as the site of subordination [1, 2] | Formal protections can be reversed; substantive control over bodies requires structural change |
| **Islamic** | Bodily autonomy negotiated through Sharia family codes; Islamic feminists reinterpret Quranic passages on modesty, polygyny, and marital relations to argue for mutual consent [4] | Reform from within religious frameworks rather than imposition of secular norms |
| **African** | Colonial regimes regulated bodily autonomy through sexual violence; African feminists are divided on sexuality and heteronormativity (Nyanzi) [6] | Multiple jeopardy—patriarchal, capitalist, neo-colonial oppression converges on the body |
| **Chinese** | Socialist revolution granted "freedom over bodies"; current state restricting abortion and promoting pro-natalism [8] | State grants and revokes bodily autonomy based on economic/demographic planning |

### Political Participation

Across all traditions, formal political participation does not guarantee substantive representation. Rwanda's 61% female parliamentary representation—a global high—exists alongside continued customary subordination in property and family law [6]. The US, Iran, Somalia, and Sudan have not ratified CEDAW, demonstrating diverse forms of structural resistance [1]. Chinese women's political representation is mediated by party structures that simultaneously promote and constrain feminist organizing [8]. Islamic feminists seek women's leadership in both religious and civil institutions, challenging patriarchal gatekeeping from within theological traditions [4].

## VI. Convergence and Divergence Across Traditions

Several themes unite these otherwise divergent traditions:

1. **Beyond formal equality**: All four traditions reject the sufficiency of formal legal equality ("treat like cases alike") and pursue substantive equity addressing material power imbalances—whether through MacKinnon's dominance framework, Fineman's vulnerability analysis, Wadud's hermeneutic of reciprocity, or African feminists' insistence on material conditions over rights discourse.

2. **Critique of the public/private divide**: Each tradition identifies how law naturalizes the subordination of women by relegating certain domains (family, sexuality, domestic labor) to a "private" sphere beyond legal scrutiny.

3. **The question of universalism vs. particularism**: This is the sharpest axis of divergence. Anglo-American theory increasingly embraces intersectionality and transnational analysis. Islamic feminism insists on religiously-grounded reform. African feminism critiques universal frameworks as neo-colonial. Chinese feminism navigates a state that alternately embraces and rejects international norms. The tension between these positions is not resolvable by theory alone—it reflects genuinely different relationships between law, religion, culture, and state power.

4. **Colonial and post-colonial legacies**: Both African and Islamic feminist legal traditions are shaped by encounters with colonial legal systems that hardened patriarchal structures in the name of "tradition" or "civilization." Chinese feminism confronts a different but related dynamic: a revolutionary state that both liberated and instrumentalized women.

## Uncertainty & Gaps

- **Chinese feminist legal scholars by name**: While the sources describe the structural evolution of Chinese women's legal status, specific contemporary Chinese feminist legal theorists and their published works were less identifiable in the available sources. Professor Lin Chun is cited, but a fuller mapping of Chinese feminist legal academia (e.g., scholars publishing in Chinese law journals on gender and the Marriage Law) would strengthen the analysis.
- **African feminist legal theorists**: The sources identify theoretical categories (Stiwanism, nego-feminism) and broad critiques, but specific legal scholars and their doctrinal interventions in African constitutional and customary law courts could be further specified.
- **Islamic feminist legal outcomes**: While the theoretical positions of Wadud, Mir-Hosseini, and al-Hibri are well-documented, the extent to which their reinterpretations have been adopted by actual Sharia courts or legislatures in Muslim-majority countries requires further empirical investigation.
- **Comparative quantitative data**: Systematic cross-national data on women's property ownership rates, bodily autonomy indices, and political participation disaggregated by legal tradition would provide a stronger empirical foundation for the comparative claims.
