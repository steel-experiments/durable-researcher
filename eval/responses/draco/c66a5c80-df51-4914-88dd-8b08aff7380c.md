### Executive Summary

The three treehouse structures featured in Longwood Gardens' 2008 exhibition "Nature's Castles: The Treehouse Reimagined" were designed by **Matthew Millan Architects** (now Millan Architects), who collaborated with two premier treehouse-building companies: **Treehouse Workshop, Inc.** of Seattle, Washington, and **Forever Young Treehouses** of Burlington, Vermont. A contemporaneous source—the *Daily Local News* (West Chester, PA) article from **April 24, 2008**—provides detailed reporting on the design concept and construction process, including the names of individual designers and construction methods.

---

### Detailed Findings

#### 1. The Architectural Firm and Design Partners

**Matthew Millan Architects** served as the lead architectural firm for the project. According to their own portfolio page, the firm "worked with Longwood Gardens and the two premier treehouse companies in the U.S. to create these unique arboreal structures." [1] The two specialist treehouse firms were:

- **Treehouse Workshop, Inc. (Seattle, WA)** — Specifically named designers **Peter Nelson** and **Jake Jacob** led the design of the *Canopy Cathedral* and *The Birdhouse*. [5]
- **Forever Young Treehouses (Burlington, VT)** — Designed the *Lookout Loft*. [5]

The project was part of a multi-year Longwood Gardens campaign to draw attention to the great trees of the Gardens—the same trees that inspired Pierre du Pont to purchase the initial parcel of land in 1906. [1][2][3]

#### 2. The Three Treehouse Structures

Each structure was designed to respond to its individual setting and offer different interpretive opportunities:

- **The Canopy Cathedral**: The largest and most ornate of the three. A two-story timber-frame structure inspired by Norwegian stave churches, it featured reclaimed Douglas fir timbers milled from salvaged logs recovered from a **2002 Oregon wildfire** and dismantled wood from an **old toothpaste factory in Toronto**. It included an intricate glass facade, cedar roofing, hand-carved fantastic creatures, a 589-square-foot main floor, a 118-square-foot wrap-around balcony, and a grand gable window offering views over the Large Lake. It sat over 10 feet off the ground, supported by 37 steel columns, 34 steel beams, and 8 steel braces. The designer began scouting the location during a snowstorm in **February 2007**. [1][4][5]

- **The Lookout Loft**: A rustic, universally accessible Adirondack-style structure designed for people of all ages and physical abilities. It consisted of a series of ramps and pavilions showcasing multiple wood species (northern white cedar, hop hornbeam, spruce, and ipe) in natural and converted forms. Over 75 feet of ramps led to three platforms up to 14 feet high. It was supported by **13,000 pounds of salvaged steel** and used a special pin foundation with concrete footings and extended steel rods fitted between tree roots to cause zero tree damage. It overlooked the meadow. [2][4][5]

- **The Birdhouse**: The smallest (255–320 sq ft) but tallest structure, sitting more than 20 feet off the ground. Inspired by **steel forest fire towers**, it featured a wide staircase winding upward around the trunk of a large tulip poplar. Built from cedar and salvaged wood from an old dairy barn and chocolate factory, it included organic door pulls (including a rhododendron branch handle) and live-edge slab benches. It was constructed carefully over a large, rotted-out tree trunk, which was intentionally preserved so visitors could peer deep inside the hollow trunk to observe the ecosystem within. [3][4][5]

#### 3. Construction Process and Sustainability

All three structures were built as permanent fixtures using **pin foundations**—anchored to pipes in the ground rather than bolted directly to the trees—to ensure no trees were harmed. Construction took approximately **four months**, and the structures were built primarily with **salvaged and reclaimed materials**, aligning with Longwood Gardens' sustainability efforts. [4][5]

The exhibition opened on **Arbor Day, April 25, 2008** (some sources say April 8) and ran through **November 23, 2008**. Despite initial rumors that the treehouses would be dismantled after the exhibition, Longwood Director **Paul Redman** confirmed the structures would remain standing. [5] They are indeed still present at Longwood Gardens today.

### Uncertainty & Gaps

- **Opening date discrepancy**: Some sources state the exhibition opened on Arbor Day (April 25, 2008), while the LancasterOnline article cites April 8. The *Daily Local News* article is dated April 24, 2008—consistent with an Arbor Day opening. The April 8 date may be erroneous or may refer to a soft opening.
- **Square footage discrepancies**: The *Daily Local News* (2008) reports the Canopy Cathedral as 400 sq ft, while the LancasterOnline article reports 589 sq ft for the main floor. The Birdhouse is reported as 320 sq ft (2008) vs. 255 sq ft (LancasterOnline). These discrepancies may reflect different measurement methodologies (main floor only vs. total enclosed space).
- **The *Pittsburgh Post-Gazette* article** (July 5, 2008) was identified as a contemporaneous source but its full text could not be retrieved; it may contain additional details about the design process and the perspective of the treehouse builders.
- **The relationship between Matthew Millan Architects and the two treehouse companies** is described but not fully delineated. It is unclear whether Millan Architects served as the overall project architect/coordinator while the two specialist firms served as design-build subcontractors, or whether the division of design responsibility was structured differently.
