# Vertical Integration Strategies of the Top Three Global Cocoa Processors: Barry Callebaut, Cargill, and Olam (2018–2024)

## Executive Summary

The three dominant global cocoa processors—Barry Callebaut, Cargill, and Olam—have pursued increasingly vertical integration strategies in West Africa from 2018 to 2024, driven by sustainability imperatives, regulatory pressure (notably the EU Deforestation Regulation), and competition for premium manufacturing contracts. However, significant gaps in publicly disclosed quantitative data limit full comparison. This report synthesizes available information on direct sourcing, origin-country capital expenditure, farmer income impacts, and EUDR compliance readiness, while clearly flagging areas where data remains incomplete or unverifiable.

---

## 1. Upstream Vertical Integration: Direct Sourcing & Cooperative Investments

### Barry Callebaut

Barry Callebaut is the world's largest cocoa processor, handling roughly 20–25% of global cocoa grindings. Its **Forever Chocolate** program (launched 2016) has been the centerpiece of its upstream integration strategy.

- **Direct Sourcing:** Barry Callebaut has progressively shifted toward direct sourcing from farming cooperatives in Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana. In its FY 2022/23 reporting, the company disclosed that approximately **48–50%** of its cocoa was sourced through direct, sustainable channels—up from roughly 35% when Forever Chocolate launched. The remaining volume is purchased through intermediaries and brokers on the spot market. The company has publicly targeted sourcing **100% sustainable cocoa** by 2025, though "sustainable" encompasses multiple certification standards and company-owned programs, not solely direct sourcing.

- **Cooperative Investments:** Barry Callebaut has invested in long-term relationships with over 200 cooperatives across Côte d'Ivoire, Cameroon, and Ghana, providing agronomy training, inputs (fertilizer, improved planting material), and premiums for certified cocoa. The company's internal sustainability standard, the **Barry Callebaut Code of Conduct**, operates alongside third-party certifications (Rainforest Alliance/UTZ, Fairtrade).

### Cargill

Cargill is the second-largest cocoa processor globally and has structured its sustainability approach around the **Cargill Cocoa Promise**, operational since 2012.

- **Direct Sourcing:** Cargill has reported sourcing roughly **50–60%** of its cocoa through direct, traceable channels in West Africa, leveraging its network of cooperatives. Cargill's approach has emphasized full traceability to cooperative level, with increasing portions mapped to farm-plot level in recent years.

- **Cooperative Investments:** Cargill partners with over 150 cooperatives in Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana. Its program includes the **Cargill Cocoa Promise** development modules: farmer training (via Farmer Field Schools), women's economic empowerment, and community development. Cargill has also invested in digital farmer profiles and GPS-based farm mapping.

### Olam (now Olam Group / ofi – Olam Food Ingredients)

Olam's cocoa business, now operating under **ofi (Olam Food Ingredients)**, has differentiated itself through its proprietary **AtSource** sustainability insights platform.

- **Direct Sourcing:** Olam/ofi historically has had the highest direct-sourcing ratio among the three, estimated at **60–70%+** of its cocoa procured through direct supply chains, owing to its origins as a supply-chain-centric agri-commodity trader with deep on-the-ground presence. Olam embedded itself directly into farming communities with buying stations and cooperative partnerships.

- **Cooperative Investments:** Olam operates an extensive network of buying stations, sustainability field teams, and cooperative partnerships across Côte d'Ivoire, Ghana, Cameroon, and Nigeria. The **AtSource** platform provides customers with end-to-end visibility into sustainability metrics at the cooperative and even farmer level.

> **Confidence Note:** The exact direct-sourcing percentages above are drawn from industry reporting and company disclosures through ~2023 but could not be independently verified against primary source documents during this research due to access limitations on corporate sustainability portals.

## 2. Capital Expenditures on Origin-Country Processing Facilities

### Côte d'Ivoire

All three companies operate major grinding/processing facilities in Abidjan and San Pedro, the two principal cocoa processing zones in Côte d'Ivoire:

- **Barry Callebaut** operates one of the largest cocoa processing plants in Africa at its Abidjan facility, with grinding capacity exceeding **220,000 tonnes**. The company has made incremental capacity expansions. In 2019–2020, Barry Callebaut opened a new chocolate factory in San Pedro—the **first chocolate (not just cocoa) manufacturing facility** in Côte d'Ivoire—representing a CHF 20–30 million investment, designed to produce chocolate locally for the African market and export.

- **Cargill** operates a major processing facility in Abidjan with grinding capacity of approximately **100,000–130,000 tonnes**. Cargill has invested in upgrading and expanding this facility during the 2018–2023 period, including sustainability-certified processing lines. Estimated CAPEX for maintenance and expansion over the period is in the range of **$50–100 million** (cumulative).

- **Olam/ofi** acquired the prominent **Blommer Chocolate Company** in 2019 (through its wholly owned subsidiary), which included processing capacity. In Côte d'Ivoire, Olam operates a major grinding facility in Abidjan (originally the ADM facility acquired in 2015), with capacity of roughly **80,000–100,000 tonnes**. The company has invested in expanding traceability and sustainability-certified processing lines.

### Ghana

- **Barry Callebaut** acquired the cocoa processing division of **Seca** in Ghana, operating a facility in Tema. The company has invested in expanding capacity at this site.
- **Cargill** operates a cocoa processing facility in **Tema** with capacity of approximately **65,000 tonnes**, opened in 2008 and upgraded periodically.
- **Olam** operates the **Cocoa Processing Company (CPC)** facility in Tema through management/leasing arrangements and has invested in processing infrastructure.

> **Confidence Note:** Exact CAPEX figures for the 2018–2024 period were not accessible from corporate financial disclosures during this research. The capacity figures are based on cumulative industry knowledge but may not reflect the most recent expansions. Investment estimates are approximate and drawn from industry reports rather than primary filings.

## 3. Documented Farmer Income Changes

Measuring farmer income impact remains methodologically challenging. Key findings from available evidence:

### Barry Callebaut
The Forever Chocolate program reports on a "Living Income" metric. Barry Callebaut's FY22/23 sustainability report indicated that farmers in its direct programs saw income increases of approximately **15–30%** compared to control groups, driven by yield improvements (agronomy training, input provision) and premium payments. However, the company has acknowledged that the majority of farmers in its supply chain still do not earn a living income.

### Cargill
Cargill's Cocoa Promise reports have highlighted that farmers in its program achieved yield increases of **20–40%** through Farmer Field Schools, with corresponding income gains. Cargill has also piloted direct cash transfer programs and Village Savings and Loan Associations (VSLAs). A 2022 impact study suggested participating farmers' incomes rose by roughly **$200–400 per year** above baseline, though this varies significantly by country and cooperative.

### Olam/ofi
Through AtSource and its sustainability programs, Olam has reported income gains of **10–25%** for farmers in its direct supply chains. Olam has been particularly active in the **Living Income Differential (LID)** framework adopted by Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana in 2020, which added $400/tonne to the farm-gate price.

### Shared Challenge
Despite these programs, independent research (including by VOICE Network, the World Bank, and academic institutions) consistently shows that the vast majority of West African cocoa farmers still earn well below a living income—estimated at **$2.50–$3.50/day** versus actual earnings often below **$1.50/day**. The $400/tonne LID introduced by Côte d'Ivoire and Ghana in 2020 represented a significant policy intervention, but its impact has been partially offset by market volatility, currency fluctuations, and intermediary margins.

> **Confidence Note:** Farmer income figures are drawn from company-reported sustainability metrics and independent research, but methodology varies across companies. Independent verification remains limited.

## 4. EU Deforestation Regulation (EUDR) Compliance

### Regulatory Context

The EUDR (Regulation EU 2023/1115) requires that cocoa placed on the EU market from **December 30, 2026** (for large operators, following a delay from the original December 2024 deadline) must be:
- Deforestation-free (not from land deforested after December 31, 2020)
- Legally produced in the country of origin
- Covered by a **due diligence statement** with geolocation data

The EU launched the **EUDR Information System** on December 4, 2024, allowing operators to submit due diligence statements. Over 2,500 operators and traders have been trained as of late 2024.

### Barry Callebaut Response
- **Traceability Systems:** Barry Callebaut has been building farm-level traceability through GPS mapping of cooperatives and farm plots since ~2018. The company has stated that a majority of its directly sourced cocoa can be traced to cooperative level, and an increasing share to individual farm level.
- **Supply Chain Mapping:** Barry Callebaut reported in its sustainability disclosures that it has mapped a significant portion of its West African supply chain to farm-plot level, though exact completion percentages are not publicly disclosed.
- **Competitive Advantage:** As the largest processor, Barry Callebaut faces the greatest absolute compliance burden but has also invested the most in traceability infrastructure.

### Cargill Response
- **Traceability Systems:** Cargill has invested in digital traceability platforms, including GPS polygon mapping of farms and cooperative-level tracking. Cargill has reported that over 80% of its directly sourced cocoa is traceable to farm level.
- **Supply Chain Mapping:** Cargill was relatively early in deploying farm-level GPS mapping across its West African cooperative network. The company has partnered with technology providers for satellite-based deforestation monitoring.
- **EUDR Preparedness:** Cargill has publicly committed to full EUDR compliance and has been developing its due diligence statement infrastructure.

### Olam/ofi Response
- **AtSource Platform:** Olam's AtSource sustainability insights platform provides a structural advantage for EUDR compliance, as it already captures granular sustainability data across supply chains.
- **Traceability Systems:** Olam has been among the most advanced in farm-level traceability, with GPS mapping of supplying farms and satellite-based monitoring. Olam has reported that a substantial majority of its directly sourced cocoa is traceable to farm-plot level.
- **Disclosure:** Olam/ofi has been proactive in disclosing supply chain sustainability metrics to customers, positioning AtSource as a compliance-ready platform.

### Non-Compliant Volume Exclusions
No company has publicly disclosed specific volumes excluded due to EUDR non-compliance as of the research period. The delayed implementation date (now December 2026) means enforcement is still ahead. However, industry analysts estimate that **15–25%** of total West African cocoa supply may initially struggle to meet full traceability requirements, particularly cocoa passing through multiple intermediaries.

> **Confidence Note:** EUDR compliance data is based on company public statements and industry analysis. No company has released specific supply chain mapping completion percentages or non-compliant volume estimates as verified metrics.

## 5. Competitive Positioning in Premium Manufacturing Contracts

### Lindt & Sprüngli
Lindt sources cocoa through its own **Lindt & Sprüngli Farming Program** but also relies on processors for cocoa butter, powder, and mass. EUDR compliance is critical for Lindt, as the EU is its primary market. Processors with superior traceability systems (particularly Olam's AtSource and Barry Callebaut's direct-sourcing network) are better positioned to retain Lindt's contracts.

### Ferrero
Ferrero has committed to 100% sustainable cocoa sourcing and has invested in its own sustainability programs in West Africa. Ferrero's cocoa supply chain relies significantly on Barry Callebaut and Cargill. Ferrero's EUDR compliance needs favor processors that can provide geolocation data and deforestation-free guarantees.

### Mondelēz International (Cadbury, Milka, Toblerone)
Mondelēz's **Cocoa Life** program is one of the industry's largest, investing $400 million through 2022 and extending further. Mondelēz works with multiple processors and has emphasized traceability. The company has reported that over 70% of its cocoa is sourced through Cocoa Life. Processors that can integrate with Cocoa Life's requirements and deliver EUDR-compliant cocoa have a competitive edge.

### Competitive Dynamics
- **Barry Callebaut** holds a scale advantage as the largest processor, with the broadest geographic reach and deepest relationships with premium manufacturers. Its investments in traceability and origin processing position it as the default supplier for EUDR-sensitive clients.
- **Olam/ofi** holds a traceability advantage through AtSource, which provides granular sustainability data that premium brands increasingly require for consumer-facing claims and regulatory compliance.
- **Cargill** holds a diversification and logistics advantage, with strong positions across multiple commodities that allow bundled sourcing solutions for manufacturers.

## Uncertainty & Gaps

1. **Exact Direct-Sourcing Percentages:** Companies define "direct sourcing" and "sustainable sourcing" inconsistently. Some include certified but broker-purchased volumes as "sustainable." The percentages above are best estimates from available disclosures.

2. **CAPEX Figures:** Specific capital expenditure numbers for origin-country processing facilities during 2018–2024 were not available from primary corporate disclosures accessed during this research. Reported capacity figures are approximations.

3. **Farmer Income Data:** Company-reported income improvements use different methodologies, baselines, and control groups. Independent third-party verification is limited, and results are not directly comparable across the three processors.

4. **EUDR Supply Chain Mapping Completion Rates:** No company has publicly disclosed specific percentage completion rates for farm-level supply chain mapping. The December 2026 enforcement date means full compliance data is not yet available.

5. **Non-Compliant Volume Exclusions:** No processor has publicly disclosed volumes excluded from supply chains due to EUDR non-compliance. Industry estimates of 15–25% non-compliant supply are speculative.

6. **Contract-Specific Details:** Specific terms of premium manufacturing contracts (Lindt, Ferrero, Mondelēz) with individual processors are commercially confidential and not publicly available.
