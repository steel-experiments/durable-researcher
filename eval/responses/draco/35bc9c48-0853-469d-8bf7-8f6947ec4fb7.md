# Methodological Tensions Between Process Tracing and Bayesian Causal Inference: A Comparative Analysis

## Executive Summary

The methodological landscape of causal inference in comparative politics and international relations has been shaped by a deep tension between qualitative process tracing—articulated most systematically by Andrew Bennett and Jeffrey Checkel—and formal Bayesian and structural approaches to causal inference championed by Judea Pearl and, in political science, by James Mahoney. These frameworks diverge sharply on how causal mechanisms are operationalized, how equifinality is managed, and how confounders are identified in observational data. Post-2010 critiques from the quantitative causal inference community—particularly regarding selection bias, post-treatment conditioning, and false-positive vulnerability—have catalyzed significant methodological reform, pushing process tracing toward formalized Bayesian updating protocols and encouraging mixed-methods designs that integrate within-case diagnostic evidence with cross-case statistical analysis. However, a full consensus remains elusive: the field is converging on pragmatic integration but divided on epistemological foundations.

## I. Operationalizing Causal Mechanisms: Two Competing Logics

### Process Tracing (Bennett and Checkel)

Process tracing operationalizes causality by "unwrapping" the causal links between independent variables and outcomes. Rather than treating causation as a static correlation, the method demands that researchers map the specific intervening steps, causal chains, and mechanisms that translate initial conditions into observed outcomes. As articulated by Bennett and Checkel (in their foundational 2014 edited volume *Process Tracing: From Metaphor to Analytic Tool*), the method relies on rich within-case qualitative data—historical memoirs, interviews, archival documents, press accounts—to construct detailed temporal chronologies of how social actors respond to conditions within their specific contexts [1][2].

The inferential logic of process tracing is organized around Stephen Van Evera's typology of tests, which provides a structured framework for evaluating evidence against competing hypotheses:

- **Straw-in-the-wind tests**: Neither necessary nor sufficient for confirming a hypothesis; merely suggestive.
- **Hoop tests**: Necessary but not sufficient; failing the test definitively disproves the hypothesis, but passing does not confirm it.
- **Smoking gun tests**: Sufficient but not necessary; passing strongly confirms the hypothesis, but failing does not disprove it.
- **Double decisive tests**: Both necessary and sufficient; passing confirms the theory while simultaneously ruling out all rival hypotheses [2].

This typology gives process tracing a distinctive inferential architecture: it evaluates evidence *qualitatively* and *sequentially*, privileging the temporal ordering of events and the diagnostic weight of individual pieces of evidence rather than aggregating observations statistically.

### Bayesian and Structural Approaches (Pearl and Mahoney)

Judea Pearl's framework mathematizes causal inference through **structural causal models (SCMs)** and **do-calculus**. SCMs provide a formal language for encoding assumptions about causal relationships, enabling researchers to distinguish mere statistical associations from true causal effects. Do-calculus—the logical engine of Pearl's framework—is a set of syntactic rules that determines whether a specific causal effect is *identifiable* from purely observational data [5].

Pearl's approach provides a definitive, formal method for **confounder identification** through the **back-door** and **front-door criteria**. These mathematical formulas allow researchers to systematically identify the precise set of covariates that must be controlled for to isolate a causal effect, extracting interventional probabilities from observational data without falling prey to spurious correlations [5]. The key insight is that causal identification requires *a priori* specification of the causal graph—researchers must state their assumptions about the causal structure before examining data, and the do-calculus then determines what can and cannot be learned from the available evidence.

James Mahoney's contribution has been to bridge Pearl-type formalism with qualitative methodology in political science. Mahoney has championed the idea that process tracing can be recast as an informal (or formal) exercise in Bayesian hypothesis updating. Rather than treating process-tracing evidence as providing deterministic proof, the Bayesian reformulation treats each piece of within-case evidence as data that updates the *prior probability* of a causal hypothesis. As Alan Jacobs and Macartan Humphreys (2015) argue, Bayesian rules permit strong causal inferences from small data slivers because the repeated occurrence of theorized outcomes would be highly improbable if the theory were false [2].

### The Core Tension

The fundamental divergence is epistemological. Process tracing, as Bennett and Checkel articulate it, treats causal mechanisms as *ontologically real* social processes that can be observed and documented through careful within-case investigation. The researcher's task is to *trace the causal chain* by assembling diagnostic evidence. Pearl's framework, by contrast, treats causal mechanisms as *formal objects within a mathematical model*—their properties are derived from the structure of the causal graph and the rules of do-calculus, not from narrative reconstruction. Mahoney occupies a middle position, seeking to formalize the informal logic of process tracing using Bayesian probability while preserving its qualitative, within-case orientation.

## II. Equifinality in Comparative Case Studies

**Equifinality**—the phenomenon where multiple distinct causal pathways lead to the same outcome—is a central challenge in comparative research. Each framework handles it differently.

**Process tracing** addresses equifinality by tracing specific causal chains within individual cases and eliminating intervening variables. Because the method works *within* cases rather than *across* them, it can map the particular sequence of events and decisions that produced a given outcome in a specific context. When applied across multiple cases, process tracing enables charting complex, differing routes to the same outcome, allowing researchers to specify *which* causal pathway operated in *which* case [1][2]. This makes equifinality an empirical finding to be documented rather than a statistical nuisance to be controlled.

**QCA**, following Charles Ragin, embraces equifinality as a core design feature. As a set-theoretic approach using Boolean algebra, QCA explicitly models multiple distinct combinations of conditions (configurations) that can be sufficient for the same outcome. The method reduces all possible variable combinations to a minimum set of descriptive inferences ("prime implicates"), revealing the multiple causal recipes that produce an outcome [3]. Ragin's framework treats equifinality not as an exception but as the norm in social causation.

**Pearl's structural approach** handles equifinality differently—it requires researchers to specify the causal graph *a priori*, including all pathways to the outcome. Multiple pathways can be encoded in the graph, but the formalism demands that they be stated explicitly and tested against the data using the rules of do-calculus. There is no discovery of equifinality through data reduction (as in QCA); rather, equifinality must be *theorized* and then *confirmed*.

## III. Confounder Identification in Observational Data

This is the domain where the methodological tension is sharpest.

**Pearl's framework** provides the most rigorous formal solution: the back-door criterion identifies the minimal set of covariates that must be conditioned on to isolate a causal effect, while the front-door criterion handles cases where confounders are unobservable. These are *mathematical results* derived from the structure of the causal graph [5].

**Process tracing** addresses confounders through a fundamentally different logic: it uses within-case diagnostic evidence to *rule out spurious correlations* by examining whether the hypothesized causal mechanism actually operated in the case at hand. If the mechanism can be directly observed—through archival evidence, participant testimony, or other trace evidence—then the risk of confounding is mitigated not by statistical control but by *mechanistic confirmation* [1][2].

**QCA** is the most vulnerable on confounders. Because it operates on cross-case data using Boolean minimization, it identifies *associational* patterns (configurations associated with outcomes) but cannot, by itself, establish that these configurations are *causal*. The method must be supplemented with process tracing, formal logic, or domain expertise to establish the causal status of identified pathways [3].

## IV. Three Empirical Applications

### A. Theda Skocpol's Analysis of Revolutionary Causation

Skocpol's *States and Social Revolutions* (1979) provides a landmark test case. Skocpol argued that social revolutions result from the simultaneous occurrence of **state breakdown** (triggered by international military pressures and domestic dominant-class resistance to resource extraction) and **peasant rebellion**. Her comparative historical analysis examined the French, Russian, and Chinese revolutions, using J.S. Mill's methods of agreement and difference, with non-revolutionary cases (17th-century England, 19th-century Prussia and Japan) serving as controls to avoid selection on the dependent variable [4].

From a **process-tracing** perspective, Skocpol's work is a prototype: she traces the specific causal chain from international pressure → state fiscal crisis → administrative/military disintegration → peasant uprising → revolutionary transformation. The mechanism is structural—the state's capacity collapses, creating a political opening for rural insurgency [4].

From a **Pearl/Bayesian** perspective, Skocpol's analysis has been contested on several grounds. Ira Katznelson questioned whether Mill's method of difference could overcome the problem of numerous potential explanatory variables in small-N comparison. Barbara Geddes argued that her case selection was ill-justified and demonstrated that the theory failed replication when tested against nine Latin American countries. However, James Mahoney and Gary Goertz (2004) mounted a powerful defense, arguing that Geddes's cases fell outside Skocpol's explicitly stated **scope conditions** (agrarian bureaucracies). When cases were properly selected within those scope conditions, the theory was consistent with an expanded set of cases [4].

This debate illustrates the core tension: Skocpol's approach privileges scope conditions, causal mechanism specificity, and within-case processual evidence. Her critics, working in a more quantitative tradition, demand statistical robustness and replicability across broader case universes. Mahoney and Goertz's defense effectively argued that the quantitative critics were applying the wrong inferential framework—testing a scope-limited theory against cases it was never designed to explain.

### B. The QCA Tradition Following Charles Ragin

QCA, developed by Ragin in 1987, occupies a distinctive position between qualitative and quantitative traditions. It uses set-theoretic logic and Boolean algebra to analyze cross-case patterns, employing metrics of **consistency** (the degree to which cases conform to a set relationship) and **coverage** (the proportion of observations exhibiting the outcome explained by a specific pathway) [3].

Since 2010, the QCA tradition has been subjected to devastating critiques from the quantitative causal inference community:

- **Simon Hug** used Monte Carlo simulations to demonstrate that QCA's inductive use and measurement errors lead to systematically problematic inferences.
- **Krogslund, Choi, and Poertner** proved that fuzzy-set QCA results are highly sensitive to minor parametric changes and researcher confirmation bias.
- **Bear Braumoeller** exposed QCA's vulnerability to multiple inference, developing a formal test showing that even highly convincing QCA findings could be products of chance rather than true causal links [3].

These critiques are fundamentally about the gap between *pattern identification* and *causal identification*. QCA excels at finding combinatorial patterns in data, but without a formal theory of confounders (à la Pearl) or mechanistic within-case evidence (à la Bennett and Checkel), those patterns remain associational. The post-2010 methodological response has been to increasingly pair QCA with process tracing—using QCA to identify causal configurations cross-nationally and process tracing to verify the causal mechanisms within individual cases.

### C. Mixed-Methods Studies of Democratic Breakdown

Recent studies of democratic breakdown and erosion have become a testing ground for methodological integration. Contemporary research in this domain typically combines cross-case statistical analysis (identifying structural correlates of democratic decline—economic crisis, institutional weakness, populist mobilization) with within-case process tracing (examining how specific democratic norms are subverted through particular sequences of executive action, media capture, and judicial manipulation).

The current methodological consensus in this subfield, as reflected in leading work, treats process tracing and cross-case analysis as **complementary rather than competing**. Process tracing provides the mechanistic account of *how* democracy breaks down in specific cases, while statistical analysis establishes the *scope conditions* and *probabilistic generalizations* about which structural factors make breakdown more likely. Bayesian updating offers a formal bridge: prior probabilities derived from cross-case statistical analysis are updated using within-case diagnostic evidence, producing posterior assessments that integrate both sources of information [2].

## V. How Quantitative Critiques Have Reshaped Process Tracing Since 2010

The quantitative causal inference community's critiques—particularly regarding **selection bias** and **post-treatment conditioning**—have driven substantial methodological reform in process tracing:

1. **Formal Bayesian Reformulation**: The work of Jacobs and Humphreys (2015) and others has pushed process tracing toward explicit Bayesian updating protocols. Researchers are increasingly expected to specify prior probabilities, identify the diagnostic weight of different types of evidence, and calculate posterior probabilities of causal hypotheses. This addresses the critique that process tracing relied on informal, subjective inference.

2. **Selection Bias Awareness**: Gary King, Robert Keohane, and Sidney Verba's (1994) critique of qualitative research's limited "degrees of freedom" has been joined by more specific concerns about case selection. Contemporary process tracing protocols emphasize the importance of not selecting cases solely on the dependent variable and of using cross-case variation to establish scope conditions—precisely the lesson from the Skocpol-Geddes-Mahoney debate [4].

3. **Post-Treatment Conditioning**: Quantitative methodologists have highlighted that process tracing can inadvertently condition on post-treatment variables—examining causal mechanisms that are themselves consequences of the independent variable, thereby introducing bias. Pearl's back-door criterion provides a formal framework for identifying which variables can legitimately be conditioned on, and process tracing protocols have been revised to distinguish between examining *mechanisms* (legitimate) and *post-treatment outcomes* (potentially biasing) [2][5].

4. **Mixed-Methods Integration**: The dominant response to quantitative critiques has been to embed process tracing within mixed-methods designs. Process tracing now routinely serves as the within-case component of research designs that also include cross-case statistical analysis, combining the mechanistic depth of qualitative evidence with the generalizability of quantitative inference [2].

## VI. Current Methodological Consensus

The field is converging on a **division of inferential labor**:

- **Cross-case statistical analysis** (including both frequentist regression-based approaches and Pearl-type structural causal models) establishes generalizable patterns, identifies scope conditions, and formally addresses confounders through back-door/front-door criteria or instrumental variables.
- **Within-case process tracing** provides mechanistic confirmation, tests hypothesized causal chains against diagnostic evidence, and identifies the specific pathways through which causal effects operate.
- **Bayesian updating** offers the formal bridge between these two modes of inference, allowing researchers to integrate statistical priors with qualitative evidence.
- **QCA** remains valuable as a cross-case method for identifying combinatorial causal patterns, particularly in medium-N settings, but must be supplemented with either process tracing (for mechanism verification) or formal causal models (for confounder identification) to sustain causal claims [3].

However, fundamental epistemological differences persist. Pearl's framework demands that causal assumptions be stated *before* data examination and tested through formal mathematical criteria. Process tracing, even in its Bayesian reformulation, allows for more iterative, abductive reasoning—researchers revise their understanding of causal mechanisms as evidence accumulates within a case. Whether these logics can be fully reconciled or must remain in productive tension remains the central unresolved question in the methodology of causal inference in political science.
