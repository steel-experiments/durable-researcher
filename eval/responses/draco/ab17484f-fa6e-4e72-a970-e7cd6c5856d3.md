# Promissory Estoppel Analysis: Hayes v. Tech Company — Remote Work Enforcement on Summary Judgment

## Executive Summary

Jordan Hayes faces significant but not insurmountable barriers to enforcing the full remote work arrangement on summary judgment. While the **substantial detriment** element is clearly satisfied ($40,000 housing loss plus lease obligations), the **clear promise** and **reasonable reliance** elements present genuine disputes of material fact — particularly given the tension between the written merger clause and the subsequent June email confirmation. The company can likely negate at least one essential element of Hayes's claim as a matter of law, but the ambiguity in "hybrid schedule per company policy," the corroborating June email, and the comparable written approvals given to two peer engineers generate sufficient factual disputes that **Hayes is more likely to survive than to win summary judgment in his own favor**, and the company's cross-motion for summary judgment should also be denied.

## I. Legal Framework

### A. Promissory Estoppel — Four Elements

Under the Restatement (Second) of Contracts § 90 and general common-law principles, promissory estoppel requires: **(1)** a clear and definite promise, **(2)** reasonable reliance on that promise, **(3)** substantial detriment resulting from the reliance, and **(4)** injustice if the promise is not enforced [1]. The doctrine applies "even if a formal contract does not exist, such as when there is no consideration to support a binding agreement" [1]. The canonical case, *Hoffman v. Red Owl Stores, Inc.*, enforced a promise where the promisor induced the promisee to sell property and invest money in reliance on representations about a franchise opportunity [1].

### B. Texas Summary Judgment Standard

Under Tex. R. Civ. P. 166a(c), a movant must establish that **no genuine issue of material fact exists** and that it is entitled to **judgment as a matter of law**. The court reviews evidence *de novo*, taking as true all evidence favorable to the nonmovant, indulging every reasonable inference, and resolving all doubts in the nonmovant's favor [5][6]. The movant bears the initial burden to conclusively negate at least one essential element of the opposing party's claim. If successful, the burden shifts to the nonmovant to produce competent evidence raising a genuine factual dispute [5][6].

### C. Parol Evidence Rule and Merger Clauses

The parol evidence rule renders prior or contemporaneous verbal agreements inadmissible when they contradict a fully integrated written contract. A merger clause — such as the bolded statement that "This agreement supersedes all prior verbal representations" — strongly indicates complete integration [2]. However, two exceptions are relevant:

- **Ambiguity Exception**: If the written contract's language is reasonably susceptible to more than one meaning, parol evidence becomes admissible to determine intent [2].
- **Collateral Contract Exception**: Evidence of a separate, collateral agreement may be admissible if it does not contradict the writing and concerns a term not ordinarily included in the main contract [2].

### D. Apparent Authority Under Texas Law

Under Texas precedent, apparent authority arises only through the **principal's** acts, not the agent's self-representations (*Ins. Co. of N. America v. Morris*, 981 S.W.2d 667; *Lifshutz v. Lifshutz*, 199 S.W.3d 9). A party dealing with an agent has a **duty to ascertain** the scope of that agent's authority; failure to do so means acting at their own risk [3]. In *Guerra v. Wal-Mart Stores* (2009), the court held a manager's lifetime employment promise unenforceable because the employee received written notice restricting modification authority to specific executives [3].

## II. Element-by-Element Analysis

### Element 1: Clear and Definite Promise

**Strengths for Hayes:**
- The hiring manager made an express verbal promise of full remote work during April 2024 negotiations.
- The manager reaffirmed this promise in a **June email**, creating a documentary trail beyond mere oral testimony [1][4].
- Two other engineers hired simultaneously received **written remote approvals**, confirming that full remote work arrangements were within the company's recognized set of employment terms [4][7].

**Weaknesses for Hayes:**
- The May 1, 2024 offer letter — which Hayes accepted — stated **"hybrid schedule per company policy"** and contained a **bolded merger clause** superseding all prior verbal representations [2][5].
- HR records confirm the manager **lacked actual authority** to modify standard terms [3][7].
- The two peer engineers' written approvals actually **undermine** Hayes's position: they demonstrate the company had a formal, documented process for remote work exceptions — a process Hayes's manager bypassed [3][5].

**Assessment:** This element presents a **genuine issue of material fact**. The June email creates evidence of a promise, but its legal effect is contested. Crucially, the timing matters: if the June email is characterized as a reaffirmation of a prior verbal promise that was already superseded by the May 1 written contract, it fails as a "clear promise" enforceable against the company. If, however, it is characterized as a **new, post-contractual representation** by an agent who appeared authorized, it may survive. The ambiguity in "hybrid schedule per company policy" — which does not define the specific remote/office ratio — may open the door for the June email under the **ambiguity exception** to the parol evidence rule [2]. This factual dispute cannot be resolved as a matter of law on summary judgment.

### Element 2: Reasonable Reliance

**Strengths for Hayes:**
- Hayes sold a Seattle home at a $40,000 loss and signed a Texas lease on May 15 — concrete, quantifiable actions tied to the relocation [4][7].
- Under apparent authority principles, it is arguable that a hiring manager negotiating recruitment terms possesses at least **apparent authority** to discuss work arrangement logistics. Internal HR restrictions unknown to Hayes should not defeat this expectation [4].
- Hayes worked fully remote through June **without objection** from the company, which could be interpreted as the company acquiescing to the arrangement [4].

**Weaknesses for Hayes:**
- Hayes **signed a written contract** containing an explicit, bolded merger clause that contradicted the verbal promise. Under Texas law, a reasonably prudent person cannot rely on a verbal representation that directly contradicts the express terms of a document they have signed (*Lifshutz* standard) [3][5].
- The *Guerra* precedent is directly adverse: an employee who received written notice of modification restrictions could not enforce a manager's contrary verbal promise [3].
- Hayes had a **duty to ascertain** the scope of the manager's authority. The merger clause itself served as written notice that prior verbal representations were not part of the agreement [3].
- Hayes's continued fully remote work without objection may actually weaken his position — it suggests the company's tolerance was permissive (during onboarding) rather than contractual.

**Assessment:** This is Hayes's **weakest element**. Under Texas law, reliance on a verbal promise is unreasonable as a matter of law when the claimant has signed a writing that expressly supersedes verbal representations and states different terms [3][5]. The company has a strong argument that it can negate reasonable reliance conclusively on summary judgment. However, the June email — post-dating the signed contract — complicates this analysis. If the June email is treated as new information that reanimated Hayes's belief in the promise, a court may find the reliance reasonableness question should go to a jury. This is a **close call**, but the weight of Texas precedent favors the company on this element.

### Element 3: Substantial Detriment

**Strengths for Hayes:**
- The $40,000 loss on the Seattle home sale is a **quantifiable, substantial financial detriment** directly tied to the relocation [4][5].
- The Texas lease obligation represents additional financial commitment.
- These costs were incurred in direct sequence: promise → acceptance → home sale → lease signing → relocation.

**Weaknesses for Hayes:**
- The company may argue that relocation costs are incident to any job change and do not constitute the type of "substantial detriment" contemplated by promissory estoppel.
- Hayes presumably gained employment and a salary, offsetting at least some of the detriment.

**Assessment:** This element is **clearly satisfied** and is Hayes's strongest. Under *Hoffman v. Red Owl*, selling property at a loss in reliance on a promise is the archetype of substantial detriment [1]. The $40,000 loss plus lease obligations are economically significant and directly traceable to the relocation. The company cannot likely negate this element on summary judgment.

### Element 4: Injustice Without Enforcement

**Strengths for Hayes:**
- Hayes suffered a $40,000 loss based on assurances from a company representative during a negotiation the company initiated and benefited from [1][4].
- The company allowed Hayes to work remotely for two months without objection, arguably creating an impression the arrangement was accepted [4].
- The manager's retirement in June — immediately after the email confirmation — creates an inference that the company's position shifted only because the champion of the promise departed.

**Weaknesses for Hayes:**
- The company has a **strong countervailing justice argument**: enforcing an unauthorized verbal promise that contradicts a signed written contract would undermine the reliability of written employment agreements [2][5].
- Two peer engineers followed proper procedure (written approvals); enforcing Hayes's claim would reward bypassing established channels [3][7].
- The July 1 policy update applies company-wide, not discriminatorily — the injustice claim is weakened if the policy change was uniform and nondiscriminatory.

**Assessment:** This element presents a **genuine issue of material fact**. The balancing of equities — Hayes's $40,000 loss versus the company's interest in enforcing its written contract and uniform policy — is quintessentially a jury question. Courts are generally reluctant to grant summary judgment on balancing-of-equities inquiries.

## III. Threshold Legal Barriers

### A. Does the Merger Clause Bar Promissory Estoppel?

This is the dispositive legal question. The merger clause bars **contract claims** based on prior verbal representations, but promissory estoppel is an **independent equitable doctrine** that does not require a contract at all [1]. Courts across jurisdictions are split on whether merger clauses categorically preclude promissory estoppel claims. Some hold that the doctrine applies only when no writing exists; others permit it when the equity demands it regardless of the writing. Under Texas law, this question has not been definitively resolved by the Supreme Court, creating additional uncertainty that **precludes summary judgment** for either party.

### B. The June Email — New Promise or Mere Confirmation?

The timing of the June email is critical. If it merely confirmed a pre-contractual verbal promise, it is likely barred by the May 1 merger clause. If it constituted a **new representation** made after contract formation — by a manager who was still employed and acting in his capacity — it may be admissible as post-contractual evidence that is not subject to the parol evidence rule at all. This characterization is a **genuine factual dispute** requiring extrinsic evidence and cannot be resolved on summary judgment.

### C. Apparent Authority

Under Texas law (*Guerra v. Wal-Mart*), the manager lacked apparent authority because: (1) the merger clause provided written notice of limitation; (2) HR records confirm lack of actual authority; (3) Hayes failed to ascertain the scope of authority [3]. However, Hayes can counter-argue that the company placed the manager in a negotiation role and never disclosed the authority limitation, and that two peer engineers' written approvals show remote work terms were within the **general scope** of hiring negotiations — even if the company required written form. This is a factual dispute inappropriate for summary adjudication.

## IV. Summary Judgment Determination

### A. Can Hayes Win Summary Judgment in His Favor?

**No.** Hayes cannot affirmatively enforce the remote work arrangement on summary judgment. To do so, he would need to establish all four promissory estoppel elements as **undisputed matters of law**. The significant disputes around whether the June email constitutes an enforceable promise, whether reliance was reasonable given the merger clause, and whether the manager had authority preclude this. Multiple material facts are genuinely disputed, and the balancing of equities under Element 4 is inherently a jury question.

### B. Can the Company Win Summary Judgment Dismissing Hayes's Claim?

**Likely not, but the outcome is close.** The company's strongest argument is that **reasonable reliance** can be negated as a matter of law: Hayes signed a contract with a bolded merger clause contradicting the verbal promise, and under *Guerra* and *Lifshutz*, such reliance is unreasonable per se under Texas law [3][5]. If the court accepts this argument, it can negate an essential element and grant summary judgment for the company.

However, three factors create sufficient factual dispute to deny the company's motion:
1. **The June email** post-dates the contract and raises a genuine issue about whether it constitutes a new promise or modification rather than a superseded prior representation.
2. **The ambiguity** in "hybrid schedule per company policy" opens the door for parol evidence under the ambiguity exception, making the parties' intent a factual question [2].
3. **The $40,000 detriment** is substantial and undisputed, creating strong equitable grounds for a jury to consider.
