# Legal Frameworks of Slavery and Trade in the 1730s British Empire
## A Research Brief for Historical Fiction

## 1. The Molasses Act (1733): Statutory Language and Purpose

**Citation:** 6 Geo. 2. c. 13.

The Molasses Act levied prohibitive duties on imports to British colonies from non-British sources:

- **Nine pence per gallon** on foreign rum and spirits
- **Six pence per gallon** on molasses and syrups
- **Five shillings per hundredweight avoirdupois** on sugars and paneles

**Crucially for your novel, the Act did NOT explicitly classify enslaved people as property or chattels.** It classified goods strictly by origin and volume. There is no clause in the Molasses Act itself that addresses the legal status of enslaved persons — it is a tariff statute, pure and simple.

However, the Act's *purpose* was inextricable from the slave economy. It was driven by British West Indies plantation owners to protect their sugar-slave economy by pricing out French and Dutch Caribbean competitors. Col. Martin Bladen of the Board of Trade explicitly stated the Act's intent was a "prohibitive duty" designed to keep New England dependent on the British crown rather than "raising the French colonies at the expense of ours." The Act was widely evaded through smuggling and bribery, was renewed in 1737, 1745, 1753, and 1757, and was eventually replaced by the more strictly enforced Sugar Act of 1763 (4 Geo. 3. c. 15) — which itself helped trigger the American Revolution.

**Where to find the original:** The full text is in the British Parliamentary statutes (Statutes at Large). The UK National Archives and the Parliamentary Archives at Westminster hold the original enrolled copies.

## 2. Colonial Slave Codes: The Chattel Language

### Barbados (the foundational model)

The Barbados Slave Code of 1661, formally titled **"An Act for the Better Ordering and Governing of Negroes,"** was reenacted with modifications in 1676, 1682, and 1688. It was the template for every subsequent British plantation code in the Caribbean and mainland North America.

The chattel classification is explicit in the preamble. Key statutory language:

> The law's purpose was to **"protect them [slaves] as we do men's other goods and Chattels."**

The code described Black people as **"an heathenish, brutish and an uncertaine, dangerous kind of people,"** and on that basis denied them English common law rights, including the right to life or trial by a jury of peers.

**Punishment provisions:**
- First offense (violence against a Christian): severe whipping
- Second offense: **"severely whipped, his nose slit, and be burned in some part of his face with a hot iron"**

**Master impunity clause:**
> **"if any Negro or other slave under punishment by his master unfortunately shall suffer in life or member… no person whatsoever shall be liable to any fine therefore."**

Willfully killing an enslaved person incurred only a treasury fine — or "double the Value" paid to the owner if the slave belonged to someone else (i.e., the legal consequence was compensation for destruction of *property*, not punishment for taking a life).

**Where to find the original:** Digitized transcriptions of the Barbados laws up to 1699 are available via the University of Michigan Library's Early English Books Online, in *The Laws of Barbados Collected in One Volume* by William Rawlin (No. 329 for the 1688 Act). Original physical copies are in The National Archives (UK).

### Jamaica

Jamaica adopted a slave code in 1664, which was substantially revised in 1684 as the **Jamaica Consolidated Slave Act**, modeled directly on the Barbados code. Like the Barbados original, it legally classified enslaved people as chattels and commodities to underpin the cash-crop economy.

By the 1730s, Jamaica's enslaved population was not self-sustaining — it decreased by roughly 3% per year due to brutal conditions and disease. The average lifespan of an enslaved person after arrival was less than two decades. This decade also saw major uprisings by the Jamaican Maroons (escaped Africans who formed sovereign communities), culminating in the 1738 treaties that ended the First Maroon War and granted the Maroons land and limited autonomy.

**Where to find the original:** The colonial statutes at large for Jamaica should be in the UK National Archives (series CO 139 — Jamaica Original Correspondence, and CO 140 — Jamaica Sessional Papers). The 1684 Consolidated Act is the key statute in force during your 1730s setting.

### South Carolina

South Carolina's code was directly descended from the Barbados-Jamaica lineage. Barbadian settlers brought their slave codes to the mainland colony in the 1670s–80s, and the Lords Proprietors revised the 1663 "Declarations and Proposals" specifically to accommodate them.

The 1669 *Fundamental Constitutions* established:

> **"Every freeman of Carolina shall have absolute power and authority over his negro slave."**

South Carolina's first comprehensive slave code was passed in **1695**, modeled on the 1684 Jamaican code. This 1695 act was the law in force throughout your 1730s setting. The most explicit chattel language came in statutory form:

> Enslaved people were **"deemed, sold, taken, reputed and adjudged in law, to be chattels, personal in the hands of their owners and possessors, and their executors, administrators, and assigns, TO ALL INTENTS, CONSTRUCTIONS AND PURPOSES WHATSOEVER."**

The law further stripped enslaved people of legal personhood:

> **"A slave is not generally regarded as legally capable of being within the peace of the State. He is not a citizen, and is not in that character entitled to her protection."**

After the Stono Rebellion in September 1739, South Carolina passed the comprehensive **Negro Act of 1740**, which added severe restrictions on movement, assembly, and education. It also legally authorized white colonists to kill "notorious runaway slaves" who had been absent for 12 months or more if they could not be recaptured.

## 3. Imperial Parliamentary Law vs. Colonial Plantation Codes

This is a critical distinction for your novel, because characters operating in different arenas of the British Empire would have experienced radically different legal regimes:

| Dimension | British Parliamentary Law (Westminster) | Colonial Plantation Codes (Local Assemblies) |
|---|---|---|
| **Focus** | Imperial trade regulation, tariffs, mercantilism | Daily control, punishment, and dehumanization of enslaved people |
| **Example** | Molasses Act 1733 — regulated *commerce* of the slave-driven economy | Barbados/Jamaica/SC codes — regulated *bodies* of the enslaved |
| **Classification of enslaved people** | Not directly addressed; enslaved people were implicit in trade but not legally classified in tariff statutes | Explicitly classified as "goods and Chattels," "chattels personal," property without legal personhood |
| **Punishment** | Parliament did not dictate how to punish enslaved people | Colonial codes specified whipping, mutilation (nose-slitting, branding), execution; protected masters who killed slaves |
| **Legal precedent** | English common law and commercial law | Drew heavily on Roman law regarding slave status; no established English precedent for race-based chattel slavery |
| **Legislative body** | Parliament at Westminster | Local colonial assemblies (Barbados Assembly, Jamaica Assembly, South Carolina Commons House of Assembly) |

**In practice:** There was no central parliamentary slave code. Local colonial judges and authorities developed these laws independently, without established English precedents for race-based slavery. A merchant in Bristol or London operated under commercial law; a plantation overseer in St. Andrew Parish, Jamaica, operated under the colonial slave code that classified the people he "managed" as chattels.

## 4. Recent Peer-Reviewed Scholarship (2013–2016)

The most relevant recent scholarship I identified through this research:

- **Jerome S. Handler (2016):** "Custom and law: The status of enslaved Africans in seventeenth-century Barbados," *Slavery & Abolition*, Vol. 37, Issue 2. Examines the legal construction of slave status in Barbados and the interplay between local custom and codified law.

- **Edward B. Rugemer (2013):** "The Development of Mastery and Race in the Comprehensive Slave Codes of the Greater Caribbean during the Seventeenth Century," *The William and Mary Quarterly*, Vol. 70, Issue 3. Traces how Barbados's code became the foundational model transmitted to Jamaica, South Carolina, and beyond.

- **Linford D. Fisher (2014):** "'Dangerous Designes': The 1676 Barbados Act to Prohibit New England Indian Slave Importation," *The William and Mary Quarterly*, Vol. 71, Issue 1. Explores how Barbados legislated racial categories and restrictions on who could be enslaved.

- **Vincent Brown** and **Justin Roberts** are also noted by scholars in this field as producing significant recent work on mortality, management, and legal dehumanization of enslaved people in 18th-century Jamaica and the British Caribbean.

## 5. The Debate: Was the Molasses Act About Trade Regulation or Slave Control?

The current scholarly consensus is nuanced — it was not *either/or* but *both simultaneously*, with trade regulation as the explicit mechanism and protection of the slave economy as the underlying purpose:

**The trade regulation argument (traditional view):** The Act was a mercantilist measure designed to force New England colonies to trade with British West Indies sugar producers rather than French and Dutch competitors. The statutory language is entirely about tariffs and trade — there is no reference to slave control whatsoever.

**The slave-economy argument (what recent scholars emphasize):** The Act's *practical effect* was to protect a system built entirely on enslaved labor. The British West Indies sugar economy was inseparable from slavery. By shielding British planters from French competition, Parliament was protecting the slave-sugar complex. The triangular trade — manufactured goods to Africa, enslaved people to the Caribbean, molasses and sugar to Europe and New England — was an integrated system. The Molasses Act protected all three legs of that triangle.

**The key point for your novel:** A character in the 1730s would likely not have framed this as a "trade vs. slavery" debate — the distinction would have been nearly meaningless to them. Protecting the sugar trade *meant* protecting slavery. The legal separation (Parliament regulates commerce, colonies regulate enslaved bodies) reflected practical governance, not moral distinction. No one in 1733 was arguing that the Molasses Act was "really about" slave control in a conspiratorial sense — it was obviously and transparently about protecting an economic system that everyone understood was built on enslaved labor.
