# Conversational UI Patterns in Voice-First Healthcare Applications: Impact on Medication Adherence Among Elderly Patients (65+) with Mild Cognitive Impairment

## Executive Summary

Voice-first healthcare applications show promising but mixed results for medication adherence in elderly patients with mild cognitive impairment. Catalia Health's Mabu robot demonstrates the strongest evidence for sustained engagement through physical co-presence and empathetic conversational design, while consumer platforms (Amazon Alexa, Google Assistant) offer accessible medication reminder functionality but lack the clinical interaction depth needed for cognitively impaired populations. Critical gaps persist in the research literature regarding specific confirmation dialog patterns, prompt verbosity thresholds, and longitudinal engagement data beyond 90 days for the 65+ MCI demographic.

---

## 1. System Comparison: Three Deployed Voice-First Healthcare Platforms

### 1.1 Amazon Alexa — Medication Reminders

Amazon Alexa pioneered HIPAA-eligible health data integration in 2019, enabling secure third-party "Skills" that manage protected health information [4]. The medication reminder system operates through straightforward voice commands (e.g., "Alexa, remind me to take my blood pressure pill at 8 a.m."), providing a low-friction mechanism for daily adherence support [4]. Alexa's interaction design follows a **command-and-confirm pattern**: the user issues a directive, Alexa acknowledges with a brief verbal confirmation, and the reminder fires at the specified time.

**Strengths for elderly populations:** Hands-free accessibility is particularly beneficial for individuals with limited mobility, visual impairments, or low technology literacy [4]. The ambient nature of smart speakers means no active device management is required.

**Limitations for MCI patients:** Alexa's default interaction pattern uses brief, system-centric confirmations that may not provide sufficient redundancy for patients with memory deficits. The platform lacks adaptive conversational depth — it does not follow up to verify whether a medication was actually taken, nor does it escalate when reminders are repeatedly ignored. Voice recognition struggles with accents, dialects, and speech impediments remain a documented source of input errors and reduced patient confidence [4]. There is no published longitudinal study specifically evaluating Alexa's medication reminder effectiveness in patients over 65 with MCI.

### 1.2 Google Assistant — Health Tracking Features

Google Assistant's healthcare positioning differs from Alexa's, focusing less on direct medical data management and more on ecosystem integration. Google supports health-related "Actions" from official providers and integrates with Google Fit, Health Connect (supporting 50+ health data types), and a Medical Records API using FHIR® format for medications, allergies, immunizations, and lab results [3].

**Interaction design approach:** Google leverages its Gemini AI models for complex health queries with conversational follow-up capability, functioning as a voice-first health *information* retrieval system rather than a medication management companion [3]. Medication reminders exist but are less differentiated from general reminder functionality.

**Limitations for the target population:** Google Assistant currently lacks the dedicated, empathetic conversational sequences that characterize clinically-oriented systems. There is no evidence of adaptive dialog branching based on patient responses, and the system does not provide the kind of daily check-in routines shown to support adherence in chronic disease management. Its health features are primarily designed for the general wellness-tracking population rather than geriatric clinical care [3][4].

### 1.3 Catalia Health's Mabu Robot — Specialized Medical Voice Assistant

Mabu represents a fundamentally different design philosophy: a purpose-built, physically present healthcare companion rather than a repurposed consumer device. Founded by Cory Kidd (MIT Media Lab), Mabu's design is grounded in empirical research showing that **sharing physical space and making eye contact with an interactive robot yields the same psychological effects and engagement as face-to-face human interaction** [1][2].

**Interaction design strategy:**
- **Empathetic opening sequences:** Conversations begin with open-ended health check-ins ("How are you feeling?") rather than task directives [1][2].
- **Adaptive dialog branching:** AI algorithms based on clinical "bedside manner" psychology dynamically adjust conversation flow based on patient responses, isolating whether symptoms relate to disease or medication [1][2].
- **Immediate feedback loops:** Mabu provides real-time clinical guidance (e.g., American Heart Association guidelines for heart failure patients) while simultaneously transmitting data to clinicians via HIPAA-compliant cloud systems [1][2].
- **Multi-channel flexibility:** Voice-first but supplemented by mobile phone interactions, providing redundancy for patients who may miss verbal cues [1].
- **Escalation pathways:** When concerning symptoms are reported, Mabu suggests actionable next steps ("You might want to call your doctor") and proactively relays information to the care team [1].

**Engagement evidence:** Kidd's randomized controlled trial at Boston University Medical Center demonstrated that patients using a tabletop robot coach were **significantly more likely to stick with their health program** compared to those using a computer running identical software — establishing that the physical embodiment of the conversational agent itself drives engagement, independent of the software logic [1][2]. In longitudinal deployments with Kaiser Permanente (heart failure patients) and Pfizer (12-month pilot launched September 2019), patients demonstrated exceptional sustained engagement, with Kidd noting that "in the end, the patients didn't want to give the robots back" [1][2].

**Business model implications for design:** Patients pay nothing; providers and pharmaceutical companies fund the service, which means Mabu's interaction design is optimized for clinical outcomes rather than consumer engagement metrics [1][2][5].

## 2. Geriatric HCI Findings: Confirmation Dialogs, Error Recovery, and Multi-Modal Feedback

### 2.1 Confirmation Dialog Patterns

The reviewed sources reveal a critical gap: **no published studies were found that directly compare confirmation dialog strategies (explicit vs. implicit vs. no confirmation) in voice-first medication systems for elderly patients with MCI.** However, indirect evidence suggests key principles:

- **Redundancy in confirmation** is likely essential for MCI populations. Mabu's approach of combining verbal feedback with clinician data relay creates a dual-confirmation pathway — the patient receives immediate verbal acknowledgment while the system simultaneously logs the interaction for professional oversight [1][2].
- **Consumer platforms' brief confirmations** ("OK, I'll remind you at 8 a.m.") may provide insufficient cognitive reinforcement for patients who may forget both the reminder and the confirmation within minutes [4].
- The ACHO voice assistant system (developed for polypharmacy management in rural elderly populations) represents a promising research direction, with published protocols for usability evaluation [6], but specific confirmation pattern outcomes were not accessible in the sources reviewed.

### 2.2 Error Recovery Mechanisms

Voice input errors remain a significant barrier. Documented error types include [4]:

- **Speech recognition failures:** Accents, dialects, and speech impediments — all more prevalent in elderly populations — cause misrecognition of medication names, dosages, and timing instructions.
- **Task abandonment from errors:** When voice recognition fails repeatedly, patients experience reduced confidence and may abandon the interaction entirely rather than attempt error recovery.
- **Privacy-related friction:** Systems requiring voice profiles or PINs for identity verification before sharing health data introduce additional error-prone steps [4].

Mabu partially addresses this through its physical presence and eye contact, which may increase patient willingness to persist through error-prone interactions [1][2]. However, specific error recovery mechanism evaluations for MCI populations were not found in the reviewed literature.

### 2.3 Multi-Modal Feedback (Voice + Visual + Haptic)

The evidence supports multi-modal feedback as an engagement correlate:

- **Voice + visual combinations** (e.g., smart screens displaying medication information alongside voice reminders) are identified as correlating with sustained patient engagement, particularly when daily check-ins combine modalities [4].
- **Physical embodiment as a modality:** Mabu's physical presence — its "small yellow body and large blue eyes" — functions as a persistent visual and spatial feedback mechanism that consumer smart speakers cannot replicate [1][2]. This embodied presence is the most distinctive multi-modal feature identified in the review, and it correlates with the strongest sustained engagement evidence.
- **Google's wearable integration** (Fitbit/Pixel Watch with haptic alerts, SpO2, heart rate monitoring) represents a voice + haptic + visual feedback pathway [3], but this has not been evaluated for medication adherence in elderly MCI patients.

## 3. Longitudinal Engagement and Sustained Use Beyond 90 Days

### 3.1 What Correlates with Sustained Engagement

Synthesizing across sources, the following interaction sequences and design features correlate with sustained engagement:

| Feature | Mabu | Alexa | Google Assistant |
|---|---|---|---|
| Physical co-presence | ✓ (strongest correlate) | ✗ | ✗ |
| Empathetic conversational openings | ✓ | ✗ | ✗ |
| Adaptive dialog branching | ✓ | ✗ | ✗ |
| Clinician feedback loop | ✓ (real-time) | ✗ | ✗ |
| Multi-modal (voice + visual) | ✓ (+ mobile) | ✓ (with Echo Show) | ✓ (with Nest Hub) |
| HIPAA-eligible data handling | ✓ | ✓ (since 2019) | Partial |
| Personalization over time | ✓ (AI-driven) | Limited | Limited |

**Key finding:** The interaction sequence that most strongly correlates with sustained engagement is **daily empathetic check-ins → adaptive follow-up → immediate feedback + clinician escalation**, a pattern exclusive to Mabu among the three systems [1][2]. The consumer platforms' command-remind-confirm pattern shows no published evidence of sustaining engagement beyond 90 days in any population.

### 3.2 Published Longitudinal Data

- **Mabu/Kaiser Permanente:** Heart failure patients used Mabu in their homes for approximately 1.5 years before the 2019 MIT News article, indicating sustained engagement well beyond 90 days [1].
- **Mabu/Pfizer:** A 12-month pilot was announced in September 2019, but quantitative outcomes were not published in the reviewed sources [5].
- **Digital Voice Assistant RCT (Hunegnaw et al., 2025):** A 12-month RCT found that a DVA intervention did **not** improve anti-osteoporosis drug adherence, knowledge, or attitudes in postmenopausal women [6] — a cautionary result suggesting that voice reminders alone, without the adaptive conversational depth of systems like Mabu, may be insufficient.
- **Kidd's original RCT:** Physical robot coaching produced significantly better program adherence than identical software on a standard computer, but specific 90-day thresholds and confidence scores were not reported in the reviewed sources [1][2].

## 4. Task Completion Rates, Error Types, and Patient-Reported Confidence

**This represents the most significant gap in the available evidence.** The reviewed sources do not contain:

- Quantitative task completion rates for medication reminders across the three platforms
- Categorized error taxonomies for voice input by elderly MCI patients
- Patient-reported confidence scores using standardized instruments
- Comparisons across different prompt verbosity levels (e.g., minimal vs. expanded confirmations)
- Systematic evaluations of explicit vs. implicit confirmation strategies

The ACHO system protocol (Luengo-Polo et al., 2021; Conde-Caballero et al., 2021) was designed to evaluate usability of a voice assistant for polypharmacy management in rural elderly populations [6], which may contain relevant data, but the full studies were not accessible during this review.

## 5. Synthesis and Design Implications

### What the evidence supports:

1. **Physical embodiment matters more than voice alone.** Mabu's physical presence and eye contact create engagement that purely voice-based systems cannot replicate, and this effect is grounded in MIT Media Lab research on human-robot psychological interaction [1][2].

2. **Empathetic, adaptive conversation sequences outperform command-response patterns.** Mabu's approach of opening with health check-ins and dynamically adapting dialog correlates with sustained engagement, while the consumer platforms' simpler reminder pattern shows no comparable longitudinal evidence [1][2][4].

3. **Voice-only reminders may be insufficient.** The 2025 RCT showing no adherence improvement from a digital voice assistant [6] suggests that simply adding voice reminders to a medication regimen — without adaptive conversation, feedback loops, or clinician integration — does not move the needle on adherence.

4. **Multi-modal redundancy is likely critical for MCI populations.** The combination of voice, visual, spatial, and human clinician feedback pathways creates multiple opportunities for information to register, even when cognitive processing is impaired [1][3][4].

### What remains unknown:

- Optimal prompt verbosity levels for MCI patients (how much confirmation is enough vs. overwhelming)
- Specific error recovery sequences that maintain patient confidence after voice misrecognition
- Quantitative 90-day retention benchmarks across platforms
- How cognitive impairment severity modulates the effectiveness of different confirmation strategies
- Whether consumer platforms can achieve Mabu-level engagement through software-only improvements

## Uncertainty & Gaps

- **No direct comparative studies** of Alexa, Google Assistant, and Mabu in the same patient population were found. The comparison is necessarily inferential, drawing on different study designs, populations, and outcome measures.
- **MCI-specific data is absent.** No reviewed study focused exclusively on the 65+ mild cognitive impairment population. Most geriatric voice interface studies involve broader elderly populations or postmenopausal women without confirmed MCI diagnosis.
- **Quantitative engagement metrics are unavailable** for any of the three platforms specifically for medication adherence beyond 90 days with standardized confidence scores. Mabu's sustained engagement is documented only anecdotally ("patients didn't want to give the robots back") and through continued deployment duration with Kaiser Permanente [1].
- **The Catalia Health website (cataliahealth.com) was compromised** at the time of research, returning sports betting content rather than company information, limiting access to potentially valuable primary data.
- **Prompt verbosity and confirmation strategy research** for voice UI in healthcare contexts appears to be an understudied area, or the relevant studies exist behind paywalls/in academic repositories not indexed by standard web search.
- **The negative DVA trial result (Hunegnaw et al., 2025)** is a critical counterpoint that cannot be fully contextualized without access to the full paper — it is unclear whether the failure was due to the voice modality itself, the specific interaction design, the patient population, or the clinical context (osteoporosis).
