## Clinical Management Report: Intermittent High-Grade AV Block in a 68-Year-Old Male

### Executive Summary

This patient presents a rapidly progressive conduction system disease — PR interval worsening from 200 ms to 240 ms in three weeks with intermittent dropped beats and symptomatic bradycardia (HR 52, lightheadedness, fatigue), temporally correlated with metoprolol initiation eight weeks ago. The combination of progressive conduction disease, a beta-blocker that may be unmasking or exacerbating underlying nodal pathology, and high social risk (lives alone, drives daily, no local support) creates an urgent clinical scenario that cannot safely wait three months for routine cardiology consultation. A tiered strategy — immediate beta-blocker adjustment, expedited 14-day external patch monitor with peer-to-peer insurance authorization, and clear clinical thresholds for emergency pacemaker consultation — is warranted.

### Part 1: Immediate Clinical Actions (Next 24–48 Hours)

#### 1A. Beta-Blocker Management — Mandatory First Step

The 2018 ACC/AHA/HRS Guideline on Bradycardia and Cardiac Conduction Delay establishes as a **Class I recommendation** that for symptomatic bradycardia, guideline-directed medical therapy should include reducing or discontinuing offending agents [1]. Metoprolol 50 mg BID was started eight weeks ago, which directly coincides with the onset of symptoms and ED visits.

**Recommended approach:**
- **Hold or significantly reduce metoprolol** — this is reversible and must be trialed before permanent pacing is considered [2].
- If the patient has known coronary artery disease, taper over 1–2 weeks rather than abrupt discontinuation [2].
- Immediate-release metoprolol carries nearly twice the risk of emergent bradycardia compared to sustained-release formulations; if any beta-blocker is resumed later, a different agent at a lower dose with closer monitoring should be used [2].
- **Critical caveat:** If the AV block persists or worsens after beta-blocker withdrawal, this confirms intrinsic conduction system disease requiring permanent pacing regardless of medication status [1].

### Part 2: Monitoring Strategy and Insurance Authorization Pathway

#### 2A. Why Standard Holter Monitoring Is Inadequate

Insurance denies Holter without documented arrhythmia. Even if approved, a 24–48 hour Holter has a diagnostic yield of only 43.9% for intermittent arrhythmias [4]. Nearly 30% of patients have their first arrhythmia detected only after the initial 48-hour monitoring period [4]. This patient's dropped beats are intermittent and have not been captured on 4–6 hour telemetry strips across four ED visits — confirming that longer-duration monitoring is essential.

#### 2B. Recommended Monitoring Tiers

| Tier | Modality | Duration | Real-Time Alerting | Diagnostic Yield | Insurance Pathway |
|------|----------|----------|-------------------|-----------------|-------------------|
| **1 (Preferred)** | Zio XT Patch or similar extended continuous monitor | Up to 14 days | No (data analyzed post-return) | 62.2% | Medically necessary for symptoms < every 48 hrs — no prior failed monitor required [3] |
| **2** | Mobile Cardiac Outpatient Telemetry (MCOT) | Up to 30 days | Yes (transmits in real-time) | Highest for infrequent events | Requires: non-diagnostic monitoring in past 60 days + cardiologist/EP consultation [3] |
| **3** | Implantable Loop Recorder (ILR) | Up to 3 years | Yes | Highest for very infrequent events | Only after failed external monitor trial [3] |

**Tier 1 — Zio XT Patch** is the most practical first step for this patient:
- Self-applied at home, water-resistant, no leads — feasible for a patient living alone [4].
- Continuous 14-day recording dramatically increases the probability of capturing intermittent dropped beats.
- Insurance authorization: BCBS and most commercial payers consider external continuous monitors recording >48 hours **medically necessary** for symptoms occurring less frequently than every 48 hours, without requiring prior failed monitors [3].

**Critical safety limitation:** The Zio patch does **not** provide real-time alerting. If the patient develops complete heart block, no one is notified. This is why beta-blocker adjustment and clear safety thresholds (Part 4) are essential.

**Tier 2 — MCOT** would be ideal for real-time safety monitoring but requires cardiology co-signature, creating a barrier given the 3-month wait. Solution: request an **urgent cardiology or electrophysiology consultation specifically for monitor co-ordering**, not for full evaluation. Many practices will accommodate a limited consultation for device ordering within days.

- **Documented progression:** PR interval increased from 200 ms to 240 ms in 3 weeks — this is progressive conduction disease, not a static finding.

**Step 4: If P2P fails, request an expedited external review** through the state insurance commissioner's office, citing patient safety.

|-----------|-------------------|

### Part 4: Clinical Thresholds for Immediate Pacemaker Consultation vs. Outpatient Workup

#### Immediate Pacemaker Consultation (Do NOT pursue outpatient monitoring — admit/transfer for urgent evaluation):

1. **Captured Mobitz II or high-grade AV block on any ECG or monitor strip.** Even a single non-conducted P wave without PR prolongation preceding it (distinguishing Mobitz II from Mobitz I) in a symptomatic patient is a Class I indication for permanent pacing [1].

2. **Symptomatic bradycardia persisting after beta-blocker withdrawal.** If holding metoprolol does not resolve the lightheadedness, fatigue, and bradycardia within 48–72 hours of appropriate washout, the block is intrinsic and pacing is indicated.

3. **Any syncopal episode or loss of consciousness.** Presyncope prompting four ED visits in six weeks is already a warning sign. Progression to frank syncope is a definitive threshold for urgent pacing.

4. **Heart rate below 40 bpm sustained or symptomatic pauses ≥3 seconds.** These represent hemodynamically significant bradycardia requiring intervention regardless of etiology.

5. **PR interval progression beyond 300 ms** or rapid further PR prolongation (e.g., from 240 ms to >280 ms on follow-up). A markedly prolonged PR interval with symptoms is a recognized Class IIa indication for pacing, as the delayed atrioventricular contraction causes suboptimal ventricular filling ("pseudo-pacemaker syndrome") [1].

6. **Development of bifascicular block + first-degree AV block.** This combination suggests trifascicular disease and carries high risk for progression to complete heart block.

#### Outpatient Workup Is Appropriate If:

- Beta-blocker reduction/cessation resolves symptoms and AV conduction normalizes (PR returns to baseline, no further dropped beats).
- Extended patch monitoring captures only Mobitz I (Wenckebach) physiology without sustained high-grade block.
- Symptoms are clearly correlated with non-cardiac etiologies after thorough evaluation.
