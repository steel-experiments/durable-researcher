# Comparative Analysis of Carbon Pricing Mechanisms: California, EU ETS, and China (2020–2024)

## Executive Summary

From 2020 to 2024, the world's three major carbon pricing systems diverged sharply in price signals, revenue generation, and structural maturity. The EU ETS emerged as the most financially consequential system—generating over EUR 80 billion in just two years (2023–2024) and driving emissions 47.6% below 2005 levels. California's cap-and-trade program delivered steady price growth to a record $41.76/tonne by early 2024 and accumulated $9.3 billion in climate investments over its lifetime. China's national ETS, the world's largest by coverage, remained in a formative phase with prices hovering near USD 13/tonne and 100% free allocation, though regulatory tightening in 2024 signaled growing ambition. All three systems proved resilient through COVID-19 disruptions, though through different mechanisms: the EU's Market Stability Reserve absorbed surplus allowances, California's floor price provided a buffer, and China's system launched only in mid-2021, after the sharpest economic shock had passed.

---

## 1. Allowance Price Trajectories

### EU ETS
The EU ETS experienced the most dramatic price volatility and recovery of the three systems. After prices crashed below EUR 15/tonne in early 2020 as pandemic lockdowns suppressed industrial demand, they staged a remarkable recovery—climbing above EUR 50 by late 2021 and peaking above EUR 100/tonne in mid-2022, driven by the energy crisis triggered by Russia's invasion of Ukraine. By 2024, prices had stabilized at an average auction clearing price of EUR 64.74 (USD 70.07), with secondary market prices averaging EUR 65.23. This trajectory reflected both the tightening of the cap under Phase 4 (2021–2030) and the Market Stability Reserve's (MSR) role in absorbing the surplus of over 1.5 billion allowances that had accumulated during prior phases.

### California
California's cap-and-trade prices followed a steadier upward path. The program's auction floor price—rising annually by 5% plus inflation—provided a stable floor. By Q1 2024, current vintage allowances cleared at a record $41.76, $17.72 above the $24.04 floor price and up $3.03 from November 2023's $38.73. Future vintage (2027) allowances sold at $41.00. The consistent premium above the floor indicated genuine scarcity and strong compliance demand. All 51.2 million current allowances on offer were sold, even as the supply was reduced 11% (6.4 million allowances) from the prior auction, reflecting tightening supply.

### China
China's national ETS, launched in July 2021, has maintained comparatively modest prices. By 2024, the average secondary market price was CNY 95.96 (approximately USD 13.33). Trading occurs on the Shanghai Environment and Energy Exchange with daily price fluctuation limits of 10% for listed trading and 30% for block trading. The low price environment reflects the system's design: 100% free allocation using output-based benchmarking means there is no net purchasing pressure on most compliance entities. The cap, estimated at ~5,100 MtCO₂ in 2021–2022, rose to ~8,000 MtCO₂ in 2024, reflecting expanding coverage rather than loosening stringency.

## 2. Emissions Reductions vs. Baseline Projections

### EU ETS
The EU ETS demonstrated the strongest documented emissions reductions. By 2023, emissions from covered installations were approximately 47.6% below 2005 levels, putting the system on track for its revised 2030 target of a 62% reduction (up from the original 43% target). The power sector drove the largest share of reductions, as renewable energy deployment and gas-to-coal switching accelerated. The European Green Deal revision of 2023 rebased the cap downward starting in 2024 to increase near-term ambition.

### California
California's cap-and-trade functions as a "backstop" within a suite of complementary climate policies. If programs like the state's Renewable Portfolio Standard (60% by 2030, targeting 100% by 2045) over-perform, allowance demand drops; if they underperform, the emissions cap guarantees total covered emissions remain within required limits. The program has driven year-on-year emissions reductions across nearly all covered sectors for over a decade, though the precise attribution between cap-and-trade and complementary measures remains debated. CARB's 2024 rulemaking proposes tightening the post-2025 cap to prioritize near-term reductions.

### China
China's ETS operates on an emissions intensity basis rather than an absolute cap, making direct comparison with the EU and California difficult. The system's primary near-term goal is peaking emissions before 2030 rather than achieving absolute reductions. The expansion to steel, cement, and aluminum—announced in March 2025 and adding 1,500 companies and approximately 3 billion tCO₂e in coverage—signals that the system is still in a scaling phase rather than a deep-abatement phase. Phase 1 (2024–2026) focuses on data familiarization, with Phase 2 (from 2027) shifting to intensity-based reduction targets.

## 3. Revenue Generation and Allocation

| Metric | EU ETS | California | China |
|--------|--------|------------|-------|
| **2023 Revenue** | EUR 43.6B (USD 47.2B) | ~$2.5B (est.) | Negligible (100% free allocation) |
| **2024 Revenue** | EUR 38.8B (USD 42B) | ~$1.31B (Q1 alone) | Negligible |
| **Cumulative Revenue** | EUR 184B (since 2005) | $9.3B (since 2013) | Not applicable |
| **Allocation** | National budgets, Innovation Fund, Modernisation Fund | Greenhouse Gas Reduction Fund (electrification, energy costs, resilience) | No revenue mechanism yet |

The revenue disparity is striking. The EU ETS generated more in a single year than California has in its entire program lifetime. China, by design, has generated essentially no auction revenue due to its 100% free allocation approach, though auctioning is envisioned as a future mechanism.

California's revenue supports the Greenhouse Gas Reduction Fund, which invests in electrification, household energy cost relief, and climate resilience. The EU channels funds to member-state national budgets, the Innovation Fund (supporting breakthrough clean technologies), and the Modernisation Fund (supporting lower-income member states' energy transitions).

## 4. Compliance Rates

### EU ETS
The EU ETS maintains consistently high compliance rates, exceeding 99% for allowance surrender obligations. The penalty for non-compliance—EUR 100/tonne (indexed to inflation from 2013) plus publication of the non-compliant operator's name—provides strong deterrence.

### California
California's program similarly achieves near-universal compliance, supported by the penalty structure of up to four allowances per tonne of excess emissions plus potential enforcement actions. The consistent sell-through at auctions (all 51.2 million current allowances sold in Q1 2024) indicates robust participation.

### China
China historically faced compliance challenges due to weak penalties (a maximum fine of CNY 30,000, or ~USD 4,416). This changed dramatically with the "Interim Regulations for the Management of Carbon Emissions Trading" promulgated by the State Council in January 2024 (effective May 2024), which introduced fines of 5–10 times the market value of any allowance shortfall—a massive increase in deterrence. Coal-fired plants face a maximum allowance shortfall of 20%, while gas-fired plants need only surrender allowances up to their free allocation level.

## 5. COVID-19 Response and Economic Disruption Management

### EU ETS
The pandemic caused a sharp but temporary decline in emissions and allowance demand in early 2020. The Market Stability Reserve—operational since 2019—proved its worth by automatically absorbing surplus allowances that would otherwise have depressed prices for years. The 2023 European Green Deal revision further strengthened the MSR and rebased the cap downward in 2024, effectively converting the COVID-era surplus into permanently removed allowances. No temporary allowance adjustments or banking rule modifications were needed as emergency measures.

### California
California's auction floor price—$24.04 in early 2024, rising annually—provided structural support during the pandemic downturn. Auctions continued to clear, and the program did not implement temporary allowance adjustments or banking modifications specifically in response to COVID-19. The floor price mechanism prevented the price collapses seen in earlier EU ETS phases during economic downturns.

### China
China's national ETS launched in July 2021, after the most acute phase of the pandemic had passed, so the system did not face the same COVID-19 shock test. However, the regional pilot markets that preceded the national system operated throughout the pandemic. In 2023–2024, China updated banking rules to permit entities to bank up to 10,000 tonnes plus 1.5 times their net sales, while explicitly canceling borrowing—suggesting a cautious approach to market stability as economic growth recovered unevenly.

## 6. Interaction with Complementary Policies

### EU ETS
The EU ETS interacts with the European Green Deal's broad policy architecture: the Renewable Energy Directive (targeting 42.5% renewables by 2030), the Energy Efficiency Directive, and the proposed Coal Phase-Out timelines across member states. The interplay is well-documented—the expansion of renewables and coal-to-gas switching were the primary drivers of EU ETS emissions reductions, with the carbon price accelerating these trends rather than being the sole cause. The REPowerEU plan, launched in response to the energy crisis, further accelerated renewable deployment and reduced ETS-covered emissions.

### California
California's system is explicitly designed as a backstop within a comprehensive policy framework. The state's Renewable Portfolio Standard (SB 100: 60% clean energy by 2030, 100% by 2045), the Low Carbon Fuel Standard, vehicle electrification mandates (Advanced Clean Cars II), and building efficiency standards all operate alongside cap-and-trade. The system ensures that if any complementary policy underperforms, the emissions cap still constrains total covered emissions. CARB's 2024 rulemaking proposes additional facility-level caps in overburdened communities (e.g., refinery fenceline limits) to address co-pollutant concerns alongside GHG reductions.

### China
China's ETS operates alongside a massive expansion of renewable energy deployment (China installed more solar capacity in 2023 than the entire world did in 2022), a national commitment to peak coal consumption before 2030, and eight regional pilot carbon markets that continue to operate. The national ETS is explicitly framed as a central mechanism toward China's 2030 peaking and 2060 carbon neutrality targets. The intensity-based design means that as renewable energy displaces coal generation, the benchmark ratios naturally tighten the effective cap—creating a complementary rather than conflicting relationship.

## 7. Carbon Leakage and Border Adjustment Mechanisms

### EU: Carbon Border Adjustment Mechanism (CBAM)
The EU implemented the most significant anti-leakage measure globally during this period. CBAM's transitional (data-collection) phase began October 1, 2023, and runs through December 31, 2025. During this phase, importers of iron/steel, cement, aluminum, fertilizers, hydrogen, and electricity must register in the CBAM Transitional Registry and submit quarterly reports on embedded emissions but face no financial obligations.

The definitive phase begins January 1, 2026, when importers must purchase CBAM certificates reflecting the carbon price equivalent to EU ETS allowances. Simultaneously, free allowance allocations to CBAM-covered sectors will be phased out between 2026 and 2034. This dual mechanism—charging imports for carbon while removing domestic free allowances—represents a fundamental restructuring of carbon leakage protection.

Quantitative data on actual industrial relocation during 2020–2024 remains limited. The transitional phase is still gathering data, and the definitive financial obligations have not yet begun. The design anticipates that border adjustment will replace free allocation as the primary leakage protection, but empirical evidence of effectiveness will only emerge after 2026.

### California
California addresses leakage risk through free allocation of allowances to industrial sectors and output-based updating of allocation benchmarks. Industrial entities receive free allowances based on production levels and efficiency benchmarks, effectively shielding them from the full carbon cost while maintaining abatement incentives. The state has not implemented a border adjustment mechanism, though the topic has been discussed in policy circles. Given California's position within the broader U.S. economy without a federal carbon price, leakage concerns are structurally different from the EU's.

### China
As a major net exporter of carbon-intensive goods, China's position on leakage is inverted—it is more often the destination concerned about border measures imposed by trading partners. The EU's CBAM directly affects Chinese exporters of steel, aluminum, and other covered goods. China's delegation has raised concerns about CBAM at WTO forums, arguing it functions as a trade barrier. Domestically, China's 100% free allocation to covered entities eliminates any near-term leakage concern from the ETS itself, though the low carbon price (USD 13/tonne) means the system creates minimal competitive distortion in any case.

## Uncertainty and Gaps

- **Year-by-year price data for California (2020–2023)** was not fully retrievable; the analysis relies on endpoint data (Q1 2024 record prices) and the EDF's trend reporting. Complete quarterly auction settlement prices would strengthen the trajectory analysis.
- **China's compliance rates** prior to the 2024 regulatory overhaul are not well documented in accessible English-language sources. The effectiveness of the new penalty structure (5–10x market value) remains untested.
- **Quantitative industrial relocation data** attributable specifically to carbon pricing differentials is unavailable for any of the three jurisdictions during 2020–2024. The EU's CBAM is still in its data-collection phase; California relies on free allocation rather than border measures; and China's system is too new to generate leakage signals.
- **Attribution between carbon pricing and complementary policies** remains methodologically challenging in all three jurisdictions. California explicitly acknowledges this by designing cap-and-trade as a backstop, but disentangling the specific contribution of the carbon price from renewable mandates or coal phase-outs requires econometric analysis not captured in the available sources.
- **COVID-19 era temporary adjustments** were documented for the EU (MSR absorption of surplus) but specific ad hoc interventions in California or China during 2020 were not found in the sources consulted, suggesting either they were not implemented or are not prominently documented.
