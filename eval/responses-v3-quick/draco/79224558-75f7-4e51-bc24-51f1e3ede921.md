# Quantum Computing: Progress Toward Practical Applications

## Executive Summary

Quantum computing remains in the Noisy Intermediate-Scale Quantum (NISQ) era—powerful in principle but constrained by error rates millions of times higher than classical processors. Of the three domains examined, **drug discovery and molecular simulation** is closest to delivering repeatable, commercially meaningful quantum advantage. **Optimization problems** are yielding incremental gains through hybrid quantum-classical workflows but have not yet demonstrated consistent superiority over best-in-class classical solvers. **Cryptography** represents a dual frontier: the threat to current encryption has shortened in estimated timeline but remains years away, while the migration to post-quantum cryptography is already underway as a defensive measure.

---

## The Current State of Quantum Hardware

The industry is moving faster than anticipated. A July 2024 survey by QuEra Computing of 927 respondents found that over 50% believe quantum development is outpacing their expectations from just a few years ago [1]. The quantum computing hardware market is projected to surpass **$25 billion by 2030** [2].

However, two stubborn technical barriers dominate: **scalability** (cited by 33.1% of respondents) and **error correction/fault tolerance** (30.9%) [1]. Current quantum processors operate with error rates vastly higher than classical silicon, limiting them to specialized tasks rather than standalone operation. Most survey respondents felt that meaningful quantum superiority for specific workloads would arrive in **6–10 years** [1].

---

## 1. Drug Discovery and Molecular Simulation

**Why quantum matters here:** Developing a single drug currently costs between $1–2.5 billion and takes up to 12–15 years [3]. Much of this cost stems from the inability of classical computers to accurately simulate molecular interactions at the quantum mechanical level. Quantum computers can natively represent and simulate molecular systems in ways classical machines cannot, making this domain a leading candidate for early practical advantage.

**Current progress:**
- Key algorithms like the **Variational Quantum Eigensolver (VQE)** and **Quantum Approximate Optimization Algorithm (QAOA)** are frequently used to calculate ground-state energies of molecules, enabling predictions of how drug candidates will bind to biological targets [3].
- In **April 2025**, IonQ and Ansys ran a medical device simulation on IonQ's 36-qubit processor that **outperformed classical high-performance computing by 12%**—a small but notable demonstration of quantum hardware delivering real-world results [5].
- Major pharmaceutical companies—**Pfizer, Roche, AstraZeneca, Merck, and Novartis**—are collaborating directly with quantum hardware providers including IBM, Google Quantum AI, IonQ, and Quantinuum [3].
- Quantum methods have the potential to **reduce early-stage research timelines by as much as 50%**, dramatically lowering capital requirements [3].

**Outlook:** Researchers expect chemistry and materials science to be among the first areas where quantum devices deliver repeatable advantage [4]. The industry is relying heavily on hybrid quantum-classical workflows and AI integration to optimize molecular screening within current hardware limitations [3].

## 2. Cryptography and Security

**The threat:** Traditional encryption methods, such as RSA, could be compromised by quantum algorithms like Shor's algorithm [2]. The critical question has always been: how far away is that capability?

**Current estimates:** Factoring a 2048-bit RSA modulus would require on the order of **tens of millions of physical qubits** under realistic error correction assumptions, far beyond today's hardware [4]. However, recent research between May 2025 and March 2026 reduced that estimate to **under 1 million physical qubits**, significantly shortening the threat timeline due to improved architectures and algorithms [5].

**The response:**
- **Post-quantum cryptography (PQC)** migration is already underway. Today's devices are far from the required scale due to the immense overhead of error correction, and "from an engineering and risk perspective, the key action is not to wait for quantum, but to prepare for it" [4].
- End-users in the quantum industry appear more concerned with **cryptography and security** than respondents in other segments, likely preparing for future encryption challenges [1].

**Outlook:** The cryptographic threat is real but not immediate. Today's quantum hardware is orders of magnitude away from the scale needed to break RSA-2048. The practical urgency lies in the **"harvest now, decrypt later"** attack model—where adversaries capture encrypted data today to decrypt it once quantum computers mature—which is why the migration to quantum-resistant encryption is a present-day priority, not a future one.

## 3. Optimization Problems

**Why quantum matters here:** Optimization—finding the best solution among exponentially many possibilities—underpins supply chain logistics, financial portfolio management, vehicle routing, scheduling, and more. Quantum algorithms can, for example, determine the most efficient routing for delivery trucks considering variables like traffic patterns and fuel consumption [2].

**Current progress:**
- Real-world benchmarks consistently show that **well-tuned classical solvers remain very competitive**. Quantum approaches sometimes outperform naive baselines but "rarely dominate best-in-class classical methods consistently" [4].
- However, **hybrid quantum-classical frameworks** are producing measurable improvements. A March 2026 study reported **12–18% cost reductions** and **20–35% faster convergence** on supply chain optimization benchmarks [5].
- Quantum algorithms are being applied to financial portfolio optimization, delivery routing, and complex logistics planning [2].

**Outlook:** Pure quantum advantage in optimization remains unproven. The near-term approach embeds quantum processors within hybrid workflows to handle specific computational bottlenecks, rather than replacing classical systems entirely [5]. This pragmatic strategy could deliver incremental but meaningful business value within a few years, even before full fault-tolerant quantum computing arrives.

## Comparative Summary

| Domain | Proximity to Practical Advantage | Current Status | Key Bottleneck |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Drug Discovery** | **Closest** | Demonstrated 12% improvement over classical HPC in specific simulations; major pharma partnerships active | Qubit count and error correction for larger molecules |
| **Optimization** | **Mid-range** | Hybrid frameworks showing 12–18% cost reductions in benchmarks; pure quantum advantage unproven | Consistency—classical solvers still match or beat quantum on most problems |
| **Cryptography** | **Farthest for threat**, **Immediate for defense** | RSA-breaking resource estimates reduced from 20M to <1M qubits; PQC migration underway | Fault-tolerant qubits at scale; the defensive response is already happening |

## Uncertainty & Gaps

- **Timeline estimates vary widely** across sources and respondent surveys. The 6–10 year estimate comes from a survey of industry participants [1], but individual predictions range more broadly. The true timeline depends heavily on breakthroughs in error correction that are inherently difficult to forecast.
- **The March 2026 benchmark data** from The Quantum Insider may reflect forward-looking or preliminary research; independent replication of the 12–18% optimization improvements was not found in other sources.
- **The RSA resource reduction** (from 20 million to under 1 million physical qubits) is based on recent architectural improvements but still represents a theoretical lower bound—actual attack capability would require additional overhead.
- **Drug discovery claims** of "50% timeline reduction" are potential/estimated impact rather than demonstrated results from deployed systems. Current quantum simulations remain limited to small molecules.
