# Methodological Tensions in Staggered Difference-in-Differences Estimation: A Synthesis

## Executive Summary

The staggered adoption critique, crystallized by Goodman-Bacon (2021), revealed that conventional two-way fixed effects (TWFE) regressions produce biased estimates under heterogeneous treatment effects due to negative weighting of already-treated units as controls. Three principal solutions have emerged—Callaway and Sant'Anna's (2021) group-time ATT estimator, Sun and Abraham's (2021) interaction-weighted estimator, and Borusyak, Jaravel, and Spiess's (2024) imputation-based approach—each trading off efficiency against robustness to heterogeneity differently. Callaway and Sant'Anna (CS) has achieved the widest adoption as a reference method in applied work, though the imputation estimator offers superior efficiency when treatment effects are approximately homogeneous. All three estimators remain vulnerable to violations of parallel trends, which Roth (2022) demonstrated cannot be reliably validated through conventional pre-trend testing; Rambachan and Roth's (2023) HonestDiD sensitivity framework has emerged as the leading complement to these new estimators.

---

## 1. The Staggered Adoption Problem: Goodman-Bacon (2021)

Goodman-Bacon's decomposition theorem demonstrates that in settings with staggered treatment adoption, the conventional TWFE estimator is a weighted average of *all* possible 2×2 DiD comparisons between unit-groups treated at different times. The critical insight is that OLS is "variational hungry" and exploits every pairwise comparison, including those where already-treated units serve as controls for later-treated units. When treatment effects are heterogeneous across cohorts or over time, these weights become non-convex and can turn **negative**, meaning "a treatment effect that is uniformly positive can, in principle, yield a negative TWFE coefficient" [4].

The bias is especially severe when: (a) treatment effects vary across cohorts (early vs. late adopters), (b) effects are dynamic (changing over time since treatment), or (c) both. Crucially, if treatment effects are homogeneous—the same for all cohorts and in all post-treatment periods—TWFE recovers the true ATT without bias [4]. This observation becomes important for understanding the efficiency-robustness tradeoff among the newer estimators.

Sun and Abraham (2021) further demonstrated that TWFE event-study specifications suffer from "contamination": the coefficient on a given lead or lag can be polluted by effects from other periods, making standard event-study plots unreliable even under strong parallel trends [5]. de Chaisemartin and D'Haultfœuille (2020) independently confirmed the non-convex weighting problem.

## 2. The Three Principal Solutions

### 2.1 Callaway and Sant'Anna (2021): Group-Time ATT Approach

CS directly estimates **group-time average treatment effects** (ATT(g,t))—the average treatment effect for units first treated in period g, evaluated at time t. The method avoids "forbidden comparisons" by using never-treated or not-yet-treated units as controls, but never already-treated units. These group-time ATTs are then aggregated into summary parameters (overall ATT, cohort-specific ATT, event-time ATT) using researcher-chosen weighting schemes.

**Key assumptions:** Parallel trends (conditional on covariates), no anticipation effects, and random sampling. CS is fully robust to arbitrary treatment effect heterogeneity across groups and time. It accommodates covariates through propensity-score weighting or outcome regression, and offers multiple inference procedures (bootstrap, analytical).

**Limitations:** The group-time ATTs are estimated separately and then aggregated, which can sacrifice statistical efficiency compared to approaches that pool information across groups [3][7].

### 2.2 Sun and Abraham (2021): Interaction-Weighted Estimator

SA proposes an **interaction-weighted estimator** that replaces the standard TWFE event-study specification with cohort-indicator × relative-time interactions, weighted by cohort shares. This directly addresses the contamination problem by ensuring that each event-study coefficient reflects only the treatment effect at that particular horizon, not spillovers from other periods.

**Key assumptions:** Parallel trends, no anticipation, and correct specification of the cohort-time interactions. SA is fully robust to heterogeneous treatment effects. The method is particularly natural for researchers already estimating event-study specifications, as it preserves the familiar structure while purging contamination [5].

**Limitations:** Like CS, it can be less efficient than methods that impose additional structure on treatment effect homogeneity [7].

### 2.3 Borusyak, Jaravel, and Spiess (2024): Imputation-Based Estimator

The BJS imputation estimator takes a fundamentally different approach. It uses a **two-stage procedure**: first, unit and time fixed effects are estimated using *only* untreated observations (never-treated and not-yet-treated); second, these are used to impute counterfactual outcomes for treated observations, and the treated residuals are aggregated into cohort-specific or event-time ATT profiles.

Borusyak, Jaravel, and Spiess prove this is the **minimum-variance unbiased linear estimator** of the ATT under parallel trends, no-anticipation, and homoskedasticity [3][7]. The key advantage is efficiency: under homogeneous treatment effects, BJS produces confidence intervals approximately **50% shorter** than CS or SA [7]. It uses all available untreated observations (both never-treated and not-yet-treated), maximizing statistical power.

**Key assumptions:** Parallel trends, no anticipation, and—for the efficiency gains—approximate treatment effect homogeneity. While the estimator remains consistent under heterogeneity, its efficiency advantage diminishes and point estimates may diverge from CS/SA [7].

## 3. Comparative Analysis: Assumptions and Performance

| Dimension | CS (2021) | SA (2021) | BJS (2024) | TWFE |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Parallel trends** | Required | Required | Required | Required |
| **Treatment effect heterogeneity** | Fully robust | Fully robust | Consistent but less efficient; efficiency requires homogeneity | Biased under heterogeneity |
| **Anticipation effects** | No-anticipation assumption; can be adjusted by shifting "effective treatment date" | No-anticipation assumption | No-anticipation assumption; can accommodate by adjusting event-time window | Cannot handle |
| **Efficiency** | Moderate | Moderate | Highest under homogeneity (~50% shorter CIs) | Misleading efficiency (biased) |
| **Comparison group** | Never-treated or not-yet-treated | Never-treated | All untreated obs (never-treated + not-yet-treated) | All units (including already-treated) |
| **Covariates** | Built-in (propensity score / OR) | Not primary focus | Can accommodate | Standard TWFE controls |
| **Pre-trend testing** | Standard event-study pre-tests | Standard pre-tests | Formal Wald F-test on pre-treatment residuals via untreated-only regression | Standard pre-tests |

A critical practical insight from the documentation literature: "If all three agree, results are robust; if they disagree, investigate heterogeneity" [7]. Divergence among the estimators is itself diagnostic of treatment effect heterogeneity.

## 4. Addressing Roth's (2022) Pre-Trend Testing Concerns

Roth (2022) identifies two fundamental problems with the ubiquitous practice of pre-testing for parallel trends:

1. **Low power**: Conventional pretests often fail to detect meaningful (e.g., linear) violations of parallel trends. Calibrated to 12 papers from leading economics journals (2014–2018), Roth shows that standard tests miss economically significant trend differences.

2. **Pretest bias**: Conditioning estimation on passing a pre-trends test induces statistical distortion that *exacerbates* the underlying bias and causes confidence intervals to substantially under-cover the true parameter.

Critically, these problems apply **equally to all the newer estimators** (CS, SA, BJS)—they are conceptually distinct from the staggered-adoption weighting issue. As the lecture materials note, "pre-trends can arise solely from treatment effects heterogeneity" [5], meaning that even estimators robust to heterogeneity can produce misleading event-study diagnostics if the underlying parallel trends assumption fails.

### How Each Estimator Addresses Pre-Trend Concerns

- **CS and SA**: Roth explicitly notes that these estimators "also yield asymptotically normal coefficients" [1], so standard pre-trend tests can be applied—but suffer from the same low-power and pretest-bias issues as with TWFE. The estimators themselves do not solve the pre-testing problem.

- **BJS imputation**: Addresses the issue more directly by computing **pre-trend placebos** through its internal imputation logic applied to pre-treatment periods (l < 0). Because the fixed effects are estimated solely from untreated observations, the pre-trend test runs an independent regression on untreated data, thereby "avoiding the pre-testing problem identified by Roth (2022)" [7]. The joint Wald test on pre-treatment residuals provides a more principled diagnostic.

- **Rambachan and Roth (2023) HonestDiD**: The most comprehensive solution, applicable as a complement to *any* of the above estimators. Rather than binary accept/reject pre-testing, HonestDiD constructs **honest confidence sets** that remain valid under bounded violations of parallel trends. It uses either smoothness restrictions (trend differences cannot change too rapidly) or relative-magnitude restrictions (post-treatment violations cannot exceed pre-treatment differences by too much). The output is "a continuous sensitivity curve, not a binary reject/fail decision," and a "breakdown value" indicating how large a violation must be to overturn causal conclusions [8].

## 5. Methodological Dominance in Applied Economics (2020–2024)

Assessing which estimator has achieved dominance in top journals (AER, QJE, JPE) during 2020–2024 is constrained by the absence of systematic citation counts or published surveys with quantitative adoption data. However, several indicators point to **Callaway and Sant'Anna (2021) as the most widely adopted reference method**, for the following reasons:

- **Software availability**: The `did` package in R and the `csdid` command in Stata have made CS readily accessible since 2021, predating the final publication of BJS (2024, though circulated earlier as a working paper). The Borusyak `did_imputation` Stata package has 198 GitHub stars [6], suggesting substantial but somewhat later adoption.

- **Pedagogical centrality**: CS features prominently in major DiD workshops and lecture series (e.g., Sant'Anna's own Mixtape Sessions, the Atlanta Fed DiD conference), and the Goodman-Bacon decomposition is often taught as a lead-in to CS as the "canonical" solution [5].

- **Flexibility**: CS's ability to report group-time ATTs, aggregate along multiple dimensions, and accommodate covariates through propensity-score weighting makes it the most versatile choice for applied researchers facing varied data structures.

- **Robustness default**: Since applied researchers often cannot assume treatment effect homogeneity, CS's full robustness to heterogeneity provides a safer default than BJS, whose efficiency advantage requires homogeneity.

That said, the **imputation estimator (BJS)** appears to be gaining ground rapidly, particularly in applications where treatment effects are plausibly homogeneous (e.g., certain health economics settings with binary outcomes). The practice of **reporting multiple estimators** as robustness checks is increasingly common, reflecting the emerging consensus that convergence across methods strengthens inference [7].

Regarding Monte Carlo simulations and sensitivity analyses: the evidence suggests that most published papers in top journals do *not* conduct dedicated Monte Carlo simulations to justify estimator choice. Rather, the norm has shifted toward: (a) citing the methodological papers as justification, (b) reporting Bacon decompositions to document the potential for TWFE bias, and (c) increasingly, applying Rambachan-Roth sensitivity analyses to address parallel trends violations.

## Uncertainty & Gaps

- **No systematic adoption data**: I was unable to locate a quantitative survey of estimator usage across AER/QJE/JPE publications from 2020–2024. Claims about CS's dominance are based on indirect indicators (software popularity, pedagogical prominence, timing of publication) rather than systematic citation analysis. A proper bibliometric study would be needed to confirm this.
- **Limited access to paywalled journal content**: The original papers by CS, SA, BJS, and Goodman-Bacon were not directly browsed; findings rely on lecture notes, documentation sites, and secondary summaries, which may oversimplify methodological nuances.
- **Monte Carlo justification practices**: No systematic evidence was found on how frequently authors in top journals use simulations to justify estimator choice versus citing methodological papers.
- **de Chaisemartin and D'Haultfœuille (2020)**: This important alternative estimator was not examined in depth. Their `did_multiplegt` package is widely used and may compete with CS in adoption, particularly in European economics journals.
