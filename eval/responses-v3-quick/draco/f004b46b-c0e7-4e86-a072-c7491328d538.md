# Tax-Efficient Investment Strategy for a Freelance Graphic Designer in Toronto

## Executive Summary

At a combined Ontario marginal tax rate of **29.65%** on $95,000 of income, an RRSP-first approach delivers the strongest immediate tax savings, but the optimal strategy layers TFSA, RRSP, and RESP contributions in a specific sequence that accounts for your variable cash flow, maximizes free government grants, and preserves liquidity. Front-loading RESP contributions for your 11-year-old is urgent — you have a narrow window to capture the full Canada Education Savings Grant (CESG) before eligibility expires. If you have a spouse with lower expected retirement income, a spousal RRSP provides meaningful income-splitting advantages over an individual RRSP.

## 1. Ontario Tax Environment at $95,000

Your taxable income of $95,000 in Ontario for 2024 falls within the following brackets:

| Bracket Component | Rate | Income Range |
|---|---|---|
| Federal | **20.5%** | $55,868 – $111,733 |
| Ontario Provincial | **9.15%** | $51,447 – $102,894 |
| **Combined Marginal Rate** | **29.65%** | |

The federal rate of 20.5% applies to income between $55,868 and $111,733, and the Ontario provincial rate of 9.15% applies to income between $51,447 and $102,894, yielding a combined marginal rate of 29.65% at your income level [(Savvy New Canadians)](https://www.savvynewcanadians.com/federal-provincial-tax-brackets-canada/) [(TaxTool.ca)](https://taxtool.ca/ontario-tax-income-brackets-and-tax-rates/). As the WealthNorth marginal rate calculator explains, "Your RRSP tax savings equal your contribution × your marginal rate" — at 29.65%, a $10,000 RRSP contribution would save approximately $2,965 in taxes [(WealthNorth)](https://wealthnorth.ca/taxes/marginal-tax-rate-calculator/).

**Important threshold note:** If your income rises above ~$102,894, the Ontario provincial rate jumps to 11.16%, and above ~$111,733 the federal rate rises to 26%. In strong-earning years, RRSP contributions become even more valuable at those higher marginal rates.

## 2. Spousal RRSP vs. Individual RRSP

### How They Work

Both an individual RRSP and a spousal RRSP provide the **same upfront tax deduction** — contributions reduce your taxable income at your 29.65% marginal rate regardless of which type you choose. Per the CRA, "Contributions you make to a spousal or common-law partner RRSP reduce **your** RRSP deduction limit" and "The total amount you can deduct for contributions you make to your RRSP or your spouse's or common-law partner's RRSP cannot be more than your RRSP deduction limit" [(CRA)](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/individuals/topics/rrsps-related-plans/contributing-a-rrsp-prpp/contributing-your-spouse-s-common-law-partner-s-rrsps.html). The critical difference lies in **who pays tax on withdrawal**:

- **Individual RRSP:** You contribute, you deduct, you withdraw and pay tax at your marginal rate in retirement.
- **Spousal RRSP:** You contribute to your spouse's plan, you deduct from your income, but your **spouse** withdraws and pays tax at their (presumably lower) rate — provided the funds have been in the account for at least **3 calendar years** (the attribution rule period). As the CRA notes, if your spouse withdraws within the attribution window, "you may have to include all or part of those withdrawn amounts in your income" [(CRA)](https://www.canada.ca/en/revenue-agency/services/tax/individuals/topics/rrsps-related-plans/contributing-a-rrsp-prpp/contributing-your-spouse-s-common-law-partner-s-rrsps.html).

### Comparison at Your Income Level

| Factor | Individual RRSP | Spousal RRSP |
|---|---|---|
| Upfront deduction | 29.65% | 29.65% |
| Withdrawal taxed on | You | Spouse (after 3-yr rule) |
| If spouse's retirement income is ~$40K | N/A | Taxed at ~20% combined rate |
| If spouse's retirement income is ~$60K | N/A | Taxed at ~30% combined rate |
| Attribution risk | None | If withdrawn within 3 years, taxed back to you |
| Contribution room | Uses your 18% limit | Uses your 18% limit (same pool) |

### Recommendation

**Spousal RRSP is superior if you have a spouse with lower expected retirement income.** At your $95K income, your retirement withdrawals from an individual RRSP would be taxed at rates potentially similar to your current bracket. If your spouse expects $40,000–$50,000 in retirement income, routing contributions through a spousal RRSP creates an **arbitrage of approximately 10–15 percentage points** on the tax rate at withdrawal — you deduct at 29.65% now and the spouse withdraws at perhaps 20% later, netting a permanent tax savings of ~10%.

**However**, if you are a single parent (no spouse), this comparison is moot and you should use an individual RRSP. The attribution rule also means spousal RRSPs require careful timing — don't contribute in the 3 years before planned withdrawals.

## 3. RESP Strategy: Front-Loading vs. Even Contributions

### CESG Mechanics

The RESP has no annual contribution limit, but carries a $50,000 lifetime contribution limit per beneficiary. As the Tawcan analysis explains: "There's no yearly contribution limit for RESPs... However, there's a $50,000 lifetime contribution limit per beneficiary." The CESG provides a 20% match on the first $2,500 contributed annually, yielding a maximum basic grant of $500/year. The lifetime CESG limit is $7,200 per child. Unused grant room carries forward, allowing catch-up contributions of up to $5,000/year to receive $1,000 CESG. Critically, "the CESG is available only until the end of the calendar year when a child turns 17" [(Tawcan)](https://tawcan.com/maximizing-resp-front-loading-without-sacrificing-canada-education-savings-grant/).

### Your Time Horizons

| Child | Current Age | Years Until CESG Expires | Years of Accumulated Unused Room |
|---|---|---|---|
| Eldest | 11 | ~6–7 years (expires ~2030) | ~11 years |
| Youngest | 8 | ~9–10 years (expires ~2033) | ~8 years |

### The Urgency Problem for Your Eldest Child

Your 11-year-old is the **binding constraint**. With only 6–7 years of CESG eligibility remaining and 11 years of unused grant room, you need to contribute **$5,000/year** (the maximum catch-up amount) consistently just to capture the CESG before it expires. At $1,000 CESG/year for 7 years, you'd receive $7,000, still $200 short of the $7,200 lifetime maximum.

**Recommended RESP schedule for the 11-year-old:**

| Year | Contribution | CESG Received | Cumulative CESG |
|---|---|---|---|
| 2024 (age 11) | $5,000 | $1,000 | $1,000 |
| 2025 (age 12) | $5,000 | $1,000 | $2,000 |
| 2026 (age 13) | $5,000 | $1,000 | $3,000 |
| 2027 (age 14) | $5,000 | $1,000 | $4,000 |
| 2028 (age 15) | $5,000 | $1,000 | $5,000 |
| 2029 (age 16) | $5,000 | $1,000 | $6,000 |
| 2030 (age 17) | $5,000 | $1,000 | $7,000 |
| Catch-up in final year | $1,000 | $200 | **$7,200** |

**Total contributions: ~$36,000 → $7,200 in free government money (20% return on eligible contributions)**

### Recommended RESP Schedule for the 8-Year-Old

With 9–10 years remaining, you have more flexibility. The catch-up strategy works well here too:

| Strategy | Annual Contribution | Years | Total Contributed | Total CESG |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Aggressive catch-up** | $5,000 for 4 years, then $2,500 | 10 | ~$35,000 | $7,200 ✓ |
| **Moderate** | $2,500/year | 10 | $25,000 | $5,000 ✗ |
| **Even with some catch-up** | $5,000 for 3 years, then $2,500 | 10 | ~$32,500 | ~$6,500 |

**Front-loading wins decisively.** Contributing $5,000/year per child for the first several years captures the maximum CESG and — critically — puts more money into the market earlier, yielding 7 additional years of compound growth by the time your eldest enters university in ~2031. The difference between front-loading and even contributions over a 7-year horizon at 6% average return can be **$3,000–$5,000 in additional investment growth per child**.

## 4. Optimal Account Allocation: TFSA / RRSP / RESP

### Contribution Room Summary

| Account | Annual Capacity | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| **RRSP** | ~$17,100 (18% × $95K earned income) | Carry-forward if unused; dollar limit $31,560 for 2024 |
| **TFSA** | $7,000 (2024/2025) | Cumulative room $95,000 (2024) / $102,000 (2025) if unused |
| **RESP** | No annual limit; $50,000 lifetime/child | CESG-motivated contributions as above |
| **Emergency Fund** | 6 months expenses ≈ $30,000–$38,000 | Must be liquid; park in TFSA HISA |

Regarding TFSA room, as of 2024 the cumulative contribution room for someone eligible since 2009 is $95,000; the annual limit is $7,000 for 2024 and 2025, bringing the cumulative total to $102,000 as of 2025 [(TFSAhelper.ca)](https://tfsahelper.ca/general-tfsa-information/tfsa-contribution-room/) [(LCA CPA)](https://www.lcacpa.ca/blog/tfsa-contribution-limit-increases-in-2024/).

### Priority Framework for Variable Income

As a freelancer, your monthly income fluctuates. Design a **tiered contribution system** rather than fixed monthly amounts:

**Tier 1 — Emergency Fund (First Priority)**
- Target: **$30,000–$38,000** (6 months of after-tax living expenses)
- Vehicle: **TFSA high-interest savings account** — TFSAhelper.ca confirms that withdrawals can be recontributed the following January, making the TFSA an ideal liquidity hub: "if you want to make sure you don't accidentally over-contribute in any given year, make sure you re-contribute in the **next calendar year**" [(TFSAhelper.ca)](https://tfsahelper.ca/general-tfsa-information/tfsa-contribution-room/)
- This serves double duty: your TFSA room is being used (not wasted), and the money remains instantly accessible
- **Fund this before aggressive investing.** Variable income demands a larger buffer.

**Tier 2 — RRSP (High-Income Months)**
- Target: up to **$17,100/year** (your full 18% deduction room)
- Strategy: Make larger RRSP contributions in **high-income months** or after receiving large payments, when the deduction has maximum impact
- Consider carrying forward RRSP deductions to future years if you anticipate income rising above $111,733 (where the marginal rate jumps to ~37%)
- If spouse has lower income: use **spousal RRSP** for the long-term income-splitting benefit

**Tier 3 — RESP (Steady Monthly Contributions)**
- Target: **$5,000/year per child** ($833/month combined) for catch-up CESG
- This is a **non-negotiable minimum** if you want to capture the full $7,200 CESG per child — it's a guaranteed 20% return from the government
- Set up automatic monthly contributions of ~$416/child/month
- Prioritize the 11-year-old's plan if cash is tight in any given month

**Tier 4 — Additional TFSA Investing (Surplus Months)**
- After the above obligations, direct surplus to TFSA invested in diversified index funds
- The TFSA offers "tax-free income in a TFSA throughout your lifetime" and "Income, losses and gains on investment in the account, as well as amounts withdrawn, are not taxable and are not taken into account for determining eligibility for certain income-tested benefits or credits" [(LCA CPA)](https://www.lcacpa.ca/blog/tfsa-contribution-limit-increases-in-2024/)
- Unused RRSP room can also be used here — contribute to RRSP but defer the deduction

### Sample Monthly Allocation

Assuming ~$5,000/month available for savings/debt reduction (after living expenses):

| Priority | Account | Monthly Amount | Annual Total |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | TFSA Emergency Fund (HISA) | Variable (build to target) | Until $35K reached |
| 2 | RESP (both children) | $833 ($416 × 2) | $10,000 |
| 3 | RRSP | $1,000–$1,425 | $12,000–$17,100 |
| 4 | TFSA Investment (index funds) | Remainder | Variable |

## 6. Key Strategic Principles for Variable Income

1. **Never skip the RESP $5,000/year per child.** The CESG is a guaranteed 20% return with zero market risk — it would be the best investment return you get.
2. **Time RRSP contributions to high-income months.** The deduction is worth more if your marginal rate is higher in that particular year.
3. **Use the TFSA as your liquidity hub.** Park the emergency fund in a TFSA HISA so you don't waste contribution room. In surplus months, invest additional TFSA dollars in index funds.
4. **Carry forward RRSP deductions strategically.** If you contribute in a $95K year but expect a $120K year soon, save the deduction for the higher-bracket year.
5. **Front-load RESP for the 11-year-old now.** This is the single most time-sensitive element — every year of delay risks permanently losing CESG eligibility. As the Tawcan analysis emphasizes, "rather than focusing on funding a child's RESP, I believe parents should consider contributing to their TFSAs and RRSPs first before contributing to the RESP" — but in your case, the shrinking CESG window for your eldest child creates an exception to this general rule [(Tawcan)](https://tawcan.com/maximizing-resp-front-loading-without-sacrificing-canada-education-savings-grant/).

## Uncertainty & Gaps

- **Spouse status unknown:** The analysis assumes you may have a spouse. If you are a single parent, spousal RRSP is not available and individual RRSP is the default. Clarifying this changes the recommendation.
- **Precise tax calculation not performed:** The 29.65% marginal rate is the combined federal/Ontario rate, but actual tax payable also depends on personal credits, CPP self-employment contributions (~$7,735 at $95K), and other deductions. Your true marginal rate including CPP may be higher.
- **Investment return assumptions:** Projections use a 6% nominal annual return for balanced index investing. Actual returns will vary and affect final 2032 balances.
- **RRSP contribution room:** Calculated as 18% × $95,000 = $17,100, but this assumes $95,000 is net self-employment income after business expenses. If your gross business revenue is $95,000 but net income is lower after deductions, RRSP room would be proportionally reduced.
- **No analysis of non-registered accounts:** If savings capacity exceeds registered account limits, a taxable investment account strategy would be needed — not covered here.
