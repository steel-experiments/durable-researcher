# Methodological Tensions in Staggered Difference-in-Differences Estimation: A Synthetic Analysis

## Executive Summary

The staggered adoption critique initiated by Goodman-Bacon (2021) revealed that conventional two-way fixed effects (TWFE) regression produces biased estimates under heterogeneous treatment effects and varied treatment timing. Three leading alternatives—Callaway and Sant'Anna's group-time estimator, Sun and Abraham's interaction-weighted estimator, and Borusyak, Jaravel, and Spiess's imputation approach—offer conceptually distinct solutions, though they converge under certain conditions. Callaway and Sant'Anna's method has achieved the widest adoption in applied work, but the imputation approach has gained momentum due to its computational elegance. All three partially address Roth's (2022) concerns about pre-trend testing but do not fully resolve the fundamental identification challenges he raises.

## Detailed Findings

### 1. The Staggered Adoption Problem: Goodman-Bacon's Decomposition

Goodman-Bacon (2021) demonstrated that with staggered treatment timing, the standard TWFE estimator decomposes into a weighted average of all possible 2×2 DiD comparisons between unit-groups treated at different times. The critical insight is that some of these comparisons use *already-treated* units as controls. When treatment effects are heterogeneous—varying across groups or over time—these "forbidden comparisons" assign negative weights to certain treatment effects, potentially producing estimates with the wrong sign even when all individual treatment effects are positive.

This decomposition has three implications: (1) TWFE estimates are not convex combinations of group-specific treatment effects, (2) the bias depends on the heterogeneity of treatment effects across adoption cohorts and over time, and (3) the problem is not a specification error but a mechanical property of TWFE with variation in treatment timing. Under homogeneous treatment effects, TWFE remains unbiased—but this assumption is rarely defensible in applied settings.

### 2. The Three Leading Solutions: Mechanisms and Assumptions

#### 2.1 Callaway and Sant'Anna (2021): Group-Time ATT Approach

Callaway and Sant'Anna (CS) propose estimating the average treatment effect for the treated (ATT) separately for each cohort (group) and time period, producing a matrix of group-time ATT(g,t) estimates. These are then aggregated into summary parameters (e.g., event-study or calendar-time effects) using researcher-chosen weights.

**Key features:**
- **Two-stage structure**: First stage estimates propensity scores (for selection into treatment timing); second stage computes doubly-robust group-time ATT estimates.
- **Control group choice**: Uses either "never-treated" units or "not-yet-treated" units as valid controls, with the latter increasing sample size but requiring stronger assumptions.
- **Parallel trends**: Requires parallel trends only between each treated group and its specific control group in periods before treatment. No global homogeneity assumption.
- **Anticipation effects**: Can be accommodated by adjusting the "effective" treatment date forward, but must be specified ex ante.
- **Heterogeneous effects**: Fully accommodates heterogeneity across groups and over time; the disaggregated ATT(g,t) estimates are the building blocks.
- **Implementation**: The `did` package in R (and later `did` in Stata) contributed substantially to adoption.

#### 2.2 Sun and Abraham (2021): Interaction-Weighted Estimator

Sun and Abraham (SA) focus on the event-study specification and show that TWFE event-study coefficients (leads and lags) are contaminated by treatment effects from other relative time periods when treatment effects are heterogeneous across cohorts. Their solution is an interaction-weighted estimator.

**Key features:**
- **Mechanism**: Estimates cohort-specific treatment effects interacted with relative time indicators (cohort × relative time interactions), then aggregates using the distribution of cohorts as weights.
- **Clean controls**: Like CS, uses only "clean" control units—never-treated or not-yet-treated.
- **Parallel trends**: Requires parallel trends across cohorts in the absence of treatment, similar to CS.
- **Anticipation**: Can be handled but must be assumed ex ante; the estimator does not inherently test for it.
- **Heterogeneous effects**: Designed specifically to handle heterogeneity across adoption cohorts in dynamic treatment effects.

The SA estimator is closely related to CS—both compute cohort-specific effects and aggregate. The primary distinction is parametric: SA's interaction-weighted approach can be implemented via a modified regression, while CS uses a semi-parametric doubly-robust framework.

#### 2.3 Borusyak, Jaravel, and Spiess (2024): Imputation Approach

Borusyak, Jaravel, and Spiess (BJS) propose an imputation-based method that separates the problem into two stages: (1) estimate the unit and time fixed effects using only untreated observations, then (2) impute counterfactual outcomes for treated observations and compute treatment effects by differencing actual from imputed values.

**Key features:**
- **Imputation logic**: Uses only pre-treatment and never-treated observations to estimate the fixed effects model, ensuring untreated data exclusively inform the counterfactual.
- **Efficiency**: Under homoskedasticity, the imputation estimator achieves the Gauss-Markov efficiency bound; it is asymptotically equivalent to CS under certain conditions.
- **Parallel trends**: Requires the standard parallel trends assumption embedded in the TWFE model, but estimated only from untreated data.
- **Anticipation**: Can be handled by excluding anticipated periods from the first-stage estimation (treating them as effectively treated).
- **Heterogeneous effects**: Accommodates arbitrary heterogeneity because treatment effects are computed as residuals after imputing counterfactuals—no parametric assumption on the treatment effect structure.
- **Implementation**: The `didimputation` package in Stata (and later R) facilitated adoption.

#### 2.4 Comparison of Assumptions

| Dimension | CS (2021) | SA (2021) | BJS (2024) |
|-----------|-----------|-----------|-------------|
| **Parallel trends** | Group-specific; between treated and clean controls | Cohort-specific; across adoption groups | Population-level; estimated from untreated observations |
| **Treatment effect heterogeneity** | Fully flexible; group-time ATT | Fully flexible; cohort × relative time | Fully flexible; computed as residuals |
| **Anticipation** | Handled by redefining treatment date | Handled by redefining treatment date | Handled by excluding anticipation periods from first stage |
| **Efficiency** | Doubly robust; efficient under correct propensity score model | OLS-based efficiency | Gauss-Markov efficient under homoskedasticity |
| **Key advantage** | Robustness via double robustness; flexibility in aggregation | Simple regression-based implementation | Clean separation of estimation and aggregation; computational simplicity |

Under the same assumptions and large samples, these three estimators produce numerically similar results. The methodological literature (including Roth et al., 2023, in their *QJE* synthesis) notes that the primary differences arise in finite samples, in how covariates are incorporated, and in the treatment of never-treated versus not-yet-treated units as controls.

### 3. Adoption Patterns in Top Economics Journals (2020–2024)

Based on the broader literature's assessment and what can be observed in published work:

**Callaway and Sant'Anna's method** appears to have achieved the widest adoption among the three alternatives. The `did` package in R has been a major driver, and the method's doubly-robust framework appeals to researchers concerned about model misspecification. Published papers in labor and health economics using CS include studies of minimum wage effects, health insurance expansions, and environmental regulations. The CS approach has been cited as a primary method or robustness check in numerous *AER* and *QJE* publications from 2021 onward.

**Borusyak et al.'s imputation approach** has gained rapid adoption since its working paper circulation began in 2021, particularly in finance and industrial organization, though it has substantial presence in labor economics as well. Its computational simplicity—requiring only OLS in two stages—makes it attractive.

**Sun and Abraham's interaction-weighted estimator** appears less frequently as a primary method but is commonly used as a robustness check. The `eventstudyinteract` Stata package has facilitated its use.

**Justification practices**: Many published papers in top-5 journals now include robustness checks using multiple estimators (e.g., reporting CS, BJS, and SA estimates alongside TWFE), rather than relying on Monte Carlo simulations. This reflects a broader normative shift: the credibility comes from demonstrating insensitivity to estimator choice rather than proving one estimator's superiority via simulation. Baker, Larcker, and Wang (2022, *JFE*) documented the prevalence of TWFE in published papers and the slow adoption of corrected estimators, finding that many published TWFE estimates could be substantively biased.

**Important caveat**: I was unable to systematically browse the full text of *AER*, *QJE*, or *JPE* articles to quantify precise adoption rates. My assessment of relative adoption frequency is based on the broader discussion in the methods literature and references in survey papers, which consistently identify CS as the most widely used, followed by BJS.

### 4. Addressing Roth's (2022) Pre-Trend Testing Concerns

Roth (2022) identifies a fundamental problem with the standard practice of "pre-testing" for parallel trends by examining event-study lead coefficients. He shows that:

1. **Pretest bias**: Conditioning the analysis on passing a pre-trend test (insignificant leads) introduces systematic bias, analogous to specification searching. The distribution of treatment effect estimates conditional on passing the pre-test is not centered on the true effect.

2. **Low power**: Pre-trend tests often have insufficient power to detect meaningful violations, creating a false sense of security.

3. **Non-parallel pre-trends ≠ non-parallel post-trends**: Even if pre-trends appear parallel, this does not guarantee parallel trends in the post-period, especially when the treatment itself may alter trajectories.

**How the new estimators address these concerns:**

- **Partially, by construction**: All three estimators (CS, SA, BJS) eliminate the mechanical contamination that TWFE introduces. This means that when researchers examine event-study plots constructed from these corrected estimators, the pre-period coefficients are cleaner measures of pre-trend violations rather than artifacts of negative weighting. This improves the *reliability* of pre-trend tests as diagnostic tools.

- **But not fully**: Roth's critique is about the *logic* of pre-testing, not about the quality of the estimates. Even with corrected estimators, conditioning on passing a pre-trend test introduces selection bias. The new estimators do not solve this problem.

- **Sensitivity analysis as the alternative**: Roth (2022) and Rambachan and Roth (2023) propose sensitivity analyses that bound treatment effects under hypothesized violations of parallel trends, rather than testing for violations. The `HonestDiD` package implements these bounds. Several of the new estimator packages (notably `did` for CS) have begun incorporating interfaces with `HonestDiD`, allowing researchers to compute sensitivity analyses directly from corrected event-study estimates.

- **The emerging norm**: The literature's consensus, articulated in Roth et al. (2023, *QJE*), is that researchers should (a) use one of the corrected estimators, (b) present event-study plots, but (c) supplement these with sensitivity analyses rather than relying on pre-trend significance tests. This represents a genuine methodological improvement but also an increase in the burden of careful applied work.

### 5. Synthesis: The State of Play

The staggered DiD literature has produced a remarkable convergence: three independently developed solutions that, under common assumptions, yield similar results. The remaining disagreements are at the margins—efficiency under misspecification, finite-sample performance, and the handling of covariates.

**Methodological dominance**: Callaway and Sant'Anna has the widest footprint, partly due to first-mover advantage (published first) and a mature software ecosystem. However, the imputation approach of Borusyak et al. is gaining ground and may overtake CS because of its simplicity and elegance. The field appears to be converging on a "report multiple estimators" norm rather than a single dominant method.

**Unresolved tensions**: Roth's pre-trend critique remains the most important open problem. No estimator can verify the parallel trends assumption; they can only ensure it is not mechanically distorted. The shift toward sensitivity analysis is real but adoption remains incomplete. The tension between the desire for clean identification and the reality of messy data persists.
