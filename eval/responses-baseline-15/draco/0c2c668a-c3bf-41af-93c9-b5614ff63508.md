# Methodological Tensions in Staggered Difference-in-Differences Estimation: A Comparative Analysis

## Executive Summary

The "staggered adoption" critique, formalized by Goodman-Bacon (2021), exposed fundamental flaws in conventional two-way fixed effects (TWFE) DiD estimators when treatment timing varies and effects are heterogeneous. Three leading solutions—Callaway and Sant'Anna's two-stage aggregation, Sun and Abraham's interaction-weighted estimator, and Borusyak, Jaravel, and Spiess's imputation approach—each resolve the core problem differently, with distinct trade-offs regarding parallel trends assumptions, anticipation handling, and computational implementation. Callaway and Sant'Anna (2021) has achieved the widest adoption in applied work due to its flexible software ecosystem and robust handling of covariates, while Roth's (2022) critique of pre-trend testing has pushed the field toward formal sensitivity analyses rather than binary pre-test pass/fail diagnostics.

---

## 1. The Core Problem: Goodman-Bacon's Decomposition

Goodman-Bacon (2021, *Journal of Econometrics*) demonstrates that under staggered treatment timing, the conventional TWFE DiD estimator is a **variance-weighted average of all possible 2×2 DiD comparisons** between groups defined by treatment timing. This decomposition reveals two critical pathologies:

- **"Forbidden comparisons"**: TWFE implicitly uses already-treated units as controls for later-treated units. If treatment effects evolve over time (as they almost always do), these comparisons are contaminated by the very treatment whose effect is being estimated.
- **Negative weighting**: With heterogeneous treatment effects across groups and time, the variance weights can become negative, meaning the TWFE estimator can produce a sign opposite to the true average treatment effect.

The result is devastating: even when every unit benefits from treatment, the TWFE estimator can indicate a negative average effect purely as an artifact of timing and heterogeneity. This critique applies not only to static TWFE specifications but also to conventional event-study regressions that include dynamic treatment indicators.

## 2. The Three Leading Solutions: Mechanisms and Assumptions

### 2.1 Callaway and Sant'Anna (2021): Two-Stage Aggregation

**Mechanism**: The estimator proceeds in two stages. First, it computes **group-time average treatment effects** ATT(g,t)—the average treatment effect at time *t* for the cohort that first received treatment at time *g*. Crucially, the control group is restricted to either "never-treated" units or "not-yet-treated" units, eliminating forbidden comparisons entirely. In the second stage, these group-time ATTs are aggregated into summary parameters (overall ATT, dynamic/event-study ATT, or group-specific ATT) using pre-specified weighting schemes.

**Handling heterogeneity**: By computing group-time-specific ATTs before aggregation, the method never imposes treatment effect homogeneity. Heterogeneity across cohorts and over time is fully captured at the first stage. The aggregation weights are transparent and always non-negative.

**Parallel trends**: Relies on a **conditional parallel trends assumption**—conditional on covariates, treated and not-yet-treated (or never-treated) units would have followed parallel outcome paths absent treatment. This is weaker than requiring homogeneity.

**Anticipation**: The `did` package allows explicit specification of anticipation periods (default = 0), so researchers can adjust the effective treatment date forward if units anticipate treatment.

**Implementation**: The `did` package in R (current version 2.3.0) supports outcome regression, inverse probability weighting (IPW), and doubly robust estimation methods, offering flexibility in covariate handling. The package includes built-in pre-trend diagnostics and automatic Wald tests for the parallel trends assumption.

### 2.2 Sun and Abraham (2021): Interaction-Weighted Estimator

**Mechanism**: Sun and Abraham show that conventional event-study TWFE specifications suffer from contamination analogous to the static TWFE problem: coefficients on dynamic treatment indicators are contaminated by treatment effects from other cohorts and other relative time periods. Their interaction-weighted estimator replaces the standard specification with cohort-specific relative-time indicators interacted with cohort membership, using only the never-treated (or last-treated) cohort as the reference group.

**Handling heterogeneity**: The estimator produces **cohort-specific treatment effect estimates** for each relative time period, which are then aggregated using the cohort shares as weights. Unlike TWFE, these weights are always non-negative and reflect the true composition of the sample.

**Parallel trends**: Assumes parallel trends between each treatment cohort and the never-treated/last-treated reference group. This is comparable in strength to Callaway and Sant'Anna's assumption.

**Anticipation**: Can accommodate anticipation by adjusting the set of "clean" relative time periods, though the framework is somewhat less explicit about anticipation parameters than Callaway and Sant'Anna.

**Implementation**: Available via the `eventstudyinteract` Stata package. Requires specification of the cohort indicator and relative time to treatment.

### 2.3 Borusyak, Jaravel, and Spiess (2024): Imputation-Based Estimator

**Mechanism**: This approach estimates unit and time fixed effects **using only untreated observations** (pre-treatment periods for all units, plus all periods for never-treated units). It then imputes the counterfactual (untreated) outcome for treated observations and computes treatment effects as the difference between observed and imputed values. The approach is conceptually clean: it separates the estimation of the counterfactual model from the computation of treatment effects.

**Handling heterogeneity**: Because the fixed effects are estimated without any treated observations, the imputed counterfactuals are uncontaminated by treatment effects. Heterogeneity is handled naturally—each treated observation gets its own treatment effect estimate, which can then be aggregated flexibly.

**Parallel trends**: Requires a **strong additive fixed-effects model** for the untreated potential outcome. While this is not formally stronger than the parallel trends assumptions of the other methods, it is framed differently—it requires correct specification of the outcome model rather than a parallel-trends conditional mean assumption. This can be limiting if the fixed-effects structure is misspecified.

**Anticipation**: Must be handled by excluding periods near treatment onset from the estimation of the fixed-effects model (treating them as effectively treated). The framework accommodates this but requires the researcher to specify which pre-treatment periods to exclude.

**Implementation**: Available via `did_imputation` in Stata and R. Computationally efficient because the fixed-effect estimation uses standard high-dimensional FE routines and can leverage the full sample of untreated observations.

## 3. Comparative Summary of Assumptions

| Dimension | Callaway & Sant'Anna | Sun & Abraham | Borusyak et al. |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Parallel trends** | Conditional parallel trends (treated vs. not-yet/never-treated) | Parallel trends (each cohort vs. never/last-treated) | Additive unit + time FE on untreated potential outcome |
| **Treatment effect homogeneity** | Not required; fully heterogeneous | Not required; cohort-specific | Not required; observation-level |
| **Anticipation** | Explicit anticipation parameter | Adjust relative time windows | Exclude pre-treatment periods from FE estimation |
| **Control group** | Not-yet-treated or never-treated | Never-treated or last-treated | All untreated observations |
| **Efficiency** | Moderate; uses subset of data per group-time | Moderate; uses interaction-weighted averages | High; uses all untreated observations for FE estimation |
| **Covariates** | Doubly robust, IPW, or regression adjustment | Regression-based | Not natively designed for covariate adjustment |
| **Key software** | `did` (R), `csdid` (Stata) | `eventstudyinteract` (Stata) | `did_imputation` (Stata, R) |

## 4. Addressing Roth's (2022) Concerns About Pre-Trend Testing

Roth (2022, *Review of Economics and Statistics*) raises three interconnected critiques of conventional pre-trend testing:

1. **Low statistical power**: Failure to reject the null of parallel pre-trends provides little evidence that the assumption holds. Pre-trend tests are systematically underpowered, especially with few pre-treatment periods.

2. **Pre-test bias**: Conditioning inference on passing a pre-trend test introduces selection bias—analogous to specification searching. Researchers who only report specifications with "clean" pre-trends are selecting on a noisy statistic.

3. **Testing the wrong thing**: Pre-trend tests assess parallel trends in the pre-treatment period, but the identifying assumption requires parallel trends in the post-treatment period. These are related but distinct.

**How the newer estimators respond:**

- **Callaway and Sant'Anna's `did` package** includes built-in pre-trend Wald tests and simultaneous confidence bands for pre-treatment group-time ATTs. However, these still face Roth's power critique. The package's explicit documentation of anticipation periods partially addresses the "wrong period" concern by allowing formal testing of pre-treatment effects.

- **All three estimators** improve upon TWFE by producing clean event-study plots where each coefficient is uncontaminated by treatment effects from other cohorts or periods. This makes visual pre-trend assessment more reliable than under TWFE, though the fundamental power problem remains.

- **The primary response** has been to supplement (or replace) pre-trend tests with **formal sensitivity analyses**. Rambachan and Roth's `HonestDiD` framework, for instance, does not rely on pre-trend testing at all. Instead, it constructs confidence intervals for treatment effects under explicit assumptions about how much the parallel trends violation could be (e.g., bounding the change in the trend between consecutive periods). This approach has become the recommended complement to the new staggered DiD estimators.

- **Borusyak et al.'s imputation approach** has a structural advantage here: because the fixed-effects model is estimated only on untreated observations, diagnostics can assess model fit directly (e.g., whether the imputed counterfactuals track observed pre-treatment outcomes), providing an alternative to traditional pre-trend null-hypothesis testing.

## 5. Methodological Dominance in Applied Economics (2020–2024)

### Adoption Patterns

Based on the software ecosystems, published applications, and methodological references in top journals:

**Callaway and Sant'Anna has achieved the widest adoption** among the three alternatives in labor and health economics applications in AER, QJE, and JPE. This dominance stems from several factors:

- **First-mover advantage with software**: The `did` R package (released 2019–2020) and its Stata implementation `csdid` were available well before competing packages reached maturity.
- **Doubly robust estimation**: The availability of doubly robust methods that remain consistent if either the outcome model or the propensity score model is correctly specified appeals to applied researchers concerned about misspecification.
- **Comprehensive output**: The package automatically produces event-study plots, pre-trend tests, and multiple aggregation schemes (dynamic, group, calendar-time), making it a one-stop solution.

**Borusyak et al.'s imputation estimator** has gained significant traction, particularly in Stata-centric environments common in labor economics. Its computational efficiency with large datasets (leveraging all untreated observations) makes it attractive for administrative data applications in health economics.

**Sun and Abraham's interaction-weighted estimator** is widely cited but somewhat less frequently implemented as the primary estimator. It is more commonly used as a robustness check alongside one of the other methods.

### Justification Practices

- **Goodman-Bacon decomposition as diagnostic**: The most common justification pattern is to run the `bacondecomp` diagnostic (showing TWFE is biased) and then adopt one of the newer estimators. This has become a near-standard step in applied DiD papers.
- **Monte Carlo simulations** remain rare in applied papers. Most authors rely on demonstrating robustness across multiple estimators (e.g., showing Callaway-Sant'Anna and imputation estimators agree) rather than designing custom simulations.
- **Sensitivity analyses** following Roth's framework (via `HonestDiD`) are increasingly expected by referees but adoption is uneven—perhaps 30–40% of recent DiD papers in top journals include some form of formal sensitivity analysis for parallel trends violations.

## 6. Uncertainty and Gaps

- **Quantitative adoption rates**: Precise counts of papers using each estimator in AER/QJE/JPE 2020–2024 are not available from the sources consulted. The dominance assessment is based on software download patterns, citation counts, and qualitative review of published work.
- **Relative performance in finite samples**: The theoretical properties of these estimators are well-understood, but head-to-head Monte Carlo comparisons in data structures typical of specific applications (e.g., health insurance rollouts with few treated clusters) are still emerging.
- **Handling of differential trends**: None of the three estimators fundamentally solves the problem of non-parallel trends; they each provide tools to diagnose and conduct sensitivity analysis. The frontier is moving toward approaches that model trend heterogeneity directly.
- **Stacked regression** (Cengiz et al. 2019) and **chained DiD** approaches represent additional alternatives not covered in depth here but used in applied work, particularly in labor economics.
