# Maternal Mortality and Prenatal Care Access: Rohingya Refugees in Cox's Bazar vs. Karen Refugees in Mae La Camp (2017–2023)

## Executive Summary

This comparative analysis reveals a stark divergence in maternal health outcomes and healthcare delivery between two major protracted refugee settings. Karen refugee communities along the Thai-Myanmar border, served by an integrated NGO–Thai Ministry of Public Health model, achieved markedly stronger maternal health indicators—including over 90% antenatal care (ANC) completion and 80% skilled birth attendance—than Rohingya refugees in Cox's Bazar, Bangladesh, where an entirely NGO-operated system faced chronic underfunding, fragmented coordination among 40+ implementing partners, and the absence of a dedicated maternal hospital until 2026. Critical data gaps, particularly regarding Rohingya-specific maternal mortality rates and neonatal mortality within 28 days, reflect the weaker surveillance infrastructure in the Bangladesh setting itself—a finding with analytical significance.

---

## 1. Background: Two Protracted Displacement Contexts

### Rohingya Crisis, Cox's Bazar, Bangladesh
In August 2017, over 750,000 Rohingya fled violence in Myanmar's Rakhine State into Cox's Bazar, Bangladesh, creating the world's largest refugee settlement (Kutupalong and Nayapara camps). The population now exceeds 1 million, with 75% women and children and 95% of households dependent on humanitarian assistance [1, 2]. The healthcare system is entirely NGO- and UN-operated, with no integration into Bangladesh's national health infrastructure for refugee populations.

### Karen Displacement, Thai-Myanmar Border
Karen refugees have lived in camps along the Thai-Myanmar border since the 1980s, making this one of the world's longest protracted refugee situations. Mae La camp is the largest, with approximately 37,000 residents at its peak. The healthcare model is distinct: international NGOs (notably Malteser International and the Shoklo Malaria Research Unit/SMRU) operate camp-level primary care integrated with the Thai Ministry of Public Health, which provides free vaccines, birth certificates, and subsidized secondary/tertiary hospital care through formal memoranda of agreement [3, 4].

## 2. Comparative Health Indicators

### 2.1 Antenatal Care Completion (4+ Visits)

| Indicator | Rohingya (Cox's Bazar) | Karen (Thai-Myanmar Border) |
|---|---|---|
| ANC 4+ visit completion | Data not systematically published; SRH Working Group reports do not provide aggregated camp-wide rates | **>90%** (2000–2018 period) [3] |

The Karen camps' achievement of over 90% ANC completion reflects decades of sustained community health worker engagement and a stable, well-defined catchment population. In Cox's Bazar, the sheer scale—over 1 million refugees across 34 camps—makes coordinated ANC tracking exponentially more difficult. UNFPA leads a Sexual and Reproductive Health Working Group with 40+ partners, but the fragmented implementing architecture complicates consistent data aggregation [5]. The 2023 SRH Working Group bulletin describes ANC as a priority but does not publish a camp-wide completion rate, a significant transparency gap.

### 2.2 Skilled Birth Attendance

| Indicator | Rohingya (Cox's Bazar) | Karen (Thai-Myanmar Border) |
|---|---|---|
| Skilled birth attendance | Not comprehensively reported; first dedicated maternal hospital opened only in January 2026 [6] | **80%** [3] |

The Karen figure of 80% SBA was achieved through SMRU-trained midwives operating within Mae La camp, supported by a clear referral pathway to Thai district hospitals [4]. For Rohingya refugees, the absence of a specialized maternal facility until 2026 is telling. Save the Children's newly opened 59-bed Maternal and Child Hospital (January 2026) provides the first dedicated 24/7 emergency obstetric care, C-section capability, and newborn stabilization in the camps [6]. Before this, deliveries were dispersed across NGO-run primary health centers with variable capacity, and emergency obstetric complications required referral to district facilities outside the camps—a process complicated by movement restrictions.

### 2.3 Maternal Mortality

| Indicator | Rohingya (Cox's Bazar) | Karen (Thai-Myanmar Border) |
|---|---|---|
| Maternal mortality ratio | Specific rate not identified in available sources; UNHCR notes maternal mortality remains a concern across operations | **126.5 per 100,000 live births** (2000–2018) [3] |

The Karen figure of 126.5/100,000, while still above the SDG 3.1 target of 70/100,000, represents a significant achievement in a resource-constrained, protracted displacement setting. For Rohingya refugees, the 2023 establishment of a Maternal and Perinatal Mortality Surveillance and Response (MPMSR) system—with an active perinatal mortality coordination officer beginning August 2023—suggests that robust mortality tracking was not previously in place [5]. UNHCR's 2020 global review acknowledged that "too many women in refugee settings continue to die from preventable and treatable pregnancy-related complications" [7].

### 2.4 Neonatal Mortality (Within 28 Days) and Postpartum Hemorrhage

Specific quantitative data on neonatal mortality within 28 days and postpartum hemorrhage incidence were not available in the identified sources for either population. This absence is itself significant: the Karen study notes that neonatal, infant, and under-five mortality indicators were tracked with a 69% overall reduction in child mortality over 2000–2018, but specific 28-day neonatal mortality rates are not reported [3]. For Rohingya refugees, the MPMSR system established in 2023 began active perinatal mortality surveillance only from August 2023 [5]. The Shoklo Malaria Research Unit's 2008 assessment of Mae La camp explicitly evaluated management of obstetric complications including (pre-)eclampsia and prolonged labor, but specific postpartum hemorrhage incidence figures are not available in accessible documents [4].

## 3. Healthcare Delivery Model Comparison

### 3.1 NGO-Operated Field Hospitals (Cox's Bazar)

The Rohingya response relies on a parallel humanitarian health system:
- **Coordination**: UNFPA leads the SRH Working Group coordinating 40+ NGO, INGO, and UN partners [5]
- **Infrastructure**: NGO-run primary health centers and field hospitals; no dedicated maternal facility until January 2026 [6]
- **Staffing**: Midwives deployed by various NGOs with variable training standards and supervision
- **National health system linkages**: Minimal; refugees cannot access Bangladesh's public health system and rely entirely on humanitarian services
- **Family planning**: A formal Family Planning Strategy for Rohingya refugees (2022–2025) was launched by the Directorate General of Family Planning in February 2023 [5]

### 3.2 Thai MoPH Integration Model (Karen Camps)

The Karen camp model represents a hybrid system:
- **Camp-level care**: Malteser International operates primary health care with community health workers [3]
- **SMRU role**: The Shoklo Malaria Research Unit provides specialized reproductive health services with trained midwives in Mae La camp [4]
- **Thai MoPH integration**: Free vaccines, birth certificates, and secondary/tertiary hospital care via formal memoranda of agreement [3]
- **Referral pathway**: Emergency cases referred to Thai district hospital in Mae Sariang, 80 km away (3.5–7 hours depending on season) [3]
- **Cost**: 115 EURO per birth (2018) [3]
- **Traditional birth attendant mediation**: SMRU's model explicitly assessed cultural and clinical mediation between trained midwives and traditional practices, evaluating staff recognition of danger signs during pregnancy and delivery [4]

### 3.3 Key Structural Differences

| Dimension | Cox's Bazar (Rohingya) | Thai-Myanmar Border (Karen) |
|---|---|---|
| Health system model | Parallel humanitarian system | NGO + national health system integration |
| Number of implementing partners | 40+ under UNFPA coordination | Concentrated (Malteser International, SMRU) |
| National health system access | Minimal to none | Formal MoA with Thai MoPH |
| Dedicated maternal facility | None until January 2026 | Embedded in camp infrastructure |
| Population scale | 1 million+ | ~37,000 (Mae La) |
| Displacement duration | Since 2017 (acute) | Since 1980s (protracted) |

The concentration of implementing partners under a single coordination framework in the Karen setting, combined with formal access to Thailand's district hospital system, created a more coherent care continuum. In Cox's Bazar, the sheer scale of displacement and the multiplicity of actors introduced coordination challenges that directly affected continuity of maternal care.

## 4. Midwife-to-Population Ratios and Emergency Obstetric Care Access

Specific midwife-to-population ratio data were not available in the identified sources for either setting. However, several proxy indicators are relevant:

- **Cox's Bazar**: UNFPA's 2023 International Midwifery Day campaign explicitly highlighted the need for increased midwife deployment, suggesting staffing gaps remain significant [5]. The 2026 opening of the Save the Children hospital—staffed by obstetricians, anesthetists, nurses, and midwives—underscores that specialized maternal health staffing was previously insufficient [6].
- **Karen camps**: SMRU-trained midwives provided the backbone of delivery care in Mae La camp [4]. The 80% SBA rate and 3.7% C-section rate suggest a functional but constrained midwifery workforce.

**Emergency obstetric care within 2-hour transport radius**: The Karen camps' referral hospital in Mae Sariang was 80 km away (3.5–7 hours depending on season), meaning emergency transport frequently exceeded the 2-hour benchmark [3]. In Cox's Bazar, the absence of a dedicated maternal facility until 2026 similarly meant that emergency obstetric care often required external referral, complicated by camp movement restrictions and traffic congestion in the world's densest refugee settlement.

---

## 5. Impact of UNFPA Funding Reductions (2021)

Specific data quantifying the 2021 UNFPA funding reduction's effect on service continuity in these two locations were not available in the identified sources. However, the broader funding trajectory provides important context:

- **Global UNFPA crisis**: In 2021, UNFPA faced significant funding shortfalls that affected reproductive health service delivery across humanitarian operations globally. As the lead agency for the SRH Working Group in Cox's Bazar, any reduction in UNFPA's operational budget directly impacted coordination capacity and commodity supply chains for 40+ implementing partners [5].
- **Cox's Bazar funding trajectory**: The Rohingya Joint Response Plan was 68% funded in 2024 and dropped to 46% in 2025 following US aid cuts [2]. This progressive underfunding has forced the closure of healthcare centers and schools in the camps [6].
- **Karen camps**: The stable, lower-cost healthcare model (115 EURO per birth) and integration with Thailand's health system likely provided some insulation against international funding fluctuations, though specific 2021 impact data were not available.
- **MSF operational reports**: Médecins Sans Frontières operational data for either location were not accessible through the searches conducted. This represents a significant gap, as MSF operates surgical and obstetric referral facilities in Cox's Bazar and has historically provided care along the Thai-Myanmar border.

## 6. Cultural Mediation and Traditional Birth Attendants

The SMRU model in Mae La camp explicitly incorporated assessment of cultural mediation between trained midwives and traditional birth practices. The 2008 quality assessment evaluated staff recognition of danger signs during pregnancy and delivery, reflecting an approach that acknowledged existing traditional practices while building clinical capacity [4]. TheKaren camps' long-term stability (since the 1980s) allowed for deeper community engagement and trust-building.

In Cox's Bazar, the acute nature of the 2017 influx and the cultural conservatism of Rohingya communities present distinct challenges. Traditional birth attendants remain influential, and cultural barriers to facility-based delivery—including modesty concerns with male providers and preference for female companions during labor—are documented in broader humanitarian literature. However, specific data on cultural mediation programs in Cox's Bazar were not available in the identified sources.

## 7. Synthesis and Analysis

### Why the Karen Model Outperformed

The Karen refugee maternal health system achieved superior documented outcomes for several interconnected reasons:

1. **Protracted stability enabled infrastructure**: Decades of continuous presence allowed SMRU and Malteser International to build mature surveillance systems, train local health workers, and establish trust.
2. **National health system integration**: Access to Thai district hospitals for emergencies and the formal MoA framework provided a referral backbone that no purely humanitarian parallel system could replicate.
3. **Concentrated accountability**: A small number of implementing organizations with clear mandates is more effective than 40+ partners under a coordination umbrella.
4. **Manageable scale**: A population of ~37,000 versus 1 million+ fundamentally changes the logistics of ANC tracking and SBA provision.

### Why Rohingya Maternal Health Faces Structural Challenges

1. **Scale and density**: The world's largest and densest refugee camp presents logistical barriers that no single model can easily overcome.
2. **No national health system access**: Refugees' exclusion from Bangladesh's public health system eliminates the referral backbone that proved critical in the Thai setting.
3. **Coordination fragmentation**: 40+ implementing partners under UNFPA coordination introduces transaction costs and variable quality standards.
4. **Acute-on-protracted timeline**: Only since 2017, insufficient time to build the mature surveillance and community health worker networks that the Karen setting developed over decades.
5. **Progressive defunding**: The trajectory from 68% to 46% JRP funding is forcing facility closures, directly threatening the maternal health gains achieved since 2017.

## Uncertainty and Gaps

This analysis is constrained by several significant data limitations that should be acknowledged:

- **Rohingya-specific maternal mortality rate**: No camp-specific MMR for Cox's Bazar was identified in publicly accessible sources. The establishment of the MPMSR system only in August 2023 suggests that systematic maternal death surveillance is recent and its outputs may not yet be published.
- **Neonatal mortality (28-day)**: Specific 28-day neonatal mortality rates were unavailable for both populations. This is a critical gap for assessing the quality of intrapartum and immediate postnatal care.
- **Postpartum hemorrhage incidence**: No quantitative PPH incidence data were available for either setting.
- **Midwife-to-population ratios**: Specific staffing ratios were not found in accessible documents.
- **2021 UNFPA funding reduction impact**: While the global funding crisis is documented, location-specific quantitative impact on service continuity indicators (ANC visits, delivery volumes, supply chain disruptions) was not available.
- **MSF operational data**: Médecins Sans Frontières reports—which likely contain rich clinical data on obstetric complications and outcomes—were not accessible through the searches conducted.
- **Mae La vs. Mae Ra Ma Luang/Mae La Oon**: The strongest Karen data come from Malteser International's operations in Mae Ra Ma Luang and Mae La Oon rather than Mae La camp specifically, though the SMRU thesis provides Mae La-specific qualitative data from 2008.
- **Temporal mismatch**: The most rigorous Karen data cover 2000–2018, while the Rohingya data cover 2017–2023. Direct temporal comparison of the 2017–2023 period requires more granular data than was available.

These gaps collectively illustrate a broader finding: the Rohingya humanitarian response, despite its scale and visibility, has produced less comprehensive published maternal health surveillance data than the much smaller, longer-established Karen camp system—a structural inequity in evidence generation that itself warrants attention.
